Participles and absolute clauses (Weather permitting…)

“Weather permitting, we'll eat outside”, “The meeting over, everyone left”, “With prices rising, families are cutting back”: participle and absolute clauses pack an idea of time, cause or condition into a few words. They make writing dense and elegant, as long as you avoid the dangling participle.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level C1

Prefer explanations in French? Version française de cette leçon →

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Build absolute clauses with their own subject (Weather permitting, The work done, There being no…).
  • Use “with + noun + participle” to express a circumstance (With the deadline approaching…).
  • Master perfect, passive and negative participles (Having finished…, Having been warned…, Not knowing…).
  • Spot and correct dangling participles, and know the accepted exceptions (generally speaking, given that, considering).

Review: the participle and its implied subject

A participle clause with no subject (“Walking home, I met Paul”) must take the subject of the main clause: I was the one walking. It expresses time, cause, manner or condition.

Present participle (-ing): active, simultaneous or causal (“Feeling tired, she went to bed”). Past participle: passive meaning (“Built in 1890, the bridge is still in use”). Perfect participle: an earlier action (“Having read the report, he called the client”).

FormMeaningExample
-ingactive, simultaneous, causeKnowing the risks, she still agreed.

Having been + ppearlier action (passive)Having been warned, they stayed at home.

  • Not having received a reply, I sent a second email.
    Negative perfect participle: “not” goes before having.
  • Having been told the news, she called her family.
    Passive perfect participle: she had been told.

Absolute clauses: a subject of their own

An absolute clause has its own subject, different from the main clause: “The rain having stopped, we went out” (the rain stopped; we went out). It is formal, common in writing and in narratives.

Several expressions are fixed and very common: “Weather permitting” (if the weather is good enough), “All things considered”, “Other things being equal”, “That said”, “God willing”, “Time permitting”.

With “there”: “There being no further questions, the meeting was closed.”

  • Weather permitting, the ceremony will take place in the garden.
    A fixed absolute: if the weather allows.
  • The work done, the builders packed up and left.
    Own subject (the work) + past participle.
  • All things considered, it was a successful year.
    = Taking everything into account.
  • There being no objections, the motion was approved.
    Absolute with there: the style of minutes.

With + noun + participle: the circumstance

The most frequent absolute in everyday English is introduced by “with”: with + noun + -ing / past participle / adjective / phrase. It expresses a cause, a circumstance or a setting, in speech as in writing.

“Without” also works: “Without anyone noticing, he left the room.”

StructureExampleMeaning
with + noun + -ingWith prices rising, families are spending less.cause
with + noun + ppWith the project finished, we can relax.cause, earlier action
with + noun + adjectiveHe fell asleep with the window open.circumstance
with + noun + prepositionShe stood there with her hands in her pockets.manner, setting
without + noun + -ingThey left without anyone seeing them.negative circumstance
  • With the elections only a month away, the parties are campaigning hard.
    = Because the elections are only a month away…

The dangling participle trap

If the implied subject of the participle is not the subject of the main clause, the sentence is wrong and sometimes comic: “Walking down the street, the trees looked beautiful” says the trees were walking. Either change the subject of the main clause (“Walking down the street, I noticed the trees”) or use a full clause (“As I walked down the street, the trees…”).

This is one of the mistakes most often marked by examiners, in academic English and in exams (IELTS, Cambridge).

DanglingCorrect
Having finished the report, the computer crashed.Having finished the report, I saw the computer crash. / Just after I had finished the report, the computer crashed.
Arriving late, the meeting had already started.Arriving late, I found that the meeting had already started.
Built in 1890, tourists love the bridge.Built in 1890, the bridge is loved by tourists.

Accepted exceptions and reduced relative clauses

Some participles have become linkers and do not need a shared subject: generally / broadly / strictly speaking, judging by / from, considering (that), given (that), assuming (that), provided / providing (that), talking of, based on (in everyday use). “Considering his age, he did very well” is perfectly correct.

Participles also reduce relative clauses: “the people living next door” (= who live), “the documents attached” (= which are attached), “a decision taken yesterday” (= which was taken). This is very common in reports and emails.

  • Judging by the queue, the new bakery is a success.
    An accepted exception.
  • Given the circumstances, we had no choice.
    A fixed linker: considering the circumstances.
  • Anyone wishing to attend should register by Friday.
    Reduced relative: anyone who wishes…

Patterns that don't work in English

Some languages use a past participle alone with verbs of movement (“Arrived at the station, he called”). In English this is impossible: say “Having arrived / On arriving / When he arrived at the station, he called”.

For the means of doing something, use by + -ing, not in + -ing: “By working hard, you'll succeed.” For “at the moment of”, use on + -ing or when: “On arriving, I…”.

The formula

Participle clause: participle + complement, subject = that of the main clause · Absolute clause: OWN SUBJECT + participle (Weather permitting; The work done; With prices rising)

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • A participle or absolute clause forms its own intonation group, separated by a pause: “Weather permitting, | we'll eat outside.”
  • “permitting” /pəˈmɪtɪŋ/ is stressed on “mit”; the t between vowels is often tapped in American English.
  • “Having been told” /ˈhævɪŋ bɪn ˈtəʊld/: “been” is reduced to /bɪn/.
  • “considered” /kənˈsɪdəd/ (BrE): three syllables, stress on “si”.
  • In “With prices rising”, the stress falls on “prices” and “rising”.

Exercises

Writing Workshop

From easy to harder. Answer without looking at the lesson, then check each exercise.

1. Participle or absolute clause?

Say whether the opening clause is a participle clause (same subject) or an absolute clause (own subject).

  1. Feeling unwell, Marc went home early.
  2. The guests having left, we started cleaning.
  3. With the shops closed, the streets were empty.
  4. Built in 1920, the station has just been renovated.
Show the answers
  1. participle clause
  2. absolute clause
  3. absolute clause (with)
  4. participle clause

2. Condense

Rewrite using a participle or absolute clause.

  1. If the weather permits, we'll have a barbecue.
  2. After she had finished her degree, she moved to Calgary.
  3. Because prices are rising, people are buying less.
  4. As there was no bus, we walked.
  5. Because he didn't know the way, he asked a policeman.
Show the answers
  1. Weather permitting, we'll have a barbecue.
  2. Having finished her degree, she moved to Calgary.
  3. With prices rising, people are buying less.
  4. There being no bus, we walked.
  5. Not knowing the way, he asked a policeman.

3. Fix the dangling participle

Rewrite the sentence so that the participle has the right subject.

  1. Having eaten lunch, the train left.
  2. Driving to Kingston, a deer jumped onto the road.
  3. Written in a hurry, the teacher found many mistakes in the essay.
Show the answers
  1. Having eaten lunch, we caught the train. / After we had eaten lunch, the train left.
  2. While I was driving to Kingston, a deer jumped onto the road.
  3. Written in a hurry, the essay contained many mistakes.

4. Correct the mistake

Correct each sentence.

  1. Arrived at the hotel, she took a shower.
  2. Having not understood, he asked again.
  3. With falling the temperature, the lake froze.
  4. The time permitting, we'll visit the museum.
Show the answers
  1. Having arrived / When she arrived at the hotel, she took a shower.
  2. Not having understood, he asked again.
  3. With the temperature falling, the lake froze.
  4. Time permitting, we'll visit the museum.

5. Rewrite with a participle structure

Rewrite each sentence with a participle or absolute structure.

  1. When everything is taken into account, it was a good decision.
  2. Because the children were asleep, we could finally talk.
  3. Because of the delay, we'll start at ten. (Given…)
Show the answers
  1. All things considered, it was a good decision.
  2. The children asleep, we could finally talk. / With the children asleep, we could finally talk.
  3. Given the delay, we'll start at ten.

6. Your turn

Write a short paragraph (4 sentences) announcing an outdoor event, using at least three structures from the lesson.

  1. Example answer
Show the answers
  1. Weather permitting, our community picnic will take place in Stanley Park on Saturday. With more than 200 families registered, it promises to be our biggest event yet. Anyone wishing to help should contact the organisers by Thursday. Having learnt from last year, we will also provide shaded areas and free water.
Key points
  • A participle clause with no subject takes the subject of the main clause; otherwise it dangles (a frequent mistake).
  • Absolute clause = own subject + participle: Weather permitting, The work done, There being no…, All things considered.
  • With + noun + participle / adjective: With prices rising, with the window open — very common.
  • Not goes before the participle; no “Arrived at…” on its own; by + -ing for the means.
