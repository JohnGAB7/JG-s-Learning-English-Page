Indirect questions (Could you tell me where…?)

Indirect questions (“Could you tell me where the station is?”) are more polite than direct questions (“Where is the station?”). They are essential with strangers, at work, at a hotel reception or on the phone. The trap: the word order goes back to that of a normal statement.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level B1

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Turn a direct question into a polite indirect question with the main opening phrases.
  • Use subject + verb order and remove do / does / did.
  • Use if / whether for yes / no questions.
  • Choose the right punctuation and avoid the most common mistakes.

Why and when?

A direct question can sound abrupt when you speak to a stranger or a customer. Starting with a phrase like “Could you tell me…”, “Do you know…” or “I was wondering…” makes it softer and more polite.

Indirect questions also express doubt or personal thoughts: “I wonder why she left.”

Opening phrasePolitenessEnd punctuation
Do you know…?neutral, common?
Can / Could you tell me…?polite?
Do you have any idea…?neutral, a bit informal?
Would you mind telling me…?very polite?
I'd like to know…polite, direct.
I was wondering… / I wonder…very polite / thinking aloud.

The word-order rule

In the indirect part, the subject and verb are not inverted: we go back to subject + verb order, as in a statement. The question word (where, when, why, how much…) stays at the start of that part.

With be and modal verbs (can, will, should…), just put the subject before the verb. This is the most common mistake: “Could you tell me where is the station?” is wrong.

Direct questionIndirect question
Where is the station?Could you tell me where the station is?
What time does the shop open?Do you know what time the shop opens?
How much did it cost?Can you tell me how much it cost?
When will the results be ready?Do you know when the results will be ready?
Why can't we park here?Could you explain why we can't park here?

Remove do, does, did

In a direct question in the present simple or past simple, we use do / does / did. In the indirect question these helpers disappear, and the verb goes back to its normal form: -s for he / she / it, or the past form.

A tip: imagine the statement (“The shop opens at nine”) and put it after the question word.

  • Where does she work? → Do you know where she works?
    does disappears; -s comes back.
  • What did he say? → Can you tell me what he said?
    did disappears; past form said.
  • How do I get to the airport? → Could you tell me how I get to the airport?
    Also possible: how to get to the airport.

Yes / no questions: if or whether

When the direct question expects a yes / no answer (Is…? Do…? Can…?), there is no question word: use “if” or “whether”. “Whether” is a little more formal and often goes with “or not”.

This “if” is not the “if” of conditions: it introduces a yes / no question.

  • Is the museum open on Monday? → Do you know if the museum is open on Monday?
    if + subject + verb.
  • Can I pay by card? → Could you tell me whether I can pay by card?
    whether: a bit more formal.
  • Did they receive my email? → I'd like to know if they received my email.
    did disappears; past form received.

Special cases

Subject questions (who, what, which + verb): the order does not change, because the question word is already the subject. “Who called? → Do you know who called?”.

“I was wondering if you could…” makes a very polite request, especially in writing: “I was wondering if you could send me the brochure.” After a past opening verb (I was wondering, I asked), you can keep the present when the situation is still true.

Indirect questions look like reported questions (lesson 105), but here you are really asking: there is no change of tense after “Do you know…” or “Could you tell me…”.

  • Who won the match? → Do you know who won the match?
    Subject question: same order.
  • I was wondering if you could help me with this form.
    A very polite request.

The formula

Could you tell me / Do you know + question word (or if / whether) + subject + verb …?

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • In “Could you tell me”, “could you” often sounds like /ˈkʊdʒuː/ and “tell me” is linked: /ˈtel mi/.
  • In “Do you know”, “do you” is often reduced to /djə/: /djə ˈnəʊ/ in fast speech.
  • An indirect question that starts with Do you know / Could you tell me usually has a rising tone at the end, like a yes / no question.
  • With “I wonder…” or “I'd like to know…”, the tone falls: they are statements.
  • “whether” sounds the same as “weather”: /ˈweðə/ (UK).

In a conversation

Listening Studio

Mariam has just arrived in Toronto for an internship. She phones the student residence where she will stay.

Receptionist

Good morning, Maple Residence. How can I help you?
A standard phone greeting.

Mariam

Hello. I'm arriving tomorrow and I was wondering if I could check in after 10 p.m.
Very polite request: I was wondering if…

Receptionist

Yes, that's fine. The night porter is here until 2 a.m.
A direct answer.

Mariam

Great. Could you tell me how I get there from the airport?
how + subject + verb.

Receptionist

Take the UP Express to Union Station, then the subway to Spadina.
Directions with imperatives.

Mariam

Thank you. Do you know how much the train costs?
No does: costs.

Receptionist

I'm not sure exactly how much it is now, but you can check on their website.
how much + it + is.

Mariam

OK. And one last thing: do you know whether the rooms have Wi-Fi?
Yes / no question with whether.

Receptionist

Yes, they all do. The password is on your key card.
Short answer.

Exercises

Writing Workshop

From easy to harder. Answer without looking at the lesson, then check each exercise.

1. Correct or incorrect?

Is the indirect question correct (C) or incorrect (I)?

  1. Do you know where the toilets are?
  2. Could you tell me what time does it start?
  3. I wonder if she's coming.
  4. Can you tell me where can I buy a ticket?
Show the answers
  1. C
  2. I (what time it starts)
  3. C
  4. I (where I can buy)

2. Transform

Make the direct question indirect with the phrase given.

  1. Where is the post office? (Could you tell me…)
  2. What time does the bank close? (Do you know…)
  3. How much did the tickets cost? (Can you tell me…)
  4. Why is the flight delayed? (I'd like to know…)
  5. When will the doctor arrive? (Do you have any idea…)
  6. Who is the manager? (Do you know…)
Show the answers
  1. Could you tell me where the post office is?
  2. Do you know what time the bank closes?
  3. Can you tell me how much the tickets cost?
  4. I'd like to know why the flight is delayed.
  5. Do you have any idea when the doctor will arrive?
  6. Do you know who the manager is?

3. Yes / no questions

Transform with if or whether.

  1. Is breakfast included? (Do you know…)
  2. Can I change my seat? (Could you tell me…)
  3. Did the parcel arrive? (I was wondering…)
  4. Does this bus go to the city centre? (Do you know…)
Show the answers
  1. Do you know if breakfast is included?
  2. Could you tell me whether I can change my seat?
  3. I was wondering if the parcel arrived / had arrived.
  4. Do you know if this bus goes to the city centre?

4. Correct the mistake

Each sentence has one mistake. Write it correctly.

  1. Do you know where does Paul live?
  2. I wonder what is he doing.
  3. Could you tell me is there a pharmacy near here?
  4. Can you tell me what did the doctor say?
Show the answers
  1. Do you know where Paul lives?
  2. I wonder what he is doing.
  3. Could you tell me if there is a pharmacy near here?
  4. Can you tell me what the doctor said?

5. Be more polite

Make these direct questions more polite with an indirect question.

  1. Where's the station?
  2. Is the shop open on Sunday?
  3. Why didn't he answer? (thinking aloud: I wonder…)
Show the answers
  1. Could you tell me where the station is?
  2. Do you know if the shop is open on Sunday?
  3. I wonder why he didn't answer.

You are planning a trip to Brussels. Write a short email (4–5 sentences) to a hotel with at least four different indirect questions.

  1. Model answer
Show the answers
  1. Dear Sir or Madam, I have a reservation from 3 to 6 May. I was wondering if I could check in early, around 10 a.m. Could you tell me how I can get to the hotel from Brussels-Midi station? I'd also like to know whether breakfast is included in the price. Finally, do you know if there is a pharmacy near the hotel? Kind regards, Claire Mballa
Key points
  • Indirect question = polite phrase + question word + subject + verb (Could you tell me where the station is?).
  • No do / does / did: Do you know what time the shop opens?
  • Yes / no questions: if or whether (Do you know if it's open?).
  • Question mark only if the opening phrase is a question (Do you know…?); full stop after I wonder / I'd like to know.
  • Subject questions (who called?): the order doesn't change.
