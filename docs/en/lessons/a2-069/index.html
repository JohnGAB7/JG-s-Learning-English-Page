Question tags (isn’t it?, don’t you?)

To check information or to invite someone to agree, English adds a “question tag” at the end of a sentence: It's cold, isn't it? You like jazz, don't you? The difficulty is that the tag changes with every sentence: it repeats the auxiliary and the subject. Many learners say “isn't it?” everywhere. This lesson teaches you to choose the right tag.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level A2

Prefer explanations in French? Version française de cette leçon →

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Make a tag with the auxiliary of the sentence + a subject pronoun (isn't it?, can you?, didn't she?).
  • Apply the rule: positive sentence → negative tag; negative sentence → positive tag.
  • Use do / does / did in the tag when the sentence has no auxiliary.
  • Understand intonation: a falling tag (I'm checking) or a rising tag (a real question).

The idea: auxiliary + pronoun

A tag has two words: the auxiliary (or be, or the modal verb) of the sentence + the matching subject pronoun. You are tired, aren't you? She can swim, can't she?

Always use a pronoun in the tag, never the noun: Paul is late, isn't he? (not “isn't Paul?”). For this / that, use it: That's your car, isn't it?

Basic rule: if the sentence is positive, the tag is negative (with a contraction). If the sentence is negative, the tag is positive.

SentenceTagRule
It's hot,isn't it?positive → negative tag
It isn't hot,is it?negative → positive tag
You can drive,can't you?same modal
They were at home,weren't they?same past of be
She won't come,will she?won't → will
  • The weather is lovely today, isn't it?
    be in the present.
  • You aren't from Lyon, are you?
    Negative sentence → positive tag.
  • We should leave now, shouldn't we?
    Modal should.

No auxiliary: do, does, did

When the sentence is in the present simple or past simple with no auxiliary, the tag uses do, does or did, as in a question: You live here, don't you? He works in Douala, doesn't he? They arrived yesterday, didn't they?

In negative sentences, the auxiliary is already there: You don't eat meat, do you? She didn't call, did she?

Be careful: have (possession) in the present also takes do: You have a car, don't you? But have got takes have: You've got a car, haven't you?

  • You speak Spanish, don't you?
    Present simple → don't.
  • Your sister lives in Québec, doesn't she?
    he / she / it → doesn't.
  • You saw the film, didn't you?
    Past simple → didn't.
  • He doesn't like football, does he?
    Negative → does.

Special cases

I am: the tag is aren't I? (not “amn't I”). I'm right, aren't I? But in the negative: I'm not late, am I?

Let's: the tag is shall we? Let's go, shall we? With an imperative, you can add will you? to sound softer: Close the door, will you?

Negative words (never, nothing, nobody) make the sentence negative, so the tag is positive. He never calls, does he? Nothing happened, did it? With nobody / somebody / everybody, use they: Nobody came, did they?

  • I'm in the right room, aren't I?
    I am → aren't I?
  • Let's have lunch, shall we?
    Let's → shall we?
  • You never eat breakfast, do you?
    never = negative → positive tag.

Intonation changes the meaning

Falling intonation ↘: you are almost sure and you expect the other person to agree. This is the most common, especially in conversation: Nice day, isn't it? ↘

Rising intonation ↗: you are not sure and it's a real question: You're coming tomorrow, aren't you? ↗ (I don't know and I want to know.)

Tags are very useful for starting a conversation with someone you don't know (small talk), for example about the weather.

  • It's a beautiful city, isn't it? ↘
    I'm sure; I expect “Yes, it is!”.
  • The shop opens at nine, doesn't it? ↗
    I'm not sure: a real question.

Answering a tag question

Answer according to the facts, with a short answer: It's cold, isn't it? — Yes, it is. / No, it isn't.

Be careful with negative sentences: You aren't tired, are you? — No, I'm not. (I'm not tired) / Yes, I am. (I am tired). Yes always goes with a positive verb, and No with a negative verb.

You can also add a comment: It was a great match, wasn't it? — Yes, it was amazing!

  • You don't know Kofi, do you? — Yes, I do. We work together.
    Yes, I do = I know him.

The formula

positive sentence + negative tag (isn't it?, don't you?) · negative sentence + positive tag (is it?, do you?) · auxiliary of the sentence + pronoun · no auxiliary → do / does / did · I am → aren't I? · Let's → shall we?

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • The tag joins the sentence with no real pause: “It's cold, isn't it?” ≈ /ɪts kəʊld ˈɪznt ɪt/.
  • isn't it often sounds like /ˈɪzənɪt/: the t of isn't almost disappears.
  • don't you ≈ /ˈdəʊntʃu/ and didn't you ≈ /ˈdɪdntʃu/: t + y join to make /tʃ/.
  • Falling intonation ↘ to check, rising ↗ for a real question.
  • aren't I /ˈɑːnt aɪ/: no r sound in British English.

In a conversation

Listening Studio

At a bus stop in Montréal, in winter, Nadia starts a conversation with a stranger, Ben.

Nadia

It's really cold today, isn't it?
Falling tag: small talk.

Ben

Yes, it is! Minus fifteen, I think. You're not from here, are you?
Negative sentence → positive tag.

Nadia

No, I'm not. I'm from Dakar. I arrived in September.
Short answer.

Ben

So this is your first winter, isn't it?
be → isn't it?

Nadia

Yes! It's very different. You were born in Montréal, weren't you?
were → weren't you?

Ben

Yes, I was. But I still don't like the cold!
Short answer with was.

Nadia

The 24 bus goes to the university, doesn't it?
Rising tag: a real question.

Ben

Yes, it does. Oh, here it comes. Let's get on, shall we?
Let's → shall we?

Nadia

Yes! It's warm inside, isn't it?
Falling tag.

Key points
  • Tag = auxiliary of the sentence + subject pronoun (isn't it?, can't she?, weren't they?).
  • Positive → negative tag; negative (or never, nothing, nobody) → positive tag.
  • No auxiliary → do / does / did: You live here, don't you?
  • I am → aren't I? · Let's → shall we?
  • Falling intonation = I'm checking; rising = a real question.
