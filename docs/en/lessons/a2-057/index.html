Could / would you…? (polite requests)

To ask for something politely, English uses questions with could or would rather than the imperative: “Could you help me?”. Choosing the right level of politeness is essential at work, in shops and with people you don't know.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level A2

Prefer explanations in French? Version française de cette leçon →

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Ask someone to do something with Can / Could / Would you + base verb.
  • Ask for permission for yourself with Can / Could / May I…?
  • Use “Would you mind + -ing?” and understand the answers.
  • Accept or refuse a request politely.

Asking someone to do something: Can / Could / Would you…?

To ask someone to do something, ask a question with a modal + you + base verb. The more “distant” the form, the more polite it sounds.

Here, “could” is not a past form: it is simply more polite. Put “please” after you or at the end of the sentence.

FormPolitenessExampleWhen
Can you + base?informalCan you pass the salt?with friends and family
Could you + base?polite — the most useful formCould you close the window, please?almost always OK
Would you + base?politeWould you sign here, please?service situations
Would you mind + -ing?very politeWould you mind waiting a moment?strangers, work
Could you possibly + base?very polite, for a big favourCould you possibly lend me your car?when you ask a lot

Asking for permission: Can / Could / May I…?

When you ask for something for yourself (permission to do something), the subject is I: Can I…?, Could I…?, May I…? (more formal).

“Could I have…?” is the perfect phrase to order or ask for something: “Could I have the bill, please?”. “Can I get…?” is common in American English in cafés, but it is more informal.

  • Can I use your phone?
    Informal permission.
  • Could I have a glass of water, please?
    Polite request for a thing.
  • May I come in?
    Formal permission.

Would you mind…?: be careful with the answers

“Would you mind + verb-ing?” means “Is it a problem for you to…?”. The verb after mind is always in the -ing form.

Trap: to say yes (to agree to help), you answer with a negative: “No, not at all.” / “Of course not.” (= it's no problem for me). Answering “Yes” would mean “yes, it is a problem”.

  • Would you mind opening the door? — No, not at all.
    “No” here = I'm happy to do it.
  • Would you mind speaking more slowly?
    mind + -ing.
  • Would you mind if I sat here? — Of course not. Go ahead.
    Asking permission with mind if.

Accepting and refusing politely

To accept, use short, friendly answers: Sure. / Of course. / Yes, no problem. / Certainly (more formal). We don't usually answer a request with “Yes, I could”.

To refuse, say sorry and give a reason: “I'm sorry, but…”, “I'm afraid I can't…”. “I'm afraid” here means “I'm sorry to say”, not “I'm scared”.

AcceptingRefusing
Sure! / Of course.I'm sorry, I can't. I'm in a hurry.
Yes, no problem.I'm afraid I can't. I'm using it right now.
Certainly. (formal)I'd love to, but I'm busy tonight.

Don't confuse

“Would you like…?” (lesson 34) is an offer or invitation: you offer something to someone. “Would you…?” is a request: you ask someone to do something.

An imperative alone (“Close the door.”) can sound rude in English, especially with a stranger. “Please” helps, but a question with could is much more natural.

  • Would you like a coffee?
    An offer.
  • Would you make some coffee?
    A request.

The formula

Can / Could / Would you + base …? · Would you mind + verb-ing …? · Can / Could / May I + base …?

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • “Could you” often sounds like /ˈkʊdʒuː/: d + y join to make /dʒ/; the same with “Would you” /ˈwʊdʒuː/.
  • “could” /kʊd/ and “would” /wʊd/: the l is silent; “would” sounds like “wood”.
  • Intonation makes you polite: a voice that rises gently at the end sounds polite; a flat or falling voice can sound like an order.
  • “please” is /pliːz/, with a z sound at the end; it is often stressed at the end of the sentence.

In a conversation

Listening Studio

At a hotel reception desk in Quebec City. Mr Ndiaye has just arrived after a long trip from Dakar.

Receptionist

Good evening, sir. How can I help you?
A standard polite question.

Mr Ndiaye

Good evening. I have a reservation. Could you check it, please? The name is Ndiaye.
Could you + base + please.

Receptionist

Of course. Would you mind spelling your name?
Very polite: Would you mind + -ing.

Mr Ndiaye

Not at all. N-D-I-A-Y-E.
“Not at all” = yes, happy to.

Receptionist

Thank you. Here's your key. Room 412.
Here's… = giving something.

Mr Ndiaye

Thanks. Could I have a wake-up call at six, please?
Could I have…? for a service.

Receptionist

Certainly. Would you like some help with your bags?
Certainly = formal yes; Would you like = an offer.

Mr Ndiaye

That would be great. Could you also tell me where I can eat tonight?
Accepting an offer, then a request.

Receptionist

I'm afraid the restaurant is closed, but there's a nice café across the street.
I'm afraid = I'm sorry to say.

Exercises

Writing Workshop

From easy to harder. Answer without looking at the lesson, then check each exercise.

1. From informal to polite

Write the best request for each situation: Can you…? (friend) or Could you…? / Would you mind…? (stranger, work).

  1. To your brother: pass you the remote control.
  2. To a stranger on the train: turn their music down.
  3. To your manager: read your report.
  4. To a friend: wait for you for five minutes.
Show the answers
  1. Can you pass me the remote?
  2. Could you turn your music down, please? / Would you mind turning your music down?
  3. Could you read my report, please? / Would you mind reading my report?
  4. Can you wait for me for five minutes?

2. Would you mind + -ing

Change the request to Would you mind…?

  1. Could you close the door?
  2. Could you speak more slowly?
  3. Could you send me the file?
  4. Could you move your car?
Show the answers
  1. Would you mind closing the door?
  2. Would you mind speaking more slowly?
  3. Would you mind sending me the file?
  4. Would you mind moving your car?

3. Asking for yourself

Write a polite request with Could I…? or May I…?

  1. You want the bill in a restaurant.
  2. You want to use the toilet at someone's house.
  3. You want to borrow a colleague's pen.
  4. You want to enter your teacher's office.
Show the answers
  1. Could I have the bill, please?
  2. Could I use your bathroom, please? / May I use your toilet?
  3. Could I borrow your pen?
  4. May I come in?

4. Accept or refuse

Answer politely (✓ accept, ✗ refuse with a reason).

  1. Would you mind opening the window? (✓)
  2. Could you help me with my bags? (✓)
  3. Could you lend me your car tonight? (✗)
Show the answers
  1. No, not at all. / Of course not.
  2. Sure! / Of course.
  3. I'm sorry, I can't. I need it tonight. / I'm afraid I can't. I'm using it.

5. Correct the mistake

Each sentence has one mistake. Write it correctly.

  1. Could you to call me back?
  2. Would you mind to wait here?
  3. Can I to sit here?
  4. Would you mind helping me? — Yes, sure!
Show the answers
  1. Could you call me back?
  2. Would you mind waiting here?
  3. Can I sit here?
  4. Would you mind helping me? — No, not at all! / Of course not!

6. Your turn

You are at an office reception or in a shop. Write a short exchange of four or five lines with at least three different polite requests.

  1. Model answer
Show the answers
  1. Me: Good morning. Could you tell me where the passport office is, please? — Clerk: Of course. It's on the second floor. — Me: Thank you. Would you mind giving me a form? — Clerk: Not at all. Here you are. — Me: Could I borrow a pen too? — Clerk: Certainly.
Key points
  • Asking someone: Can you…? (informal) → Could / Would you…? (polite) → Would you mind + -ing? (very polite).
  • Asking for yourself: Can / Could / May I…?; Could I have…, please?
  • Would you mind…? → to agree: No, not at all / Of course not.
  • Refusing politely: I'm sorry, but… / I'm afraid I can't…
  • Here, could is not past; and Would you like…? is an offer, not a request.
