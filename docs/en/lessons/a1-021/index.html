A1 · Beginner · lesson 21

Telling the time

Ask the time, give the time, and say when a class, a film or a train starts. You need this every day.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level A1

Prefer explanations in French? Version française de cette leçon →

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Ask the time politely (What time is it? / What's the time?).
  • Give the time in two ways: with past and to (half past seven) and the digital way (seven thirty).
  • Show morning or evening with a.m., p.m., in the morning, in the evening.
  • Say when something happens with “at” (The film starts at eight).

Asking and giving the time

To ask the time, say “What time is it?” or “What's the time?” (very common in British English). With a stranger, be more polite: “Excuse me, do you have the time?” or “Have you got the time?”.

To answer, always start with “It's”. The subject “it” is necessary, like for the weather (lesson 1). Never say “It's seven hours”: we don't use “hours” to give the time.

Use “o'clock” only for exact hours: “It's seven o'clock.” Never use it with minutes.

  • What time is it? — It's three o'clock.
    Question and answer.
  • Excuse me, have you got the time? — Yes, it's ten past four.
    Polite question to a stranger.
  • It's nearly six.
    nearly = almost.
“Do you have the time?” (with the) = What time is it? Without “the”, “Do you have time?” means “Are you free? Can you do something?”.

The traditional way: past and to

Say the minutes first, then the hour. From 1 to 30 minutes, use “past” (after the hour). From 31 to 59 minutes, count the minutes before the next hour and use “to”.

15 minutes = “a quarter”, 30 minutes = “half”. We say “a quarter past”, “a quarter to”, but “half past” (never “a half past”).

When the minutes are not 5, 10, 15 and so on, add the word “minutes”: “It's twenty-two minutes past six.”

Careful: with “to”, you say the NEXT hour. 7:45 = a quarter to EIGHT. In American English you also hear “a quarter after seven” (7:15) and “a quarter of eight” (7:45).

The digital way: read the numbers

You can also say the hour and then the minutes, like on a screen. It is easy and very common. We always use it for train, plane and appointment times.

For 01 to 09 minutes, say “oh” for the zero: 9:05 = “nine oh five”.

In informal British English, people also say “half seven”. It means 7:30 (half past seven), never 6:30.

  • It's seven thirty.
    7:30.
  • It's nine oh five.
    9:05 (oh = zero).
  • It's eleven forty-five.
    11:45.
  • The train leaves at eighteen twenty.
    18:20: official timetables use the 24-hour clock.

Morning, afternoon, evening

In conversation, English speakers mostly use the 12-hour clock. To be clear, they add “a.m.” (from midnight to midday) or “p.m.” (from midday to midnight), or “in the morning”, “in the afternoon”, “in the evening”, “at night”.

Don't say “It's 14 p.m.”: with a.m. and p.m., use only the numbers 1 to 12. Don't use “o'clock” with a.m. or p.m. either: say “2 p.m.” or “two o'clock in the afternoon”.

    9:00 in the morning.

When? at, from… to, about

To say when something happens, use “at” before the time (lesson 18): “at seven”, “at half past six”. The question is “What time…?”: “What time does the film start?”.

For a period: “from nine to five”. For a time that is not exact: “at about eight” or “around eight”.

Three useful expressions: “early” (before the right time), “late” (after the right time), “on time” (at the right time).

  • What time does the film start? — At a quarter past eight.
    Question with “What time”, answer with “at”.
  • I work from nine to five.
    A period of time.
  • Let's meet at about seven.
    Not exactly seven.
  • The bus is never on time.
    It is always late.
Don't say “At what hour…?”. Say “What time…?”.

The formula

It's + time: seven o'clock · ten past seven · half past seven · a quarter to eight · seven forty-five

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • “o'clock” is /əˈklɒk/: the first o is weak, the stress is on “clock”.
  • In “half” /hɑːf/, you don't say the l. The same in “calm” and “walk”.
  • “quarter” /ˈkwɔːtə/: there is a w sound after the k (kwo-). In British English you don't say the final r.
  • Stress: thirTEEN (13) but THIRty (30), fifTEEN but FIFty. A mistake here can make you miss a train!
  • “past” /pɑːst/ and “to” /tə/ are quick and weak: “ten past four” sounds like “ten pas four”.

In a conversation

Listening Studio

At Brussels-South station, Lucie asks a traveller the time and looks for her train.

Lucie

Excuse me, have you got the time?
A polite way to ask the time.

Tom

Yes, it's twenty to five.
4:40.

Lucie

Thanks. What time is the next train to Paris?
“What time” = at what moment.

Tom

Let me see… at five fifteen, from platform 3.
The digital way: 5:15.

Lucie

A quarter past five? Great, I'm early!
The same time, the traditional way.

Tom

Yes, but this train is often late.
late = after the right time.

Lucie

Oh no. My meeting is at eight p.m.
p.m. = after midday.

Tom

Don't worry. It's only one hour and twenty minutes to Paris.
“hour” for a length of time.

Key points
  • Question: What time is it? / What's the time? Answer: It's… (you need the subject “it”).
  • Minutes first: ten past seven (7:05 to 7:30), twenty to eight (7:31 to 7:59); a quarter, half past.
  • Use “o'clock” only for exact hours; never “hours” to give the time.
  • Digital way: seven thirty, nine oh five; a.m. before midday, p.m. after midday (1 to 12).
  • When? → What time…? → at eight, at half past six.
