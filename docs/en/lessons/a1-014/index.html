Possessive adjectives (my, your…)

My, your, his, her, its, our, their show who something belongs to. They never change with the thing you own, but “his” and “her” depend on the owner: a man → his, a woman → her.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level A1

Prefer explanations in French? Version française de cette leçon →

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Know the seven possessive adjectives and match them with the subject pronouns.
  • Choose between his and her by the owner (a man or a woman), not by the thing.
  • Use a possessive with parts of the body and clothes.
  • Don't confuse its / it's, their / there / they're, your / you're.

The table of possessives

The possessive adjective goes before the noun. It never changes: not in the plural, not with the gender of the thing. “My car, my cars”; “their house, their houses”.

Never use an article with a possessive: “my book”, never “the my book” or “a my friend” (say “a friend of mine”, which you will see later).

His or her? Look at the owner

In English, “his” or “her” depends on the OWNER: if the owner is a man or a boy, his; if the owner is a woman or a girl, her. The thing doesn't matter.

So: Paul and his sister (Paul's sister); Marie and her brother (Marie's brother); Marie and her husband; Paul and his wife. In languages like French, the word changes with the thing, so this is a very common mistake.

    Karim → his.
  • Sophie is talking to her son.
    Sophie → her.
  • The company is moving its office to Lille.
    a company → its.

The body and clothes

English almost always uses a possessive with parts of the body and the clothes a person wears: I wash my hands, She's putting on her coat, He's got his hands in his pockets. Many languages use “the” here; English doesn't.

With “have got” and descriptions, you don't need a possessive: She's got long hair. But for an action on your body, the possessive is necessary.

  • Wash your hands before dinner.
    your hands, not “the hands”.
  • I brush my teeth twice a day.
    my teeth.
  • He's shaking his head.
    his head.
  • Take off your shoes, please.
    your shoes.

Your, their, its: one or more owners

“Your” is for one person or many people, friendly or formal: “your car” can belong to one friend, a customer, or a group.

“Their” is for several owners. Like “they”, it can also mean one person when you don't know their gender: Every student needs their own laptop.

“Its” is for a thing, an animal you don't know, a company, a country or a city: The city is famous for its museums.

  • Children, take your books!
    your = plural here.
  • My neighbours love their garden.
    several owners.
  • Canada is famous for its lakes.
    a country → its.

Words that sound the same

Some possessives sound exactly like other words. When you speak, there is no problem; in writing, it is a common mistake, even for native speakers.

Tip: if you can say “it is”, “they are” or “you are”, you need the apostrophe (it's, they're, you're). A possessive never has an apostrophe.

PossessiveSame soundExample
itsit's (= it is / it has)The cat is eating its dinner. It's hungry.
theirthey're (= they are), there (in that place)They're in their car, over there.
youryou're (= you are)You're late! Where's your bag?

The formula

I → my · you → your · he → his · she → her · it → its · we → our · they → their (+ noun, never an article)

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • “his” /hɪz/: breathed h + voiced z. Without the h, it sounds like “is”.
  • “her” is /hɜː/ when strong; in fast speech, often /ə/: Call her mother /ˈkɔːl ə ˈmʌðə/.
  • “their”, “there” and “they're” all sound like /ðeə/; “your” and “you're” like /jɔː/; “its” and “it's” like /ɪts/.
  • “our” /ˈaʊə/ sounds like “hour” (the h in hour is silent).
  • Possessives are normally weak. Stress them for contrast: It's MY bag, not YOUR bag.

In a conversation

Listening Studio

At a family party in Ottawa, Mia shows photos to her new colleague, Fatou.

Mia

Look, this is my family. These are my parents, and that's my brother Leo with his wife.
Leo → his wife.

Fatou

Is that their baby?
their = Leo and his wife.

Mia

Yes! Her name is Chloé. She's eight months old.
her = the baby girl.

Fatou

She's beautiful. And who's the woman with the red hat?
A question about a person.

Mia

That's my aunt Rose and her dog, Max. Max loves his red ball!
Rose → her; Max (a pet) → his.

Fatou

Ha ha! Our dog is the same. Its favourite toy is an old shoe.
its: an animal; no apostrophe.

Mia

And what about your family? Are your parents in Senegal?
your = Fatou's.

Fatou

Yes, they are. Their house is in Dakar, near the sea.
their = her parents'.

Key points
  • my, your, his, her, its, our, their + noun. They never change and never take an article.
  • His / her depends on the owner: Paul and his sister, Marie and her brother.
  • Use a possessive with the body and clothes: I wash my hands, Put on your coat.
  • its (possessive) ≠ it's (it is); their ≠ they're ≠ there; your ≠ you're.
  • Its for a thing, an animal, a company, a country.
