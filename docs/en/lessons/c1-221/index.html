C1 · Advanced · lesson 221

Idioms at work

The figurative expressions you hear in meetings, internal emails and interviews. Understanding them prevents misunderstandings; using them accurately makes your English sound natural and credible.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level C1

Prefer explanations in French? Version française de cette leçon →

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Understand around thirty common workplace idioms (meetings, projects, workload, results).
  • Use them in their exact form: the right preposition, article and possessive, and the right tense.
  • Choose the right register: an idiom in a meeting or internal email, a neutral equivalent in formal writing or with a client.
  • Recognise irritating corporate jargon (circle back, synergy) and replace it with plain wording.

How a workplace idiom works

An idiom is a chunk whose meaning cannot be worked out from its words: “learn the ropes” is not about ropes but about learning how a job is done. Many idioms come from sailing (learn the ropes, on board), sport (ballpark, touch base, the ball is in your court) or accounting (the bottom line, in the red).

The form is fixed: we say “on the same page”, never “in the same page”, and “pull your weight”, never “pull your weights”. What can change is the tense of the verb (we touched base, I'll touch base) and the possessive, which agrees with the subject (she pulls her weight, they pull their weight).

At C1 the challenge is not to know many idioms but to use a few, accurately. One or two well-chosen idioms sound natural; a stream of them sounds rehearsed or even comic.

  • I'm still learning the ropes, so bear with me.
    “Bear with me” = please be patient with me.
  • She really pulls her weight on this team.
    She does her fair share of the work; the possessive agrees with “she”.
  • We touched base last week, but nothing's been decided.
    The verb changes tense; the rest of the idiom stays the same.

Meetings and communication

These are the idioms you will hear most often. They organise the discussion: starting, updating, postponing and informing.

IdiomMeaningExample
touch base (with sb)make brief contact, have a quick updateLet's touch base on Friday.
be on the same pageagree, share the same understandingBefore we start, are we all on the same page?
get the ball rollingstart somethingWho wants to get the ball rolling?
keep sb in the loopkeep sb informedPlease keep me in the loop.
keep sb postedtell sb about new developmentsI'll keep you posted on the budget.
run sth by sbask sb for their opinion on sthCan I run an idea by you?
take it offlinediscuss it separately, outside the meetingThis is getting detailed, let's take it offline.
put a pin in itset it aside to return to later (informal, mainly US)Good point, let's put a pin in it for now.
the ball is in your courtit's your turn to act or decideWe've sent our offer: the ball is in their court.
“Keep me in the loop” and “keep me posted” are near synonyms; “keep me updated” is the neutral version that works everywhere.

Projects, deadlines and workload

These idioms describe how a project is going and how people work. Several are critical: “cut corners” (do something badly to save time or money) and “drop the ball” (fail to do something you were responsible for) are used to criticise, so handle them with tact.

IdiomMeaningExample
hit the ground runningbe effective from the very startWe need someone who can hit the ground running.
bring sb up to speedgive sb the latest informationCan you bring Léa up to speed on the client?
back to the drawing boardwe must start again from the beginningThe client rejected it, so it's back to the drawing board.
put sth on the back burnermake sth a lower priority for nowThe app redesign is on the back burner.
cut cornersdo sth less well to save time or moneyWe can't cut corners on safety.
drop the ballmake a mistake by forgetting a taskI dropped the ball on that invoice, sorry.
go the extra milemake more effort than expectedOur support team always goes the extra mile.
be snowed under (BrE) / swampedhave too much workI'm snowed under this week.
call it a daystop working for todayIt's seven o'clock, let's call it a day.
  • I'm afraid I dropped the ball on the shipping dates.
    Admitting a mistake with an idiom: direct but not dramatic.
  • We've put the merger on the back burner until the audit is finished.
    “On the back burner” = postponed, not abandoned.

Results, money and decisions

Idioms about figures and results are very common in spoken reports and presentations. “The bottom line” was originally the last line of a profit and loss account (the profit or loss); by extension it means the most important fact in a situation.

IdiomMeaningExample
a ballpark figurea rough estimateCan you give me a ballpark figure?
the bottom linethe key point; the final financial resultThe bottom line is that we're over budget.
in the red / in the blacklosing money / making a profitThe branch has been in the red for two years.
break evenmake neither a profit nor a lossWe expect to break even next year.
low-hanging fruiteasy, quick gainsLet's start with the low-hanging fruit.
a win-win (situation)a situation good for everyoneFlexible hours are a win-win.
cut your lossesstop something failing to avoid losing moreThe project isn't working; it's time to cut our losses.
a game changersomething that changes a situation completelyAutomation has been a game changer for us.
  • Ballpark, we're looking at around fifty thousand euros.
    “Ballpark” alone at the start of a sentence = roughly; very spoken.
  • The bottom line is that we can't afford a second office.
    Used to close a discussion by stating the key point.

Register: when to use (or avoid) an idiom

In meetings, internal emails and conversations with colleagues, idioms are natural. In an official letter, a report, a contract or a first email to a client, choose the neutral equivalent.

Some idioms have become corporate jargon that many native speakers find empty: “circle back”, “move the needle”, “leverage synergies”, “think outside the box”. You must understand them, but using them constantly makes you sound like a press release.

British vs American: “snowed under” is mainly British, “swamped” is used everywhere; “put a pin in it” and “touch base” are American in origin but now common in the UK.

Idiom (spoken, internal)Neutral or formal equivalent
Let's touch base next week.Shall we arrange a short follow-up meeting next week?
Can you give me a ballpark figure?Could you provide an approximate estimate?
Keep me in the loop.Please keep me informed.
We're snowed under.We are currently dealing with a very high workload.
It's back to the drawing board.We will need to revise the proposal entirely.
Let's circle back to this.Let's return to this point later.
  • Could you provide an approximate estimate by Friday?
    The formal version of “a ballpark figure”, for a client.

The formula

Idiom = fixed chunk (preposition, article and noun do not change) + verb in the right tense and matching possessive; for speech and internal writing

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • Stress falls on the most important content word, often the last one: on the same PAGE, back to the DRAWing board, the bottom LINE.
  • “ballpark” is /ˈbɔːlpɑːk/ (BrE), stressed on the first syllable; “ropes” is /rəʊps/, with a diphthong.
  • Grammar words are reduced: “to the” /tə ðə/ in “back to the drawing board”, “in the” /ɪn ðə/ in “in the loop”.
  • “touch base”: link /tʌtʃ beɪs/ with no pause; “base” rhymes with “face”.
  • “posted” in “keep you posted” is /ˈpəʊstɪd/: two syllables, because -ed is /ɪd/ after t.

Common mistakes

Don’t saySayWhy
✗ We are in the same page.✓ We're on the same page.The preposition is part of the idiom: “on” the same page.
✗ I'll keep you in the course.✓ I'll keep you posted. / I'll keep you in the loop.A translation of the French “au courant”; the English idiom is “keep sb posted / in the loop”.
✗ I'm overbooked this week.✓ I'm snowed under / swamped this week.“Overbooked” describes a flight or hotel, not a busy person.
✗ He doesn't pull his weights.✓ He doesn't pull his weight.“Weight” stays singular in this idiom.
✗ Let's put it in the back burner.✓ Let's put it on the back burner.You put a pan “on” the back burner of a cooker.
✗ I'm still learning the strings.✓ I'm still learning the ropes.“The ropes” = how a job works; “pull strings” means using your contacts.
✗ Let's do a brainstorming.✓ Let's brainstorm. / Let's have a brainstorming session.“Brainstorm” is a verb; “a brainstorming” on its own is not standard English.
✗ Dear Mr Lee, please give us a ballpark figure ASAP.✓ Dear Mr Lee, could you provide an approximate estimate at your earliest convenience?Idioms and ASAP are too informal for a first formal email to a client.

In a conversation

Listening Studio

A Monday morning team video call between Montreal and Brussels.

Nadia

Morning, everyone. Let's get the ball rolling. Tom, can you bring us up to speed on the Lyon project?
Two idioms to open a meeting: start, and give us an update.

Tom

Sure. The bottom line is that the client didn't like the prototype, so it's back to the drawing board.
Key point first, then: we must start again.

Nadia

Oh dear. Do we have a ballpark figure for the extra cost?
She asks for a rough estimate.

Tom

Ballpark, around twelve thousand euros. But we can't cut corners on testing.
“Ballpark” alone = roughly; “cut corners” = reduce quality.

Awa

Agreed. And honestly, I'm snowed under with the Douala launch, so I can't take on much more.
She explains her heavy workload.

Nadia

Understood. Let's put the website update on the back burner, then.
A lower priority for now.

Tom

The pricing question is getting quite detailed. Shall we take it offline?
A polite way to move a detailed topic out of the meeting.

Nadia

Good idea. Tom and Awa, touch base this afternoon and keep me in the loop.
Two idioms to close: talk briefly, and keep me informed.

Exercises

Writing Workshop

From easy to harder. Answer without looking at the lesson, then check each exercise.

1. Recognise the meaning

Explain the idiom in each sentence in plain English.

  1. We need someone who can hit the ground running.
  2. The ball is in their court now.
  3. Let's start with the low-hanging fruit.
  4. The project isn't working; let's cut our losses.
Show the answers
  1. be effective immediately, with no long training period
  2. it's their turn to decide or act
  3. the easiest, quickest gains
  4. stop the project to avoid losing more

2. Complete the idiom

Write the missing word (preposition, noun or verb).

  1. Are we all ___ the same page?
  2. I'm new here, so I'm still learning the ___.
  3. Please keep me in the ___.
  4. The redesign has been put on the back ___.
  5. Our team always goes the extra ___.
  6. The client said no, so it's back to the drawing ___.
Show the answers
  1. on
  2. ropes
  3. loop
  4. burner
  5. mile
  6. board

3. Make it formal

Rewrite the sentence for a formal email to a client, without an idiom.

  1. Can you give us a ballpark figure?
  2. I'll keep you posted.
  3. We're snowed under right now.
  4. Let's touch base next week.
Show the answers
  1. Could you provide an approximate estimate?
  2. I will keep you informed of any developments.
  3. We are currently dealing with a very high volume of work.
  4. I suggest we arrange a short follow-up call next week.

4. Correct the mistake

Each sentence has one mistake. Write it correctly.

  1. She really pulls her weights.
  2. I'm completely overbooked this week.
  3. Let's put this idea in the back burner.
  4. I'm sorry, I let the ball drop on your invoice.
Show the answers
  1. She really pulls her weight.
  2. I'm completely snowed under / swamped this week.
  3. Let's put this idea on the back burner.
  4. I'm sorry, I dropped the ball on your invoice.

5. Say it with an idiom

Rewrite the sentence with an idiom from the lesson.

  1. It's 7 p.m., let's stop working for today.
  2. We hope to make neither a profit nor a loss next year.
Show the answers
  1. It's 7 p.m., let's call it a day.
  2. We hope to break even next year.

6. Your turn

Write a short internal message (4–5 sentences) to your team about a project that is behind schedule. Use three idioms from the lesson correctly.

  1. Example answer
Show the answers
  1. Hi all, quick update on the Kribi project. The bottom line is that we're two weeks behind schedule, mainly because the supplier dropped the ball on the delivery. I've put the marketing plan on the back burner so we can focus on the build. Let's touch base on Thursday, and please keep me in the loop if anything changes.
Key points
  • An idiom is a fixed chunk: preposition, article and noun do not change (on the same page, pull your weight); only the tense and the possessive adapt.
  • Meetings and internal emails: idioms are welcome. Formal letters, reports, first contact with a client: use the neutral equivalent.
  • Few but accurate: one or two idioms per exchange are enough; jargon (circle back, synergy) is for understanding more than imitating.
  • Common traps: keep sb posted (not “in the course”), snowed under / swamped (not “overbooked”), learn the ropes (not “the strings”).
