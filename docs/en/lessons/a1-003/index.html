Numbers from 0 to 20

You use numbers from 0 to 20 every day: to give your age, a phone number, a room number, a quantity or a simple price. They are the base of all other numbers.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level A1

Prefer explanations in French? Version française de cette leçon →

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Say, write and spell the numbers from 0 to 20 easily.
  • Hear and say the -teen numbers with the right stress.
  • Say a phone number digit by digit, with “oh” and “double”.
  • Use numbers with plural nouns and do small sums out loud.

0 to 12: words to learn by heart

The numbers from 0 to 12 have no rule: each one is a different word. Learn the spelling too, because some are not written the way they sound (eight, two, one).

“One” sounds like /wʌn/, the same as “won”, with a w sound at the start. “Two” sounds like /tuː/, the same as “too” and “to”: the w is silent.

13 to 19: the -teen ending

From 13 to 19, add -teen to the number. Three numbers change: three → thirteen, five → fifteen, eight → eighteen (only one t, not “eightteen”). Four keeps its u: fourteen.

When you say the number alone, stress -TEEN: thirTEEN, fourTEEN. This helps people hear the difference from thirty and forty (30, 40), which are stressed at the start (THIRty). You will see them in lesson 20.

20 is “twenty” /ˈtwenti/. The word “teenager” comes from these numbers: a person from 13 to 19 years old.

Zero: zero, oh or nil?

There are several ways to say 0. “Zero” is the general word (temperatures, maths, and almost everywhere in American English).

In a phone number, a room number or a bus number, British people often say “oh”, like the letter O. In football, British English says “nil”: two-nil (2-0). In tennis, 0 is “love”.

  • It's zero degrees today.
    Temperature: zero.
  • My room is two-oh-five (205).
    British: digit by digit, 0 = oh.
  • Cameroon won two-nil.
    Football score (British).

Phone numbers

In English, say a phone number digit by digit: 06 12 = “oh six, one two”. Make a small pause between the groups.

When two digits are the same, British people often say “double”: 77 = “double seven”. Americans usually say “seven seven”. For three of the same digit, say “triple” or each digit.

To ask: “What's your phone number?”. To check: “Can you repeat that, please?”.

  • My number is oh-seven-nine, double four, one-six.
    079 44 16 (British style).
  • Call me on 0472 15 88 30.
    Say: oh-four-seven-two, one-five, double eight, three-oh.
American English says “call me at…”; British English says “call me on…”.

Numbers with nouns and small sums

After a number bigger than 1, the noun takes -s: one book, two books, twelve students. After “one”, the noun stays singular. The number itself never takes -s: “three apples”, never “threes apples”.

“A” and “one” both mean 1, but “one” makes the number important: I have one brother (only one, not two).

For sums: + plus, − minus, × times, ÷ divided by, = is / equals. The verb is singular: Two plus two is four.

SumIn English
3 + 4 = 7Three plus four is seven.
10 − 6 = 4Ten minus six is four.
5 × 3 = 15Five times three is fifteen.
20 ÷ 2 = 10Twenty divided by two is ten.
  • I have two sisters and one brother.
    Plural noun after two.
  • There are eighteen students in the class.
    A quantity.

The formula

0 zero · 1 one · 2 two … 12 twelve · 13–19: number + -teen (thirteen, fifteen, eighteen) · 20 twenty

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • Alone, a -teen number is stressed at the end: thirTEEN /θɜːˈtiːn/. Before a noun the stress can move (THIRteen students), but the long /iː/ of -teen is always clear.
  • “Three” /θriː/: put your tongue between your teeth and breathe out. Don't say “tree” or “free”.
  • “Eight” /eɪt/ sounds like “ate”: the gh is silent.
  • “Twelve” /twelv/ and “twenty” /ˈtwenti/: say the w after the t. In American English, “twenty” often sounds like /ˈtweni/.
  • In a phone number, your voice goes up a little at the end of each group and down at the end of the number.

Key points
  • 0 to 12: different words to learn (one /wʌn/, two /tuː/, eight /eɪt/, twelve).
  • 13 to 19: number + -teen, with thirteen, fifteen, eighteen (one t).
  • Zero: zero (general), oh (phone, room), nil (British football).
  • Phone numbers: digit by digit, “double” for two of the same digit.
  • Number > 1 + plural noun: two books. Sums use “is”: Two plus two is four.
