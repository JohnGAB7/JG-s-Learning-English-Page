Relative clauses (who, which, that)

Relative clauses tell us which person or thing we are talking about, joining two ideas in one sentence: “the woman who lives next door”, “the book that you lent me”. They make your English smoother and more precise.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level B1

Prefer explanations in French? Version française de cette leçon →

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Choose between who (people), which (things, animals) and that (both) in a defining relative clause.
  • Build subject and object relative clauses without repeating the pronoun (not “the man who I saw him”).
  • Place prepositions correctly in relative clauses (the house that I grew up in).
  • Recognise and punctuate a non-defining relative clause with commas, where that is not possible.

Who, which, that

A relative pronoun replaces a noun we have just mentioned and introduces a clause that describes it. Who is for people, which for things and (usually) animals, and that for people and things.

In defining relative clauses (no commas), that is very common, especially in speech, for things. For people, who is more common than that, but both are correct.

The choice depends on the noun (person or thing), not on whether the pronoun is the subject or the object: “the woman who helped me” and “the woman who I helped” both use who.

NounPronounExample
personwho / thatThe nurse who (that) helped me was very kind.
thingwhich / thatThe bus which (that) goes to the airport leaves at six.
animalwhich / that (who for a pet you see as a person)The dog which (that) lives next door barks all night.
after all, everything, nothing, something, a superlativethat (preferred)It's the best film that I've ever seen.

Subject and object relative clauses

If the relative pronoun is the subject of the verb in the clause, the verb comes straight after it: “the man who lives here” (he lives here).

If the pronoun is the object, another subject follows it: “the man who I met” (I met the man). The pronoun replaces the object, so never repeat him, her or it in the clause.

Whom is the object form of who. It is very formal and mostly written (“the person whom I contacted”); in speech, we say who or that, or leave the pronoun out (next lesson).

  • The girl who won the prize is from Bamenda.
    Subject: who + verb (she won).
  • The girl who I met at the party is from Bamenda.
    Object: who + subject + verb (I met her).
  • The phone that I bought last week has stopped working.
    Object: no “it” after bought.
  • The phone that costs 300 euros is too expensive.
    Subject: that + verb.

Prepositions in relative clauses

When the verb in the clause has a preposition (live in, talk to, look for, work with), the preposition usually stays at the end of the clause in everyday English: “the city that I live in”, “the person who I talked to”.

In formal English, the preposition goes before the pronoun, and then we use which or whom (never that or who): “the city in which I live”, “the person to whom I spoke”.

Where can replace in which / at which for a place: “the hotel where we stayed” = “the hotel that we stayed at”.

EverydayFormal
the house that I grew up inthe house in which I grew up
the colleague who I work withthe colleague with whom I work
the job that she applied forthe job for which she applied
the hotel that we stayed at / where we stayedthe hotel at which we stayed

Defining or non-defining?

Defining relative clause: necessary to know who or what we mean. No commas. “My brother who lives in Canada is a doctor” (I have several brothers; I mean the one in Canada).

Non-defining relative clause: it adds extra information about someone or something already identified. It goes between commas, and that is not allowed: use who or which. “My brother, who lives in Canada, is a doctor” (I have only one brother).

Which after a comma can also refer to a whole sentence: “He passed his driving test, which surprised everyone.”

  • Paris, which is the capital of France, has twenty districts.
    Non-defining: extra information, between commas.
  • My mother, who is 70, still works.
    No that in a non-defining clause.
  • She didn't call me back, which was strange.
    Which refers to the whole sentence.

Where, when, why

Use where after a place (the town where I was born), when after a time (the day when we met, the year when…), and why after reason (the reason why she left).

When and why are often left out or replaced by that: “the day (that) we met”, “the reason (that) she left”. Don't confuse where (a place where something happens) and which: “the city which I visited” (object of visit) / “the city where I live” (the place where I live).

  • This is the café where we first met.
    Place.
  • I remember the day when you arrived in Lille.
    Time.

The formula

person + who / that · thing + which / that · non-defining: , who / which , (never that)

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • who /huː/, which /wɪtʃ/ (like “witch”); relative that is weak /ðət/: “the book that I read” /ðə ˈbʊk ðət aɪ ˈred/.
  • Say a defining clause in one breath, with no pause: “the man who lives next door”.
  • A non-defining clause has small pauses around it and a slightly lower voice, like brackets: “My brother | who lives in Canada | is a doctor”.
  • where /weə/ and were /wɜː/ sound different: don't mix them up in writing either.

Common mistakes

Don’t saySayWhy
✗ The man which lives here is a doctor.✓ The man who lives here is a doctor.For a person: who or that, not which.
✗ The book that I read it was boring.✓ The book that I read was boring.The relative pronoun already replaces the object: no it.
✗ My father, that is a teacher, lives in Liège.✓ My father, who is a teacher, lives in Liège.That is not possible in a non-defining clause (with commas).
✗ The city where I visited was beautiful.✓ The city that (which) I visited was beautiful.Visit takes a direct object: which / that, not where.
✗ The woman who she called me is my aunt.✓ The woman who called me is my aunt.Subject clause: no second subject she.
✗ The house in that I grew up is old.✓ The house in which I grew up is old. / The house that I grew up in is old.After a preposition: which (or whom), never that.
✗ He was late, what annoyed the boss.✓ He was late, which annoyed the boss.To refer to a whole sentence: which, not what.

In a conversation

Listening Studio

Toronto, at an evening event with colleagues. Sophie introduces people to Ahmed, who has just arrived from Algiers.

Sophie

Do you see the woman who's talking to the manager?
Subject clause: who + verb.

Ahmed

The one in the red dress?
The one = the woman.

Sophie

Yes. That's Grace, who runs our office in Montreal.
Non-defining: extra information about Grace.

Ahmed

Is she the person that I need to send my report to?
Preposition at the end.

Sophie

Exactly. And the man who's standing next to her is Daniel. He's the one who organised this party.
Two defining clauses.

Ahmed

The food that he chose is excellent.
Object clause: that + subject + verb.

Sophie

He ordered it from a restaurant which belongs to his cousin, which is very convenient!
The second which refers to the whole idea.

Ahmed

Can you introduce me to the people who work in marketing?
Who for people.

Exercises

Writing Workshop

From easy to harder. Answer without looking at the lesson, then check each exercise.

1. Who or which?

Complete with who or which.

  1. The teacher ___ taught me English lives in Douala.
  2. The laptop ___ I bought is very fast.
  3. I have a friend ___ speaks five languages.
  4. The train ___ goes to Brussels is late.
  5. The people ___ live upstairs are noisy.
Show the answers
  1. who
  2. which
  3. who
  4. which
  5. who

2. Is that possible?

Say if that can replace who or which in the sentence (yes / no).

  1. The film which we watched was long.
  2. My sister, who lives in Lyon, is a lawyer.
  3. The man who repaired my car was very fast.
  4. The Eiffel Tower, which was built in 1889, is in Paris.
Show the answers
  1. yes
  2. no (non-defining clause with commas)
  3. yes
  4. no (non-defining clause)

3. Join the sentences

Join the two sentences with a relative pronoun.

  1. A woman called you. She didn't leave her name.
  2. I lost a ring. My grandmother gave it to me.
  3. This is the restaurant. We had dinner there last night.
  4. My uncle is 80. He still plays football.
  5. She passed all her exams. This made her parents very proud.
Show the answers
  1. The woman who called you didn't leave her name.
  2. I lost the ring that (which) my grandmother gave me.
  3. This is the restaurant where we had dinner last night. / This is the restaurant that we had dinner at last night.
  4. My uncle, who is 80, still plays football.
  5. She passed all her exams, which made her parents very proud.

4. Correct the mistakes

Each sentence has one mistake. Write it correctly.

  1. The doctor which I saw was very kind.
  2. The shoes that I bought them are too small.
  3. My boss, that is from Quebec, speaks French.
  4. The hotel where we booked was full.
Show the answers
  1. The doctor who (that) I saw was very kind.
  2. The shoes that I bought are too small.
  3. My boss, who is from Quebec, speaks French.
  4. The hotel that (which) we booked was full.

5. Define it

Write one sentence with a relative clause from the information given.

  1. a woman / live next door / a nurse
  2. a book / you lent me / fascinating
  3. this / the town / I was born there
  4. He lost his keys. It made him angry. (use which)
Show the answers
  1. The woman who lives next door is a nurse.
  2. The book that (which) you lent me is fascinating.
  3. This is the town where I was born.
  4. He lost his keys, which made him angry.

6. Your turn

Describe 4 people, places or objects that are important to you, with a relative clause in each sentence (at least one non-defining clause).

  1. Example answer
Show the answers
  1. My best friend is someone who always tells me the truth. Kribi is the place where I spent all my childhood holidays. The watch that I wear every day was a gift from my father. My grandmother, who is 85, still cooks for the whole family on Sundays.
Key points
  • People: who / that; things and animals: which / that.
  • The relative pronoun replaces the subject or the object: never add him / it again.
  • Preposition at the end of the clause (everyday) or before which / whom (formal), never before that.
  • Non-defining clause between commas: who or which, never that.
  • Which after a comma can refer to a whole sentence.
