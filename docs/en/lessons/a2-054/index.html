Must and have to

“Must” and “have to” both express obligation (something is necessary). In positive sentences they often mean almost the same, but the feeling and the grammar are different: must is a modal verb that never changes, have to works like a normal verb.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level A2

Prefer explanations in French? Version française de cette leçon →

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Form must + base verb and have to / has to + base verb correctly.
  • Tell the difference between the speaker's own obligation (must) and an outside obligation (have to).
  • Talk about obligation in the past (had to) and the future (will have to).
  • Ask about obligation with “Do I have to…?”.

The form of must

“Must” is a modal verb: it does not change with the subject (no -s) and it is followed directly by the base verb, without to.

Questions with must (“Must I…?”) exist but sound formal or old-fashioned; we almost always say “Do I have to…?”. The negative mustn't has a special meaning: see lesson 55.

  • I must call my mother tonight.
    I think it's important.
  • You must wear a seatbelt.
    A strong obligation.
  • Passengers must show their tickets.
    A written rule.

The form of have to

“Have to” works like a normal verb: has to for he / she / it, and do / does / did in questions and negatives.

In spoken British English, you also hear “have got to” (I've got to go) with the same meaning, especially for one particular obligation.

FormPatternExample
positivesubject + have to / has to + baseShe has to work on Saturdays.
questionDo / Does + subject + have to + base?Do we have to pay now?
short answerYes, you do. / No, you don't.Does he have to come? — Yes, he does.
pastsubject + had to + baseI had to wait for an hour.
futuresubject + will have to + baseYou'll have to take a taxi.

The difference: who makes the obligation?

“Must” often shows an obligation that comes from the speaker: their opinion, a personal decision, or a rule they make. It is also common in written rules and signs.

“Have to” usually shows an outside obligation: a law, a rule, a boss, a timetable, a situation you don't control. In everyday conversation, have to is much more common than must.

must (personal obligation / written rule)have to (outside obligation)
I must stop eating so much sugar. (my decision)I have to take my medicine twice a day. (the doctor said so)
You must visit us in Dakar! (friendly insistence)You have to get a visa to visit Canada. (the law)
I must study tonight. (I feel it's necessary)I have to start work at 7. (my contract)
In many sentences both are possible and the difference is small. If you are not sure, “have to” is almost always correct in speech.

Past and future: only have to

“Must” has no past or future form. For a past obligation use “had to”; for a future obligation use “will have to”.

Never say “I musted” or “I will must”.

  • We had to leave early because of the rain.
    Past obligation.
  • Did you have to show your passport?
    Past question with did.
  • You'll have to book in advance.
    Future obligation.

Must for a strong recommendation

In speech, “must” is often used to recommend something with enthusiasm: a film, a restaurant, a visit. It is not a real obligation, but very strong advice.

For softer advice, use “should” (lesson 56).

  • You must try the grilled fish in Kribi. It's delicious!
    Enthusiastic recommendation.
  • You must see this film.
    I really recommend it.

The formula

must + base (never changes) · have to / has to + base · had to (past) · will have to (future)

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • “must” is often weak: /məst/ or even /məs/ before a consonant (I must go /aɪ məs ˈɡəʊ/). For emphasis, say /mʌst/.
  • “have to” is /ˈhæf tə/ with an f sound, not v; “has to” is /ˈhæs tə/ with an s; “had to” is /ˈhæt tə/.
  • “have got to” often sounds like /ˈɡɒtə/ in speech: I've got to go /aɪv ˈɡɒtə ˈɡəʊ/ (sometimes written “gotta” in text messages — avoid it in writing).
  • In “mustn't” /ˈmʌsnt/, the first t is silent.

In a conversation

Listening Studio

Jean-Paul is starting a new job in a hospital in Liège. A colleague, Nathalie, explains the rules.

Nathalie

Welcome! First, you have to wear your badge at all times.
A hospital rule: have to.

Jean-Paul

OK. Do I have to sign in when I arrive?
Question: Do I have to…?

Nathalie

Yes, you do. And you have to wash your hands before you enter each room.
Short answer: Yes, you do.

Jean-Paul

Of course. What time does my shift start?
Asking about the timetable.

Nathalie

At seven. Yesterday the new nurse had to start at six because of an emergency.
Past obligation: had to.

Jean-Paul

I see. I must buy a good alarm clock!
Personal obligation: must.

Nathalie

Ha! And you must try the cafeteria's soup. It's the best in Liège.
Recommendation: must.

Exercises

Writing Workshop

From easy to harder. Answer without looking at the lesson, then check each exercise.

1. Must, have to or has to?

Complete with must, have to or has to (sometimes more than one answer is possible).

  1. My sister ___ wear a uniform at school.
  2. I ___ remember to buy bread.
  3. Students ___ arrive before 8:30.
  4. He ___ take the bus because his car is broken.
  5. You ___ come to our wedding! We'd love to see you.
Show the answers
  1. has to
  2. must (or have to)
  3. have to (or must, in written rules)
  4. has to
  5. must

2. Past and future

Change the obligation to the time in brackets, using have to.

  1. I have to work late. (yesterday)
  2. She has to take an exam. (next year)
  3. We have to wait. (last week)
  4. Do you have to wear a tie? (yesterday, question)
Show the answers
  1. I had to work late yesterday.
  2. She'll have to take an exam next year.
  3. We had to wait last week.
  4. Did you have to wear a tie yesterday?

3. Ask the question

Write the question with do / does / did + have to.

  1. you / pay / now
  2. she / work / on Sundays
  3. they / show / their passports (past)
  4. I / bring / anything
Show the answers
  1. Do you have to pay now?
  2. Does she have to work on Sundays?
  3. Did they have to show their passports?
  4. Do I have to bring anything?

4. Correct the mistake

Each sentence has one mistake. Write it correctly.

  1. You must to be careful.
  2. She have to call the bank.
  3. Yesterday I must leave early.
  4. Does he has to come?
Show the answers
  1. You must be careful.
  2. She has to call the bank.
  3. Yesterday I had to leave early.
  4. Does he have to come?

5. Say it another way

Write a sentence with must or have to that means the same.

  1. It's necessary for me to go now.
  2. Is it necessary for me to book?
  3. It was necessary for us to take a taxi.
Show the answers
  1. I must go now. / I have to go now.
  2. Do I have to book?
  3. We had to take a taxi.

6. Your turn

Describe the obligations of your job or studies in five sentences (present, past, and one recommendation with must).

  1. Model answer
Show the answers
  1. I'm a bank clerk in Yaoundé. I have to start work at 7:30 and I have to wear a suit. I don't like it, but it's a rule. Last month I had to work every Saturday because a colleague was ill. If you visit Yaoundé, you must try the restaurants in Bastos!
Key points
  • must + base: never changes, no to (I must go, she must go).
  • have to / has to + base: changes with the subject; questions with do / does (Do I have to…?).
  • must = personal obligation or written rule; have to = outside obligation (law, job).
  • Past: had to; future: will have to (must has no past form).
  • You must try / see… = enthusiastic recommendation.
