Formal register in writing

Reports, official letters, applications and articles: formal English follows precise rules of vocabulary, grammar and tone. Mastering them lets you write a serious text without sounding heavy or translated from another language's administrative style.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level C1

Prefer explanations in French? Version française de cette leçon →

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Identify the features of formal writing: vocabulary, impersonal structures, no contractions, linkers.
  • Turn a neutral text into a formal one (and back) while keeping the English clear.
  • Punctuate and place formal linkers correctly (however, moreover, consequently).
  • Avoid translated administrative formulas (“I remain at your disposal”, “actually” for “currently”, “In this report we will see”).

What makes a text formal?

Formal register is not about complicated words but about a set of consistent choices: precise vocabulary, often of Latin origin; complete sentences without contractions; an impersonal, objective tone; and explicit logical links.

The goal is still clarity. Modern English, influenced by the Plain English movement in the UK and the US, penalises pomposity: “utilise” instead of “use” or “commence” instead of “start” often sounds pretentious. Formal does not mean obscure.

Choosing words

Formal English often prefers a single, Latin-based verb to a phrasal verb or to “get”. Lesson 243 explores these pairs in depth; learn the most frequent ones here.

Avoid vague words (thing, stuff, nice, big, a lot) and emotional intensifiers (really, totally, awful). Replace them with an exact term: “a major obstacle”, “a substantial increase”, “a serious concern”.

NeutralFormalFormal example

ask forrequestWe request a copy of the invoice.

  • We need to find out why sales went down.
    Neutral: need, find out, went down.
  • It is necessary to determine the causes of the decline in sales.
    Formal: impersonal structure, Latin-based verb, noun (decline).
Don't change everything: “use”, “start” and “show” are perfectly acceptable in a report. Keep “utilise” and “commence” for technical or very ceremonial contexts.

The grammar of formality

No contractions: cannot, do not, it is. For negatives, the clearest forms are preferred: “There is no evidence” rather than “There isn't any evidence”.

Impersonal structures and the passive put the focus on the action rather than the person: “It has been decided that…”, “Applications must be submitted by 1 May.”

In relative clauses, use “whom” after a preposition and put the preposition before the pronoun: “the committee to which the report was sent” (neutral: “the committee the report was sent to”).

Some conditional inversions belong to formal letters: “Should you require further information, please contact…” (= If you require…).

  • The candidates to whom the offer was made have all accepted.
    Preposition + whom: a strong marker of formality.
  • Should you have any questions, please contact the HR department.
    A typical inversion in letters.
  • It is recommended that all staff complete the training.
    Impersonal: the recommendation is not attributed to anyone.

Linkers and sentence construction

Formal linkers connect two complete sentences. “However”, “moreover”, “consequently” and “nevertheless” cannot join two clauses with just a comma: use a full stop or a semicolon before them, and a comma after.

Avoid starting a sentence with “And”, “But” or “So” in very formal writing; prefer “In addition”, “However”, “Therefore”. Inside a sentence, “but” and “so” are still fine.

Subordination (although, whereas, provided that, in order to) gives a denser style than a series of short sentences joined with “and”.

FunctionFormal linkers
Additionmoreover, furthermore, in addition
Contrasthowever, nevertheless, whereas, by contrast
Resultconsequently, therefore, as a result, thus
Conditionprovided that, in the event that, unless
Purposein order to, so as to, with a view to (+ -ing)
  • The budget was approved; however, the timeline remains uncertain.
    Semicolon before however, comma after.
  • The project was delayed. Consequently, costs increased by 10 %.
    Two separate sentences: the linker begins the second.
  • Funding will continue provided that the targets are met.
    “Provided that” = only if.

Tone: objective and impersonal

Formal writing avoids generic “you” (“You can see that…”): write “It can be seen that…” or “As Table 2 shows…”. It also avoids rhetorical questions (“So what does this mean?”) and exclamation marks.

In a report or article, “I” and “we” are not forbidden, especially in science, but they are used sparingly: “We propose…”, “This report argues…”. An inanimate subject can take an action verb: “This report examines…”, “The data show…”.

Judgements are expressed with restraint: “This approach has significant limitations” rather than “This approach is terrible”.

  • As Figure 3 shows, demand fell sharply in 2024.
    Instead of “You can see in Figure 3 that…”.
  • This report examines the impact of the new tax.
    Instead of “In this report, we will see…”.

Translated formulas to avoid

Administrative styles in other languages have set phrases that do not translate word for word. English writers are shorter and more direct, even in formal register.

“Actually” means “in fact”, never “at present” (= currently). “Eventually” means “in the end”. “Sensible” means “reasonable”, not “sensitive”.

Translated phrase to avoidNatural English
I remain at your disposal for any further information.Please do not hesitate to contact me if you require further information.
In this report, we will see the causes of…This report examines the causes of…
Actually, the company employs 200 people.The company currently employs 200 people.
I would be grateful if you could send me it.I would be grateful if you could send it to me.
Please accept, Sir, my distinguished salutations.Yours faithfully, (Dear Sir or Madam) / Yours sincerely, (Dear Ms Lee)

The formula

Formal = precise (often Latin-based) words + full sentences without contractions + impersonal / passive + explicit linkers + restrained tone, without pomposity

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • When reading a formal text aloud, full forms are pronounced but stay natural: “cannot” /ˈkænɒt/ (BrE), stressed on the first syllable.
  • A linker at the start of a sentence forms its own group, followed by a pause: “However, | the results…”, “Consequently, | the project…”.
  • “furthermore” is /ˌfɜːðəˈmɔː/, main stress on “more”; “moreover” is /mɔːrˈəʊvə/, stress on “o”.
  • “require” /rɪˈkwaɪə/ and “sufficient” /səˈfɪʃənt/ are stressed on the second syllable.
  • “whom” is /huːm/: the w is silent.

Common mistakes

Don’t saySayWhy
✗ I remain at your disposal.✓ Please do not hesitate to contact me.A translated formula that sounds unnatural in English.
✗ Actually, unemployment is at 7 %.✓ Currently / At present, unemployment is at 7 %.“Actually” = in fact; for “now”, use currently.
✗ The results are good, however the costs are high.✓ The results are good; however, the costs are high.“However” cannot join two sentences with only a comma.
✗ We can't accept your proposal.✓ We cannot accept your proposal.No contractions in formal writing.
✗ You can see that prices have risen.✓ It can be seen that prices have risen. / As the data show, prices have risen.Generic “you” is too informal.
✗ We need to look into the thing about the delays.✓ The causes of the delays need to be investigated.A phrasal verb and a vague word replaced by precise terms.
✗ In this essay, we will see why…✓ This essay examines why…A translated structure; make the text the subject of the verb.

In a conversation

Listening Studio

In Ottawa, a manager checks the email a new colleague wants to send to a government department.

Hélène

Your email starts with “Hi guys, we got your letter”. That's too casual for a ministry.
The register is wrong for the reader.

David

OK. “Dear Sir or Madam, we have received your letter of 3 March”?
A formal greeting and a precise reference.

Hélène

Much better. And here, “we need to look into it” could be “we will investigate the matter”.
Phrasal verb → single formal verb.

David

Should I also change “a lot of applications”?
He spots a vague quantity.

Hélène

Yes: “a considerable number of applications”. And remove the contractions, “can't” and “won't”.
Two more formal features.

David

I ended with “I remain at your disposal”. Is that all right?
A translated formula.

Hélène

It sounds translated. Try “Should you require any further information, please do not hesitate to contact me.”
Inversion + standard closing sentence.

David

And then “Yours faithfully”, since I don't know their name.
Correct British closing after “Dear Sir or Madam”.

Hélène

Exactly. Formal, but still clear and short.
The key principle.

Exercises

Writing Workshop

From easy to harder. Answer without looking at the lesson, then check each exercise.

1. Formal or neutral?

Say whether the sentence is formal or neutral / informal.

  1. We'll sort it out next week.
  2. The matter will be resolved next week.
  3. Should you wish to attend, please register online.
  4. Loads of people turned up.
Show the answers
  1. neutral / informal
  2. formal
  3. formal
  4. neutral / informal

2. The formal word

Replace the word or phrase in brackets with a formal equivalent.

  1. Applicants [need] a valid passport.
  2. We [asked for] a refund.
  3. The study aims to [find out] the causes.
  4. [Before] the meeting, please read the report.
  5. There is not [enough] evidence.
  6. Our team will [help] you.
Show the answers
  1. require
  2. requested
  3. determine / establish
  4. Prior to
  5. sufficient
  6. assist

3. Rewrite in formal register

Rewrite each sentence in formal English.

  1. We can't give you the money back.
  2. You can see that prices went up a lot.
  3. So we stopped the project.
  4. If you have any questions, just get in touch.
Show the answers
  1. We are unable to issue a refund. / We cannot refund the payment.
  2. It can be seen that prices increased considerably.
  3. Consequently, the project was discontinued.
  4. Should you have any questions, please do not hesitate to contact us.

4. Correct the mistakes

Correct each sentence.

  1. Actually, the firm has 50 employees. (meaning: at present)
  2. In this report, we will see the main risks.
  3. The plan is ambitious, however it is costly.
Show the answers
  1. Currently, the firm has 50 employees.
  2. This report examines the main risks.
  3. The plan is ambitious; however, it is costly.

5. Formal closing and opening sentences

Write the formal sentence that expresses the idea.

  1. Offer more help at the end of a letter.
  2. Say that you have received a request sent on 12 May.
Show the answers
  1. Please do not hesitate to contact me should you require any further information.
  2. We acknowledge receipt of your request of 12 May. / We have received your request of 12 May.

6. Your turn

Rewrite this message in formal register (a letter to a public office): “Hi, I sent you my papers a month ago and I still haven't heard anything. Can you check what's going on? Thanks!”

  1. Example answer
Show the answers
  1. Dear Sir or Madam, I am writing regarding the documents I submitted on 4 March in support of my application. As I have not yet received a response, I would be grateful if you could inform me of the current status of my file. Should any further documents be required, please do not hesitate to contact me. Yours faithfully, Amina Bello
Key points
  • Formal = a set of consistent choices: precise vocabulary, no contractions, impersonal structures, explicit linkers, restrained tone.
  • Prefer require, obtain, request, assist, prior to, sufficient… but avoid pomposity: “use” and “start” are fine.
  • However / moreover / consequently: full stop or semicolon before, comma after.
  • Avoid translated formulas: actually ≠ currently; not “I remain at your disposal”; “This report examines…”, not “we will see”.
