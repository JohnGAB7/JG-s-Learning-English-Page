Would like (asking politely)

“Would like” is the polite way to say what you want: “I'd like a coffee, please.” We also use it to offer and invite: “Would you like some tea?” You need it in restaurants, in shops and at work.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level A1

Prefer explanations in French? Version française de cette leçon →

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Use would like + noun and would like + to + verb, with the short form I'd like.
  • Make a polite request instead of “I want”.
  • Offer and invite with Would you like…? and answer (Yes, please / I'd love to).
  • Know the difference between “like” (enjoy in general) and “would like” (want now).

The form

“Would like” is the same for every subject: I would like, she would like, they would like. No s on “like”! In speech, we almost always use the short form: I'd like, you'd like, she'd like, we'd like.

You can put a noun after it: “I'd like a sandwich.” For a verb, you need “to”: “I'd like to go home.”

Question: “Would you like…?” Short answers: “Yes, please.” / “No, thank you.” (for an offer) or “Yes, I would.” / “No, I wouldn't.”

Answer to an offerYes, please. / No, thank you.Would you like tea? — Yes, please.
The negative “I wouldn't like…” exists but is not common for saying no. To say no politely, say “No, thank you” or “I'd rather not”.

Asking politely

“I want…” is correct, but it can sound direct, even rude, with a waiter, a shop assistant or a stranger. “I'd like…” is the normal polite form.

We often add “please”: “I'd like a ticket to Ghent, please.” In restaurants, you also hear “Can I have…?”, “Could I have…?” and “I'll have…”.

  • I'd like a return ticket to Lyon, please.
    At the station.
  • We'd like to book a table for four.
    On the phone to a restaurant.
  • Could I have the bill, please?
    At the end of a meal (US: the check).

Offering and inviting

“Would you like + noun?” offers something: “Would you like a biscuit?”. “Would you like to + verb?” invites someone: “Would you like to come to my party?”

To accept an invitation: “Yes, I'd love to!” (keep “to” at the end, but don't repeat the verb). To say no politely: “I'd love to, but I can't. I'm busy on Saturday.”

To accept an offer: “Yes, please.”; to say no: “No, thank you.” / “No, thanks.” Don't answer “Yes, I like”!

  • Would you like some more rice? — No, thank you. I'm full.
    full = I have eaten enough.
  • Would you like to have dinner with us on Friday? — I'd love to!
    Accepting an invitation.
  • Would you like to come to the cinema? — I'd love to, but I'm working.
    Saying no politely.

Like or would like?

“Like” (lesson 33) is about general, permanent tastes: “I like coffee” (I enjoy it). “Would like” is about a wish now: “I'd like a coffee” (I want one now).

The questions are very different: “Do you like tea?” asks about your taste; “Would you like a tea?” is an offer.

Like (general taste)Would like (a wish now)
I like dancing.I'd like to dance with you.
Do you like coffee? — Yes, I do.Would you like a coffee? — Yes, please.
She likes travelling.She'd like to travel to Japan.
like + -ing / nounwould like + to + verb / noun

Would like + someone + to

To say what you want another person to do, use “would like + person + to + verb”. Never use “that” here.

This is very useful at work and to introduce people.

  • I'd like you to meet my colleague, Ana.
    Introducing someone.
  • We'd like you to check this document.
    A polite request at work.

The formula

I'd like + noun · I'd like to + verb · Would you like…? — Yes, please. / I'd love to!

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • “would” is /wʊd/: don't say the l. “Would” and “wood” sound the same.
  • In “I'd like”, you hardly hear the d: /aɪd ˈlaɪk/. Listen carefully: “I like” and “I'd like” sound very similar.
  • “Would you” is linked: /ˈwʊdʒu/ or /ˈwʊdʒə/ (“woodjoo”).
  • “to” is weak in “I'd like to go” /tə/, but strong at the end of a sentence: “I'd love to!” /tuː/.
  • An offer goes up at the end: “Would you like some TEA? ↗”.

In a conversation

Listening Studio

In a beach restaurant in Kribi, Cameroon. Nadia and her Belgian colleague Pieter order food.

Waiter

Good evening. Would you like something to drink?
An offer.

Nadia

Yes, please. I'd like a fresh pineapple juice.
I'd like + noun.

Pieter

And I'd like a bottle of water, please.
A polite request.

Waiter

Of course. And what would you like to eat?
Wh- question.

Nadia

I'd like the grilled fish with plantain.
Ordering food.

Pieter

The same for me. I'd like to try the local food.
I'd like + to + verb.

Waiter

Would you like some chilli sauce?
Another offer.

Pieter

Yes, please, but just a little!
Accepting an offer.

Nadia

Pieter, would you like to visit the waterfalls tomorrow?
An invitation.

Pieter

I'd love to!
Accepting an invitation.

Key points
  • I'd like + noun (I'd like a coffee); I'd like + to + verb (I'd like to go). No s, no -ing.
  • “I'd like” is more polite than “I want”.
  • Offer: Would you like…? — Yes, please. / No, thank you.
  • Invitation: Would you like to…? — I'd love to! / I'd love to, but I can't.
  • like = enjoy (in general); would like = want (now). I'd like you to meet… (no “that”).
