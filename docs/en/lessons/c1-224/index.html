C1 · Advanced · lesson 224

Everyday idioms

Everyday idioms (moods, relationships, money, surprises, conversation) are everywhere in series, podcasts and conversations between friends. At C1 you need to understand them instantly and use a few of them with no mistakes in form.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level C1

Prefer explanations in French? Version française de cette leçon →

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Understand about forty everyday idioms, organised by theme.
  • Avoid word-for-word translations of idioms from your own language.
  • Master their grammar: what can change (tense, possessive), what cannot, and why the passive is almost impossible.
  • Recognise shortened proverbs (“Speak of the devil…”, “When in Rome…”) and British and American variants.

Moods and emotions

Many emotion idioms use images of the body or the sky. They are very common in speech and messages; in formal writing, a simple adjective is usually better (delighted, nervous, exhausted).

IdiomMeaningPlain equivalent
be over the moonbe extremely happydelighted
be on cloud ninebe extremely happy, in a dreamy wayelated
be down in the dumpsfeel sad and lowdepressed, miserable
have butterflies in your stomachfeel nervous before somethingnervous
be at the end of your tether (BrE) / rope (AmE)have no patience or energy leftexhausted, desperate
get cold feetsuddenly become too nervous to do somethinglose your nerve
let off steamrelease strong feelings or energyrelax, unwind
a weight off your shouldersa great reliefa relief
  • She was over the moon when she got the scholarship.
    Extremely happy.
  • He got cold feet a week before the wedding.
    He suddenly became too nervous to go ahead.

Relationships

These idioms describe how well people get on, their conflicts and their reconciliations. The image can be surprising: “get on like a house on fire” (get on extremely well) has nothing to do with danger.

IdiomMeaningExample
get on like a house on fireget on extremely wellMy mum and my wife get on like a house on fire.
see eye to eye (with sb)agree (often negative)We don't always see eye to eye on money.
give sb the cold shoulderdeliberately ignore sbAfter the argument, she gave me the cold shoulder.
rub sb up the wrong way (BrE) / rub sb the wrong way (AmE)annoy sbHis tone really rubs me up the wrong way.
clear the airtalk openly to end a tensionWe had a long talk to clear the air.
bury the hatchetend a quarrel and become friends againThe two brothers finally buried the hatchet.
  • We don't see eye to eye on politics, but we're still close.
    “See eye to eye” is mostly used in the negative.

Money, time and the unexpected

Money idioms are essential for talking about the cost of living; idioms of time and surprise appear in every story.

IdiomMeaningExample
cost an arm and a legbe very expensiveRent in Vancouver costs an arm and a leg.
make ends meethave just enough money to liveThey work two jobs to make ends meet.
tighten your beltspend less moneyWe'll have to tighten our belts this year.
splash out (on sth) (BrE)spend a lot on something specialWe splashed out on a new sofa.
on a shoestringwith very little moneyThey travelled around Asia on a shoestring.
out of the bluesuddenly and unexpectedlyHe called me out of the blue after ten years.
once in a blue moonvery rarelyI only eat meat once in a blue moon.
in the nick of timejust in timeWe got to the station in the nick of time.
a blessing in disguisesomething bad that has a good resultLosing that job was a blessing in disguise.
the last strawthe final problem that makes you lose patienceThe noise at 3 a.m. was the last straw.
  • With two children and one salary, it's hard to make ends meet.
    “Make” changes form; the rest is fixed.
  • Missing the bus turned out to be a blessing in disguise.
    The bad event had a good result.

Talking, revealing, keeping quiet

These idioms comment on conversation itself: telling a secret, getting to the point, making a social mistake, guessing right. Note the variants: “beat about the bush” (BrE) / “beat around the bush” (AmE), “put your foot in it” (BrE) / “put your foot in your mouth” (AmE), “take sth with a pinch of salt” (BrE) / “with a grain of salt” (AmE).

IdiomMeaningExample
spill the beans / let the cat out of the bagreveal a secretWho spilled the beans about the party?
beat about the bushavoid saying something directlyStop beating about the bush and tell me.
cut to the chaseget to the main pointLet me cut to the chase: I'm leaving.
hit the nail on the headdescribe something exactly rightYou've hit the nail on the head.
put your foot in itsay something embarrassing by mistakeI put my foot in it by mentioning his ex.
bite your tonguestop yourself from saying somethingI wanted to argue, but I bit my tongue.
take sth with a pinch of saltnot completely believe sthTake online reviews with a pinch of salt.
  • I really put my foot in it when I asked about her job.
    The possessive agrees with the subject (my, his, her…).

The grammar of idioms and shortened proverbs

What changes: the tense of the verb (spill / spilled / has spilled the beans) and the possessive, which agrees with the subject (I bit my tongue, she bit her tongue). What does not change: nouns, articles, prepositions and word order. You cannot say “spill the bean” or “a house in fire”.

The passive is almost always impossible: “The beans were spilled by Tom” loses the idiomatic meaning and sounds like a joke. Keep the active voice.

Native speakers often shorten a well-known proverb: “Speak of the devil!”, “When in Rome…” (do as the Romans do), “Too many cooks…” (spoil the broth). Recognising the beginning is enough to understand the reference.

  • Speak of the devil! We were just talking about you.
    Said when someone arrives just as you mention them (the full proverb ends “and he shall appear”).
  • I don't usually eat insects, but when in Rome…
    A reference to “When in Rome, do as the Romans do”.
Past of “spill”: spilled (everywhere) or spilt (mainly British).

The formula

Verb in the right tense + fixed chunk (nouns, articles, prepositions unchanged) + matching possessive; active voice; informal or neutral register

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • Stress falls on the words that carry the image: over the MOON, a BLESSing in disGUISE, the last STRAW.
  • “tether” is /ˈteðə/ (BrE), with the voiced /ð/ of “the”.
  • “hatchet” is /ˈhætʃɪt/, with the open /æ/ of “cat”.
  • “an arm and a leg” is linked: /ən ˈɑːm ən ə ˈleɡ/, with “and” reduced to /ən/.
  • In shortened proverbs, the voice stays up (rising or level tone) on the last word: “When in ROME…”

Common mistakes

Don’t saySayWhy
✗ It costs the eyes of the head.✓ It costs an arm and a leg.A translation from French; in English it is an arm and a leg.
✗ Stop turning around the pot.✓ Stop beating about / around the bush.The English idiom is “beat about the bush”.
✗ That was the drop that overflowed the vase.✓ That was the last straw.From “the straw that broke the camel's back”.
✗ Talk of the wolf!✓ Speak / Talk of the devil!In English it is the devil, not the wolf.
✗ They get on like a house in fire.✓ They get on like a house on fire.Fixed preposition: “on fire”.
✗ I have butterflies in the stomach.✓ I have butterflies in my stomach.English uses a possessive with parts of the body.
✗ The beans were spilled by my sister.✓ My sister spilled the beans.Idioms are very rarely used in the passive.
✗ She's in the seventh sky.✓ She's on cloud nine. / She's over the moon.English says “cloud nine”.

In a conversation

Listening Studio

Two friends have coffee in Liège; one describes a difficult week.

Chloé

You look exhausted. What's up?
A friendly opening question.

Fatou

Honestly, I'm at the end of my tether. My landlord put the rent up out of the blue.
No patience left; a sudden rise.

Chloé

Again? Rent here costs an arm and a leg already.
It is very expensive.

Fatou

I know. And then my boss gave me the cold shoulder all week. That was the last straw.
He ignored her; the final problem.

Chloé

Did you talk to him? Sometimes you need to clear the air.
Talk openly to end the tension.

Fatou

I did. I didn't beat about the bush. And guess what? He offered me a transfer to the Namur office.
She spoke directly.

Chloé

So the whole thing was a blessing in disguise!
A bad situation with a good result.

Fatou

Exactly. Cheaper rent, and a team I get on with like a house on fire. It's a weight off my shoulders.
Great relationships and a great relief.

Exercises

Writing Workshop

From easy to harder. Answer without looking at the lesson, then check each exercise.

1. Match idiom and meaning

Explain the idiom in plain English.

  1. once in a blue moon
  2. in the nick of time
  3. bury the hatchet
  4. let off steam
Show the answers
  1. very rarely
  2. just in time
  3. end a quarrel and make peace
  4. release strong feelings or energy

2. Complete

Write the missing word of the idiom.

  1. Let me cut to the ___: we can't afford it.
  2. You've hit the nail on the ___.
  3. I wanted to answer back, but I bit my ___.
  4. With prices rising, we all have to tighten our ___.
  5. They get on like a house ___ fire.
  6. My brother put his foot ___ it at dinner.
Show the answers
  1. chase
  2. head
  3. tongue
  4. belts
  5. on
  6. in

3. Choose the idiom

Rewrite the sentence, replacing the words in brackets with an idiom from the lesson.

  1. She was [extremely happy] with her results.
  2. He [suddenly decided not to do it] the day before the race.
  3. Who [revealed the secret]?
  4. We [never agree] with our neighbours.
Show the answers
  1. She was over the moon with her results.
  2. He got cold feet the day before the race.
  3. Who spilled the beans? / Who let the cat out of the bag?
  4. We never see eye to eye with our neighbours.

4. Correct the mistake

Correct the mistake (often a translated or altered idiom).

  1. This phone costs the eyes of the head.
  2. Talk of the wolf, here's Sam!
  3. I always have butterflies in the stomach before exams.
Show the answers
  1. This phone costs an arm and a leg.
  2. Speak of the devil, here's Sam!
  3. I always have butterflies in my stomach before exams.

5. Say it with an idiom

Rewrite the sentence with an idiom.

  1. Stop avoiding the subject and say it.
  2. With one salary, it's hard to have enough money to live.
Show the answers
  1. Stop beating about the bush (AmE: around the bush) and say it.
  2. With one salary, it's hard to make ends meet.

6. Your turn

In 4–5 sentences, tell the story of something unexpected that happened to you. Use at least three idioms from the lesson.

  1. Example answer
Show the answers
  1. Last year, my company closed its Yaoundé office out of the blue, and I was down in the dumps for weeks. Money was tight, so we had to tighten our belts and live on a shoestring. Then a former colleague called me and offered me a job in a start-up. Looking back, losing that job was a blessing in disguise.
Key points
  • Group idioms by theme (emotions, relationships, money, time, conversation) and learn them in full sentences.
  • Only the tense and the possessive change; nouns, articles and prepositions are fixed; no passive.
  • Beware of translating: cost an arm and a leg, beat about the bush, the last straw, speak of the devil.
  • Know the variants: beat about (BrE) / around (AmE) the bush; a pinch (BrE) / a grain (AmE) of salt; the end of your tether (BrE) / rope (AmE).
