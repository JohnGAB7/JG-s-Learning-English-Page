Plural nouns

In English, most nouns take -s in the plural, and you pronounce this -s. But there are spelling rules, irregular plurals (men, children, people) and some nouns with no plural at all (information, advice).

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level A1

Prefer explanations in French? Version française de cette leçon →

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Make regular plurals and use the spelling rules (-es, -ies, -ves).
  • Know the most common irregular plurals.
  • Pronounce the plural -s: /s/, /z/ or /ɪz/.
  • Recognise nouns that are always plural (jeans, trousers) and uncountable nouns with no plural (information, advice, furniture).

Regular plurals and spelling

The general rule is to add -s: a book → two books. Some endings need a different spelling, often to make the word easier to say. These rules are the same as the -s of the present simple (lesson 6).

Adjectives never take -s in English: big houses, red cars (never “bigs houses”).

EndingRuleExamples
most nouns+ scar → cars, day → days, apple → apples
-s, -ss, -sh, -ch, -x, -z+ esbus → buses, glass → glasses, dish → dishes, watch → watches, box → boxes
consonant + yy → iescity → cities, baby → babies, country → countries
vowel + y+ sboy → boys, key → keys, holiday → holidays
-o (common words)+ espotato → potatoes, tomato → tomatoes, hero → heroes
-o (short or foreign words)+ sphoto → photos, piano → pianos, radio → radios, video → videos
  • I've got two brothers and three sisters.
    + s.
  • There are many beautiful cities in Belgium.
    city → cities.
  • Can you wash the dishes?
    dish → dishes.

Nouns ending in -f / -fe

About twelve common nouns ending in -f or -fe change to -ves: knife → knives, wife → wives, life → lives, leaf → leaves, half → halves, shelf → shelves, wolf → wolves, thief → thieves.

Others just add -s: roof → roofs, chief → chiefs, belief → beliefs, chef → chefs, cliff → cliffs. If you are not sure, check in the dictionary.

  • Put the knives in the drawer.
    knife → knives.
  • The leaves are yellow in October.
    leaf → leaves.

Irregular plurals

Some very common nouns have an irregular plural. Learn them by heart: you use them every day.

“People” is the normal plural of “person”: two people, ten people. “Persons” is only for official texts.

  • There are twenty people in the room.

Always plural, or never plural

Some things made of two parts are always plural in English, with a plural verb: trousers, jeans, shorts, glasses (for your eyes), scissors, pyjamas. To count them, say “a pair of”: a pair of jeans, two pairs of glasses. “Clothes” has no singular.

“People” and “police” are always plural: The police are here. People are nice.

On the other hand, some uncountable nouns have no plural in English: information, advice, news (singular verb), furniture, luggage, homework, hair. Don't say “an information” or “informations”. You will study them in lesson 59.

WrongRightWhy
My trouser is new.My trousers are new.always plural
useful informationsuseful informationuncountable
Thanks for your advices.Thanks for your advice.uncountable
The news are good.The news is good.uncountable, singular verb
I've got a lot of luggages.I've got a lot of luggage.uncountable

After numbers and quantity words

After a number bigger than one, and after “these, those, many, a few, both”, a countable noun is plural: two cats, these shoes, many people.

A noun used like an adjective before another noun stays singular: a ten-euro note, a three-day trip, a shoe shop.

  • We need six eggs and two kilos of tomatoes.
    Plural after numbers.
  • It's a two-hour film.
    Noun as adjective: singular.

The formula

noun + s · -s / -sh / -ch / -x + es · consonant + y → ies · -f(e) → ves (often) · irregular: men, women, children, people, feet, teeth

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • The -s sounds like /s/ after p, t, k, f: cats /kæts/, books, cups.
  • It sounds like /z/ after a vowel or a voiced sound: dogs /dɒɡz/, days, cars, knives /naɪvz/.
  • -es / -s sounds like /ɪz/ (one more syllable) after s, z, sh, ch, j, x sounds: buses /ˈbʌsɪz/, watches, boxes, pages /ˈpeɪdʒɪz/.
  • “woman” /ˈwʊmən/ and “women” /ˈwɪmɪn/: the first syllable changes when you speak, not the second.
  • “houses” is /ˈhaʊzɪz/: the s of “house” becomes /z/ in the plural.

In a conversation

Listening Studio

Before a picnic in the Parc de la Tête d'Or in Lyon, two flatmates write a shopping list.

Théo

How many people are coming to the picnic?
people: plural of person.

Nina

Twelve: eight adults and four children.
child → children.

Théo

OK. So we need bread, cheese, tomatoes and some potatoes for a salad.
bread, cheese: uncountable; tomatoes, potatoes: -es.

Nina

And knives, forks and glasses. Have we got enough?
knife → knives.

Théo

We've got six knives. Let's buy plastic ones.
ones = knives.

Nina

No, it's bad for the planet. My parents have got a lot of knives. I can ask them.
them = my parents.

Théo

Good idea. Oh, and the news says it's sunny tomorrow.
news + singular verb (says).

Nina

Great! I need my sunglasses and my new shorts.
Always plural: sunglasses, shorts.

Key points
  • Noun + s; + es after s, sh, ch, x; consonant + y → ies; often -f(e) → ves.
  • Irregular: men, women, children, people, feet, teeth, mice; no change: sheep, fish.
  • The -s sounds /s/, /z/ or /ɪz/ (buses, watches).
  • Always plural: trousers, jeans, glasses, scissors, clothes, people, police.
  • No plural: information, advice, news (singular verb), furniture, luggage, homework.
