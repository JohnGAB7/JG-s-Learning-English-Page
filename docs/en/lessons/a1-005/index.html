“Have got” (possession)

In spoken British English, “have got” is the most common way to talk about things you own, your family, how people look, illnesses and appointments.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level A1

Prefer explanations in French? Version française de cette leçon →

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Use “have got” in the present in positive sentences, negatives and questions, with short forms.
  • Give correct short answers (Yes, I have. / No, she hasn't.).
  • Use “have got” for possessions, family, appearance, health and plans in your diary.
  • Know when you can't use “have got” (actions, age, the past), and use “have / do you have”, which is more common in American English.

Positive sentences

“Have got” has two words: have (or has for he, she, it) + got. It simply means “have”: “got” doesn't add any idea of the past.

When we speak, we almost always use short forms: I've got, she's got. Be careful: “'s” can mean “is” (she's French) or “has” (she's got a car). The word “got” tells you that it means “has”.

SubjectFull formShort formExample
II have gotI've gotI've got a bike.
youyou have gotyou've gotYou've got a message.
he / she / ithe has gothe's gotShe's got two children.
wewe have gotwe've gotWe've got a big garden.
theythey have gotthey've gotThey've got a dog.
Only he / she / it changes: he / she / it HAS got. Never say “she have got”.

Negatives, questions and short answers

Like “to be”, “have got” doesn't use “do”. For the negative, add not after have / has (haven't got, hasn't got). For a question, put have / has before the subject (Have you got…?).

In short answers, use only have / has, without got: Yes, I have. No, she hasn't. Like with “to be”, no short form after Yes: don't say “Yes, I've”.

NegativeQuestionYesNo
I haven't got a car.Have you got a car?Yes, I have.No, I haven't.
He hasn't got a job.Has he got a job?Yes, he has.No, he hasn't.
We haven't got time.Have we got time?Yes, we have.No, we haven't.
They haven't got a TV.Have they got a TV?Yes, they have.No, they haven't.
  • Have you got a pen? — Yes, I have. Here you are.
    A short answer, then you give the pen.
  • She hasn't got any brothers or sisters.
    any in negatives (lesson 31).
  • What have you got in your bag?
    A Wh- question.

What we use “have got” for

Use “have got” for a situation, not an action: things you own, family and friends, how a person looks, illnesses, and plans in your diary.

  • I've got a new phone.
    Possession.
  • We've got three children.
    Family.
  • Amina has got long black hair and brown eyes.
    Appearance.
  • I've got a headache. / He's got a cold.
    Health.
  • Sorry, I've got a meeting at three.
    Plans in your diary.
  • Have you got any questions?
    A common phrase at the end of a presentation.
“Hair” (on your head) is uncountable and has no article: She's got long hair (not “a long hair” or “long hairs”).

When you can't use “have got”

Don't use “have got” for actions. For breakfast, a shower, a good time or a party, use “have” alone: I have breakfast at seven, We have a party on Saturday. (You will see this “have” again with the present simple, lesson 6.)

Don't use it for age (I'm 20, not “I've got 20 years”) or in the past: in the past, just say “had” (I had a dog when I was a child). Don't use it after “to” or a modal either: say “You need to have a passport”, not “to have got”.

WrongRightWhy
I've got breakfast at 7.I have breakfast at 7.Action → have alone.
I've got 25 years.I'm 25.Age → to be.
I had got a cat in 2010.I had a cat in 2010.Past → had.

“Have got” or “have”? British and American English

For possession, you can also use “have” alone, like a normal verb. Then it uses do / does in negatives and questions: I don't have a car. Do you have a car? — Yes, I do.

“Have got” is more common in spoken British English and a little informal. “Have” + do is normal in American English and also very common in the UK, especially in writing. Both are correct. Just don't mix them in one sentence: never say “Do you have got…?” or “Have you got…? — Yes, I do”.

Have got (mostly British)Have (American and British)
PositiveShe's got a car.She has a car.
NegativeShe hasn't got a car.She doesn't have a car.
QuestionHas she got a car?Does she have a car?
Short answerYes, she has. / No, she hasn't.Yes, she does. / No, she doesn't.
In informal American English, you also hear “I got a car” (without have). It is very informal: don't write it.

The formula

I / you / we / they have got ('ve got) · he / she / it has got ('s got) · haven't / hasn't got · Have you got…? — Yes, I have.

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • Short forms “I've got” /aɪv ɡɒt/ and “she's got” /ʃiːz ɡɒt/: the v and z are light but you must hear them, or it sounds like “I got”.
  • “got” is /ɡɒt/ in British English (short, open o) and /ɡɑːt/ in American English.
  • “haven't” /ˈhævnt/ and “hasn't” /ˈhæznt/: two syllables, stress on the first; the final t is often very light.
  • In a question, “have” is weak: Have you got…? /həv juː ɡɒt/. In a short answer, it is strong: Yes, I HAVE /hæv/.
  • Breathe out the h in “have” and “has”. Don't say “I 'ave”.

In a conversation

Listening Studio

Two neighbours talk on the stairs of a building in Liège. One of them is new.

Chloé

Hi! I'm Chloé, your new neighbour. Have you got a minute?
A polite question with have got.

Didier

Sure. Welcome! Have you got everything for the flat?
flat = apartment (British).

Chloé

Almost. But I haven't got a drill. Have you got one?
Negative, then question with “one”.

Didier

Yes, I have. I can bring it tonight. Have you got children?
Short answer without got.

Chloé

Yes, I've got a son. He's six. And he's got a very noisy dog!
Age with “to be”: He's six.

Didier

No problem. We've got a cat, so they can be friends.
We've got = we have got.

Chloé

Great. Oh, sorry, I've got an appointment at five. See you later!
Have got for a plan in your diary.

Didier

See you!
A relaxed goodbye.

Exercises

Writing Workshop

From easy to harder. Answer without looking at the lesson, then check each exercise.

1. Have or has?

Complete with have or has.

  1. I ___ got two sisters.
  2. My father ___ got a beard.
  3. We ___ got a small flat.
  4. ___ she got a bike?
  5. They ___ got a lot of friends.
  6. It ___ got four doors.
Show the answers
  1. have
  2. has
  3. have
  4. Has
  5. have
  6. has

2. Negative and question

Write the sentence in the negative, then as a question.

  1. She's got a car.
  2. You've got my number.
  3. They've got a garden.
  4. He's got a cold.
Show the answers
  1. She hasn't got a car. / Has she got a car?
  2. You haven't got my number. / Have you got my number?
  3. They haven't got a garden. / Have they got a garden?
  4. He hasn't got a cold. / Has he got a cold?

3. Short answers

Give a short answer (yes or no, as shown).

  1. Have you got a pen? (yes)
  2. Has Marie got a brother? (no)
  3. Have they got a dog? (yes)
  4. Has the hotel got a pool? (no)
Show the answers
  1. Yes, I have.
  2. No, she hasn't.
  3. Yes, they have.
  4. No, it hasn't.

4. Have got or another verb?

Each sentence has one mistake. Correct it.

  1. I've got 18 years.
  2. We've got lunch at one o'clock.
  3. Do you have got a pen?
  4. He have got blue eyes.
  5. She's got a long black hair.
Show the answers
  1. I'm 18.
  2. We have lunch at one o'clock.
  3. Have you got a pen? / Do you have a pen?
  4. He has got blue eyes. / He's got blue eyes.
  5. She's got long black hair.

5. Change to “have got”

Rewrite the sentence with “have got” (short form).

  1. My head hurts. (I / a headache)
  2. He doesn't have a car.
  3. Do you have any pets?
  4. We have a meeting at ten.
Show the answers
  1. I've got a headache.
  2. He hasn't got a car.
  3. Have you got any pets?
  4. We've got a meeting at ten.

6. Your turn

Write four true sentences with “have got”: something you have, something you haven't got, your family, and how you look (hair, eyes).

  1. Example answer
Show the answers
  1. I've got a laptop but I haven't got a car. I've got two brothers and a sister. I've got short brown hair and dark eyes.
Key points
  • I / you / we / they've got · he / she / it's got (= has got).
  • Negative: haven't got / hasn't got. Question: Have you got…? Has she got…?
  • Short answer without got: Yes, I have. / No, she hasn't.
  • For possessions and states, never for actions (I have breakfast), age (I'm 25) or the past (I had).
  • The same idea with do: Do you have…? — Yes, I do. Don't mix the two.
