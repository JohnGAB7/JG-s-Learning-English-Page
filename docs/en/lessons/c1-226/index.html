C1 · Advanced · lesson 226

Academic collocations

In a dissertation, an article or a research report, it is word partnerships (“conduct research”, “draw a conclusion”, “a significant difference”) that make a text sound right. Perfect grammar is not enough if the collocations are translated from another language.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level C1

Prefer explanations in French? Version française de cette leçon →

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Use the main verb + noun collocations of research (conduct a study, collect data, draw a conclusion, address an issue).
  • Choose the expected adjective and adverb (a significant difference, closely related, widely accepted).
  • Use the right preposition after key nouns (an increase in, an impact on, research into).
  • Avoid typical learner mistakes (make a research, an important number of, realise a study).

Why collocations matter

A collocation is a combination of words that native speakers use habitually. Logically nothing stops you saying “make a conclusion”, but English says “draw / reach a conclusion”. Readers notice the difference at once, and the text loses credibility.

In academic English, four types dominate: verb + noun (conduct research), adjective + noun (compelling evidence), adverb + adjective or verb (closely linked, clearly demonstrate) and noun + preposition (an increase in). Learn them as chunks, with an example.

  • We conducted a survey of 500 households.
    “Conduct”: the neutral, formal verb for a survey or study.
  • The study draws three main conclusions.
    You “draw” a conclusion, never “make” one.

Verb + noun: doing research and arguing

These collocations describe the stages of research: designing, collecting, analysing, concluding and discussing. “Do research” is possible in speech, but “conduct” or “carry out” is preferred in writing.

StageCollocationsExample
Doing the studyconduct / carry out research, a study, an experiment, a surveyThe team carried out a series of experiments.
Datacollect / gather / analyse dataData were collected over six months.
Hypothesisput forward / test / support / reject a hypothesisThe results support our initial hypothesis.
Evidenceprovide / present / find evidenceThe study provides strong evidence of a link.
Problemsraise / address / tackle an issue; pose a problem / a threatThis chapter addresses the issue of access.
Roleplay a (key / crucial) role inEducation plays a crucial role in mobility.
Conclusiondraw / reach / come to a conclusionIt is too early to draw firm conclusions.
Contributionshed / throw light on; fill / bridge a gap (in the literature)This paper fills a gap in the literature.
  • Several questions are raised by these findings.
    “Raise a question”, not “rise”.
  • The report fails to address the issue of cost.
    “Address an issue” = deal with it (academic meaning of address).

Adjective + noun: describing precisely

An academic adjective is chosen for precision, not intensity. “A big difference” is informal; “a significant difference” is expected (and in statistics means the difference is not due to chance).

The words “evidence”, “research” and “data” have their usual adjectives: compelling / conclusive / limited evidence, extensive / recent research, reliable / limited data.

NounFrequent adjectivesExample
evidencestrong, compelling, conclusive, limited, conflictingThere is conflicting evidence on this point.
differencesignificant, marked, slight, considerableWe found a marked difference between groups.
increase / declinesharp, steady, substantial, gradualThere was a sharp decline in enrolment.
role / factorkey, crucial, major, contributingPrice is a key factor in consumer choice.
researchextensive, recent, further, empiricalFurther research is needed.
consensus / beliefbroad, general / widespread, commonThere is a broad consensus among economists.
bodya growing / large body of research / evidenceA growing body of research links sleep and memory.
  • A growing body of evidence suggests that the policy is effective.
    The verb agrees with “body” (suggests).

Adverb + adjective or verb

Adverbs “stick” to certain adjectives and verbs. We say “closely related”, “widely used” and “highly significant”, but not “highly used”.

CollocationMeaningExample
closely related / linkedstrongly connectedIncome is closely linked to education.
widely accepted / usedaccepted / used by many peopleThis model is widely used in finance.
highly significant / effectivevery significant / effectiveThe effect was highly significant.
statistically significantnot likely to be due to chanceThe difference is not statistically significant.
largely due tomainly caused byThe rise is largely due to inflation.
heavily dependent onrelying very much onThe region is heavily dependent on tourism.
clearly demonstrate / critically examine / briefly outlineshow clearly / examine carefully and critically / describe in a few wordsThis section briefly outlines the method.
  • Unemployment is closely linked to the level of qualification.
    Adverb + participle + preposition.

Noun + preposition and fixed phrases

The preposition after an academic noun must be learnt. “An increase of 5 %” gives the amount; “an increase in prices” gives what changes. Also: the impact on, research into / on.

NounPrepositionExample
increase / decrease / rise / declinein (what changes) / of (the amount)a rise in prices of 4 %
impact / effect / influenceonthe effect of stress on memory
researchinto / onresearch into renewable energy
approachtoa new approach to teaching
evidenceof / forevidence of fraud / evidence for a theory
the extentto whichWe examine the extent to which age matters.
fixed phrasesin terms of, with respect to, on the basis of, in contrast toOn the basis of these results, we recommend…
  • The report measures the impact of the reform on small businesses.
    impact OF the reform ON small businesses.

Common traps

“Research”, “evidence”, “information” and “advice” are uncountable: no “researches”, “evidences” or “informations”. To count, say “a study”, “studies”, “a piece of evidence”.

“Data” is a Latin plural: “the data show” is standard in many scientific journals, but “the data shows” (uncountable) is now very common and accepted. Be consistent throughout your text.

False friends: “realise” = become aware (to carry out a study: conduct); “an experience” = something you live through (a scientific test = an experiment); “important” does not describe a quantity (an important number → a large number).

  • Two recent studies confirm this result.
    To count pieces of research, use “studies”, not “researches”.
  • The data show / The data shows a clear trend.
    Both are used; choose one and keep it throughout the text.

The formula

Verb + noun (conduct a study, draw a conclusion) · adjective + noun (a significant difference) · adverb + adjective (closely linked) · noun + preposition (an increase in, an impact on)

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • “conduct” changes stress: the verb is /kənˈdʌkt/ (second syllable), the noun /ˈkɒndʌkt/ (first syllable).
  • “research” is /rɪˈsɜːtʃ/ (BrE, stressed on the second syllable); in American English the noun is also often /ˈriːsɜːrtʃ/.
  • “data”: /ˈdeɪtə/ (most common) or /ˈdɑːtə/; both are correct.
  • “hypothesis” is /haɪˈpɒθəsɪs/; the plural “hypotheses” ends in /iːz/.
  • “analyse” (BrE) / “analyze” (AmE) is /ˈænəlaɪz/; the noun “analysis” /əˈnæləsɪs/ moves the stress.

Common mistakes

Don’t saySayWhy
✗ We made a research on migration.✓ We conducted research into / on migration.“Research” is uncountable and goes with conduct / carry out.
✗ An important number of students failed.✓ A large / significant number of students failed.“Important” does not measure quantity in English.
✗ We realised a study in 2024.✓ We conducted / carried out a study in 2024.“Realise” = become aware.
✗ They did an experience in the lab.✓ They carried out an experiment in the lab.A scientific test is an experiment.
✗ We can make the conclusion that…✓ We can draw the conclusion that… / We can conclude that…You “draw” a conclusion.
✗ There are many evidences of a link.✓ There is a great deal of evidence of a link.“Evidence” is uncountable.
✗ The increase of prices was sharp.✓ The increase in prices was sharp.“Increase in” + what rises; “of” + the amount.
✗ This rises an important question.✓ This raises an important question.“Raise” (transitive) ≠ “rise” (intransitive).

In a conversation

Listening Studio

At the University of Buea, a PhD student goes through the introduction of her article with her supervisor.

Dr Mbah

Your first sentence says “We made a research on mobile banking.” Let's fix that.
A typical collocation mistake.

Esther

“We conducted research into mobile banking”?
Correct verb, uncountable noun, right preposition.

Dr Mbah

Perfect. And “an important number of users”?
A false friend.

Esther

“A significant number of users”… or “a large number”, since it's not a statistical test.
She thinks about the statistical meaning of “significant”.

Dr Mbah

Good thinking. Then you write that mobile money “has a big role”.
An informal, imprecise phrase.

Esther

It “plays a crucial role in financial inclusion”.
play + role + in.

Dr Mbah

Exactly. And how would you describe your contribution?
Asking about the paper's value.

Esther

“This paper fills a gap in the literature and sheds light on rural users.”
Two classic contribution collocations.

Dr Mbah

Excellent. Now it sounds like a real journal article.
Approval.

Exercises

Writing Workshop

From easy to harder. Answer without looking at the lesson, then check each exercise.

1. Choose the verb

Choose the verb that forms the correct collocation.

  1. to ___ a conclusion (make / draw)
  2. to ___ a survey (conduct / realise)
  3. to ___ a question (raise / rise)
  4. to ___ a crucial role (have / play)
  5. to ___ light on a problem (throw / give)
Show the answers
  1. draw
  2. conduct
  3. raise
  4. play
  5. throw (or shed)

2. Complete with the adjective or adverb

Complete with: growing, closely, widely, sharp, conflicting, significant.

  1. There was a ___ decline in sales in March.
  2. Income is ___ linked to education.
  3. This method is ___ used in medicine.
  4. A ___ body of research supports this view.
  5. Studies have produced ___ evidence on this point.
  6. The difference between the two groups was not statistically ___.
Show the answers
  1. sharp
  2. closely
  3. widely
  4. growing
  5. conflicting
  6. significant

3. The right preposition

Complete with the correct preposition.

  1. an increase ___ unemployment
  2. the impact of social media ___ teenagers
  3. research ___ climate change
  4. a new approach ___ the problem
Show the answers
  1. in
  2. on
  3. into (or on)
  4. to

4. Correct the mistake

Correct each sentence.

  1. We made a research on rural schools.
  2. An important number of patients improved.
  3. The researchers did an experience on mice.
Show the answers
  1. We conducted research into / on rural schools.
  2. A large / significant number of patients improved.
  3. The researchers carried out an experiment on mice.

5. Rewrite academically

Rewrite each sentence with an academic collocation.

  1. It's too early to say anything for sure.
  2. This rise is mainly because of inflation.
Show the answers
  1. It is too early to draw firm conclusions.
  2. This increase is largely due to inflation.

6. Your turn

Write a short paragraph (4 sentences) presenting a real or imaginary study. Use at least five collocations from the lesson.

  1. Example answer
Show the answers
  1. We conducted a survey of 800 small farmers in the West Region in order to examine the impact of mobile money on savings. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews and analysed using regression models. The findings show a significant increase in savings among users, which is largely due to lower transaction costs. These results shed light on an area that has received little attention, although further research is needed.
Key points
  • Learn collocations as chunks: conduct a study, collect data, draw a conclusion, address an issue, play a role.
  • Precise rather than intense adjectives: a significant difference, a sharp decline, compelling evidence, a growing body of research.
  • Prepositions: an increase in, an impact on, research into, an approach to, the extent to which.
  • Uncountable: research, evidence, information; false friends: realise, experience, important (for a quantity).
