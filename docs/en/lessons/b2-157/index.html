Inversion after negative expressions

“Never have I seen…”, “Not only did she…”, “No sooner had we arrived than…”: when a negative or restrictive expression starts the sentence, English puts the auxiliary before the subject, as in a question. It is a way of adding emphasis, typical of speeches, articles and careful writing, and very popular in exams (FCE, IELTS, TOEIC).

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Recognise the expressions that trigger inversion (never, rarely, little, not only, under no circumstances, not until, only when…).
  • Make the inversion with the right auxiliary, including do / does / did.
  • Use the two-part structures: no sooner… than, hardly… when, not only… but also.
  • Know when not to invert and choose the right register.

The principle

In a normal sentence, the negative adverb goes near the verb: “I have never seen such a mess.” For emphasis, you can put it at the start of the sentence. Then you need question order: auxiliary + subject + verb. “Never have I seen such a mess.”

The effect is solemn, dramatic or very written. In everyday speech, you hear it in speeches, sports commentary, advertising, or in jokes when people exaggerate.

After these expressions in first position, inversion is obligatory: “Never I have seen…” is wrong.

  • I have never felt so proud. → Never have I felt so proud.
    Neutral → emphatic.
  • She rarely makes mistakes. → Rarely does she make mistakes.
    No auxiliary: add “does”.
  • We had seldom seen such enthusiasm. → Seldom had we seen such enthusiasm.
    Past perfect: “had” comes first.

Expressions that trigger inversion

They are negative adverbs or phrases (never, at no time, under no circumstances), adverbs of very low frequency (rarely, seldom, hardly ever), or restrictive phrases starting with “only” or “not”.

Choosing the auxiliary

Follow the rules for questions: if there is already an auxiliary (be, have, a modal), it moves before the subject. If not, add do / does / did and use the base form of the verb.

When “be” is the main verb, “be” itself moves: “Rarely is he late.”

The negative adverb already contains the negative idea, so do not add “not”: “Never have I seen…”, not “Never haven't I seen…”.

TenseNeutral sentenceInversion
Present simpleHe seldom calls me.Seldom does he call me.
Past simpleI didn't once see her smile.Not once did I see her smile.
BeHe is rarely late.Rarely is he late.
Present perfectI have never been so tired.Never have I been so tired.
ModalYou should not open it in any circumstances.Under no circumstances should you open it.
FutureYou won't find a better price anywhere.Nowhere will you find a better price.

Not until, only when, only after, only by

With “Not until…” and “Only + a time or method expression”, the inversion happens in the main clause, not in the clause after “until / when / after”.

So the time clause keeps normal word order: “Not until I got home did I realise I had lost my wallet.” (not “did I get home”).

“Only” directly before a subject does not cause inversion: “Only John knows the code” (nobody else knows it).

  • Not until the end of the film did we understand the title.
    We only understood at the end.
  • Only when the rain stopped did we leave the shelter.
    Inversion in the main clause only.
  • Only after three interviews was she offered the job.
    Passive: “was” comes first.
  • Only by working together can we solve this problem.
    Method: only by + -ing.

Two-part structures

“Not only… but (also)…”: invert the first part only. “Not only did she write the report, but she also presented it.”

“No sooner… than” and “Hardly / Scarcely / Barely… when” say that one thing happened immediately after another. They are almost always used with the past perfect in the first part and the past simple in the second.

Trap: “no sooner” goes with “than” (it is a comparative); “hardly / scarcely” go with “when” (sometimes “before”). Don't mix them.

  • Not only is the hotel cheap, but it's also right next to the beach.
    Not only… but also.
  • No sooner had I sat down than the phone rang.
    Immediately after I sat down.
  • Hardly had we left the house when it started to pour.
    Immediately after we left.
  • Scarcely had the match begun when the lights went out.
    scarcely: more literary.

Register and neutral alternatives

Inversion is a choice of style. In an ordinary email or conversation, the neutral version sounds more natural: “I've never seen anything like it.” Used too often, inversion sounds pompous.

It is very effective in a speech, at the end of an essay, in an official notice (“On no account should passengers leave their luggage unattended”), or for a humorous effect.

Don't confuse it with conditional inversion (Had I known…, Should you need…), studied in lesson 160, or with emphatic do / does / did (lesson 168).

  • Neutral: I didn't realise until later. / Emphatic: Only later did I realise.
    Same meaning, different register.
  • On no account should passengers leave their luggage unattended.
    Official notice.
  • Never again will I eat seven doughnuts in one go!
    Humorous effect.

The formula

Negative / restrictive expression first + auxiliary + subject + verb (Never have I… / Rarely does she… / Not until X did we…)

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • The main stress falls on the negative expression at the start: “NEVer have I…”, “NOT ONCE did he…”. It carries the emphasis.
  • The inverted auxiliary is usually weak: “Never have I seen” /ˈnevə həv aɪ ˈsiːn/, “Rarely does she” /ˈreəli dəz ʃi/.
  • No comma and no pause after the expression: it forms one block with the auxiliary and the subject.
  • “seldom” /ˈseldəm/, “scarcely” /ˈskeəsli/, “circumstances” /ˈsɜːkəmstænsɪz/ or /ˈsɜːkəmstɑːnsɪz/.
  • In “No sooner… than”, “than” is weak: /ðən/.

Common mistakes

Don’t saySayWhy
✗ Never I have seen such a mess.✓ Never have I seen such a mess.After “never” at the start: auxiliary + subject.
✗ Rarely he goes out.✓ Rarely does he go out.With no auxiliary, add does and use the base form.
✗ Not only she speaks Arabic, but she also speaks Wolof.✓ Not only does she speak Arabic, but she also speaks Wolof.Invert the first part.
✗ No sooner had I arrived when the show began.✓ No sooner had I arrived than the show began.no sooner… than; hardly… when.
✗ Not until I got home I realised.✓ Not until I got home did I realise.Invert the main clause.
✗ Only when did the manager arrive, we started.✓ Only when the manager arrived did we start.The time clause keeps normal order; the main clause is inverted.
✗ Little he knew about the plan.✓ Little did he know about the plan.“Little” first (negative meaning): inversion with did.

In a conversation

Listening Studio

In Douala, at a farewell party, Mrs Mbarga, a director who is retiring, gives a speech. Afterwards, two employees, Serge and Laure, talk about it.

Mrs Mbarga

When I joined this company in 1992, little did I know that I would stay for thirty years.
Little did I know = I had no idea.

Mrs Mbarga

Not only have you been wonderful colleagues, but you have also become my second family.
Not only + inversion… but also.

Mrs Mbarga

Never have I worked with people so generous. Thank you, all of you.
Formal, emotional style.

Serge

What a speech! Hardly had she started when people began to cry.
Hardly had… when: immediately after.

Laure

I know. Only when she mentioned the 1998 floods did I realise how much she had done for us.
Inversion in the main clause only.

Serge

She never complained, did she? Not once did I hear her raise her voice.
Not once + did + subject.

Laure

True. Seldom do you find a boss like that.
Seldom + do + you.

Serge

And under no circumstances will I let the new director change her coffee machine!
Humorous use of a formal structure.

Exercises

Writing Workshop

From easy to harder. Answer without looking at the lesson, then check each exercise.

1. Choose

Choose the correct word order.

  1. Never (I have / have I) eaten such spicy food.
  2. Rarely (does she / she does) arrive late.
  3. Not until I saw the photos (I understood / did I understand).
  4. Only John (knows / does John know) the password.
Show the answers
  1. have I
  2. does she
  3. did I understand
  4. knows (“only” + subject: no inversion)

2. Complete

Complete with the auxiliary + subject + the verb in brackets.

  1. Rarely ___ (she / complain) about her job.
  2. Never ___ (I / see) such a beautiful sunset.
  3. Under no circumstances ___ (you / should / open) this door.
  4. Not until the next morning ___ (we / discover) the truth.
  5. No sooner ___ (he / leave) than it started to rain.
  6. Only by working together ___ (we / can / solve) this problem.
Show the answers
  1. does she complain
  2. have I seen
  3. should you open
  4. did we discover
  5. had he left
  6. can we solve

3. Rewrite

Rewrite the sentence starting with the word or phrase in brackets.

  1. I have never been so embarrassed. (Never)
  2. He had no idea that he was being followed. (Little)
  3. We had just started the meeting when the power went off. (Hardly)
  4. She wrote the report and she also presented it. (Not only)
  5. You must not leave your bag unattended in any circumstances. (Under no circumstances)
Show the answers
  1. Never have I been so embarrassed.
  2. Little did he know that he was being followed.
  3. Hardly had we started the meeting when the power went off.
  4. Not only did she write the report, but she also presented it.
  5. Under no circumstances must (should) you leave your bag unattended.

4. Correct the mistakes

Each sentence has one mistake. Write it correctly.

  1. Rarely he goes to the cinema.
  2. No sooner had we arrived when the show began.
  3. Not until I read the letter I understood.
  4. Only when did the manager arrive, we started.
Show the answers
  1. Rarely does he go to the cinema.
  2. No sooner had we arrived than the show began. (or: Hardly had we arrived when the show began.)
  3. Not until I read the letter did I understand.
  4. Only when the manager arrived did we start.

5. Back to neutral

Rewrite each emphatic sentence in neutral, everyday English (no inversion).

  1. Never have I seen so many people.
  2. Hardly had he arrived when the phone rang.
  3. Only after the call did I understand.
  4. Under no circumstances should you share your password.
Show the answers
  1. I have never seen so many people.
  2. He had only just arrived when the phone rang.
  3. I only understood after the call. (or: I didn't understand until after the call.)
  4. You should never share your password.

6. Your turn

Write a short speech (4–5 sentences) to thank a team, a teacher or a friend, with at least four different inversions.

  1. Example answer
Show the answers
  1. When I started this course, little did I imagine how much I would learn. Never have I had such a patient teacher. Not only did you explain grammar clearly, but you also gave us confidence. Only now do I realise how far we have come. Seldom does a class end with so many smiles.
Key points
  • Negative or restrictive expression at the start → auxiliary + subject (question order).
  • With no auxiliary, add do / does / did: Rarely does she…, Not once did he…
  • Not until / Only when / Only after: the inversion is in the main clause.
  • No sooner… than; Hardly / Scarcely… when, with the past perfect in the first part.
  • Formal or emphatic: use it with care; “Only John knows” has no inversion.
