Discourse markers in speech

Well, so, anyway, I mean, mind you, the thing is… These small words carry almost no meaning, but they organise conversation, show your attitude and give you time to think. Used well, they make the difference between correct English and truly fluent English.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level C1

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Understand what discourse markers do: organise, rephrase, qualify and manage turns.
  • Use the essential markers with the right meaning: well, so, right, anyway, actually, I mean, mind you, then again.
  • React naturally as a listener (fair enough, I see, exactly, no way).
  • Replace fillers translated from your own language with real English equivalents.

What do markers do?

A discourse marker does not change the content of a sentence, but it tells the listener how to interpret it: “Well…” announces a qualified answer or a reservation, “Anyway…” brings the conversation back to the main topic, “Mind you…” adds a restriction.

They are not “mistakes” or hesitations to remove: native speakers use them all the time. The problem is overuse (“like” in every sentence) or misuse, when learners transfer the fillers of their first language.

They usually come at the start of an utterance, form their own short intonation group and are often followed by a tiny pause.

  • Well, it's not quite that simple.
    “Well”: an answer with a reservation, not a simple yes.
  • Anyway, where was I? Oh yes, the budget.
    “Anyway”: back to the topic after a digression.

Organising: opening, moving on, changing topic

These markers structure the conversation like road signs. “Right” and “OK” open or close a stage; “so” introduces a conclusion or a new question; “by the way” starts a digression; “anyway” ends it.

MarkerFunctionExample
Right / OK / Nowopen, move to the next stageRight, let's get started.
Sorestart, conclude, introduce a questionSo, what did the doctor say?
By the way / Incidentallyintroduce a side topicBy the way, did you get my email?
Speaking of whichbuild on what was just saidSpeaking of which, how's your new flat?
Anywayend the digression, return to the topicAnyway, as I was saying…
As I was saying / Going back to…pick up an interrupted threadGoing back to the timetable, …
  • Right, I think we've covered everything. Shall we stop there?
    “Right” to close a stage.

Rephrasing, clarifying, correcting

When you are looking for words or want to be more exact, English uses “I mean”, “or rather”, “in other words”, “let me put it another way”.

“Actually” is mainly used to correct or politely contradict what was just said, or to give surprising information: “Actually, it was Tuesday, not Monday.” It is not a filler for every sentence.

“Sort of” and “kind of” soften a word (“it's sort of complicated”); very common in speech, best avoided in formal writing.

  • It was a good meeting. I mean, nothing was decided, but people talked.
    “I mean” clarifies or qualifies what came before.
  • “You're from Lyon, right?” “Actually, I'm from Grenoble.”
    “Actually”: a polite correction.
  • It's kind of hard to explain.
    “Kind of”: informal softening.

Showing attitude, qualifying, contrasting

These markers show your position. “Mind you” (mainly BrE) adds a restriction to what you just said; “then again” gives the other side of the question; “still” keeps an idea in spite of everything; “after all” reminds people of an obvious reason.

“The thing is…” introduces the real problem; “to be honest / frankly” announces a direct opinion; “look” or “listen” at the start of a sentence signals firmness (even irritation), so use them carefully.

MarkerFunctionExample
Mind you (BrE)add a reservationThe hotel was great. Mind you, it was expensive.
Then againgive the other point of viewIt's far. Then again, the trains are fast.
Stillmaintain an idea despite somethingIt rained all week. Still, we had fun.
After allremind of an obvious reasonLet him rest. After all, he's been ill.
The thing isintroduce the real problemI'd love to come. The thing is, I'm working.
To be honest / Franklyannounce a frank opinionTo be honest, I didn't enjoy the film.
At the end of the daywhat really matters in the endAt the end of the day, it's your decision.
Look / Listeninsist, take control (firm)Look, I understand, but we can't change it now.
  • The salary isn't great. Then again, the team is lovely.
    “Then again”: the opposite argument.

Responding as a listener

In English, listeners show they are following with short responses (backchannelling). Staying silent can seem cold or uninterested, especially on the phone.

Agreement: “Exactly.”, “Absolutely.”, “Fair enough.” (= that's reasonable, I accept it). Understanding: “I see.”, “Right.”, “Mm-hm.”. Surprise: “Really?”, “No way!” (informal), “You're joking!”. Sympathy: “Oh no.”, “That's a shame.”, “I know.”

  • “I can't come because my daughter's ill.” “Oh, fair enough.”
    “Fair enough”: you accept the reason.
  • “They've cancelled the trip.” “No way! Why?”
    “No way!”: surprise, informal.

Fillers that don't translate

Learners often translate the fillers of their first language word for word, which produces strange sentences. French speakers, for example, say “After, …” for “That said, …” or “Finally, …” for “I mean, …”. Learn the real English equivalents.

IdeaTranslated (avoid)Natural English

“that said”After, it depends.Then again / That said, it depends.
closing a storyVoilà.So there you go. / That's it, really.
a tag at the end of a sentence…what.…you know.

The formula

Marker at the start + tiny pause: organise (right, so, anyway) · rephrase (I mean, actually) · qualify (mind you, then again, still) · respond (fair enough, I see)

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • Hesitant “Well” is often lengthened with a fall-rise: “We-ell…”; it signals a reservation.
  • “So” to restart is light and short; “So?” alone with a rise means “And what of it?”, which can sound rude.
  • “Actually” often reduces to /ˈæktʃli/ (three syllables instead of four) in fast speech.
  • Opening “Right” falls firmly; “…, right?” rising at the end asks for confirmation.
  • “Fair enough”: stress on “nough” /feər ɪˈnʌf/, falling tone.

Common mistakes

Don’t saySayWhy
✗ After, it depends on the price.✓ Then again / That said, it depends on the price.“After” is not a discourse marker for “that said”.
✗ In fact, I went to the shop. In fact, it was closed.✓ I went to the shop, but it was actually closed.“Actually” highlights unexpected information; don't repeat “in fact”.
✗ Finally, I mean, it wasn't bad.✓ Well, I mean, it wasn't bad.“Finally” means “at the end”, not “what I mean is”.
✗ Good, let's start.✓ Right, let's start. / OK, let's start.To organise, use right / OK.
✗ It was expensive, voilà.✓ It was expensive, you know. / So there you go.“Voilà” at the end is not natural in English.
✗ Eventually, by the way, we need to finish the report.✓ Anyway, we need to finish the report.To return to the main topic: anyway.
✗ Like, I was, like, so tired, like.✓ I was so tired.Repeated “like” is very informal and irritating outside casual chat.

In a conversation

Listening Studio

Two colleagues chat during a coffee break at a company in Quebec City.

Marc

So, how was the conference in Halifax?
“So” opens a new topic.

Leïla

Well, the talks were interesting. Mind you, the hotel was miles from the venue.
A positive answer with a reservation.

Marc

Oh no. Did you have to take taxis?
Sympathy.

Leïla

Actually, there was a shuttle. By the way, I met your old manager there.
A correction, then a digression.

Marc

No way! Speaking of which, is she still at the bank?
Surprise, then building on the topic.

Leïla

She's left, actually. Anyway, the thing is, they've offered me a job.
Back to the main news, and the real point.

Marc

Really? Are you going to take it?
Surprise and interest.

Leïla

I don't know. The salary's better. Then again, I'd have to move.
Both sides of the question.

Marc

Fair enough. At the end of the day, it's your call.
Acceptance and what matters in the end.

Exercises

Writing Workshop

From easy to harder. Answer without looking at the lesson, then check each exercise.

1. What is the function?

Give the function of the marker: return to the topic, correct, add a reservation, give the other point of view.

  1. Anyway, as I was saying, the deadline is Friday.
  2. Actually, the meeting is at ten, not nine.
  3. The flat is lovely. Mind you, it's noisy.
  4. It's a long trip. Then again, the scenery is amazing.
Show the answers
  1. return to the topic
  2. correct
  3. add a reservation
  4. give the other point of view

2. Choose the marker

Complete with: by the way, after all, the thing is, still, fair enough, I mean.

  1. I'd love to help. ___, I'm away that weekend.
  2. “I can't come, I'm ill.” “Oh, ___.”
  3. Don't be too hard on him. ___, it's his first week.
  4. It was a difficult year. ___, we finished the project.
  5. ___, have you seen my keys?
  6. The film was fine. ___, it wasn't brilliant, but I enjoyed it.
Show the answers
  1. The thing is
  2. fair enough
  3. After all
  4. Still
  5. By the way
  6. I mean

3. Respond

Suggest a natural listener response.

  1. “We've won the contract!”
  2. “I can't stay late, I have to pick up my son.”
  3. “My flight was cancelled.”
Show the answers
  1. Really? That's fantastic! / No way! Brilliant!
  2. Fair enough. / Of course, no problem.
  3. Oh no! That's a shame. / Oh no, what happened?

4. Correct the translated filler

Replace the unnatural marker.

  1. After, it's your choice.
  2. Good, let's begin the meeting.
  3. The train was late, so of the blow I missed my interview.
Show the answers
  1. Then again / That said, it's your choice.
  2. Right / OK, let's begin the meeting.
  3. The train was late, so I missed my interview.

5. Add the marker

Rewrite the sentence, starting with a suitable marker.

  1. Let's stop talking about this; see you tomorrow.
  2. The real problem is that I don't have time.
  3. On the other hand, the price is reasonable. (use a spoken marker)
Show the answers
  1. Anyway, see you tomorrow.
  2. The thing is, I don't have time.
  3. Then again, the price is reasonable.

6. Your turn

Write a short exchange (5–6 lines) in which you tell a friend some news. Use at least five different markers.

  1. Example answer
Show the answers
  1. A: So, guess what? I've been offered a place at the University of Montreal. B: No way! That's amazing! A: Well, it's not all good news. The thing is, there's no scholarship. B: Oh, I see. Then again, you could work part-time. A: True. Mind you, rent there isn't cheap. Anyway, I'll decide next week.
Key points
  • Markers organise (right, so, by the way, anyway), rephrase (I mean, actually), qualify (mind you, then again, still, after all) and show listening (fair enough, I see).
  • “Actually” corrects or surprises; it is not a filler for every sentence.
  • They come first, followed by a tiny pause; in formal writing, replace them with real linkers (however, in addition).
  • Don't translate your own fillers: “that said” = then again, not “after”; “I mean”, not “finally”.
