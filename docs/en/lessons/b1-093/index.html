The past perfect

The past perfect (had + past participle) is the “past of the past”: it shows that one past action happened before another past action. You need it to tell stories clearly and to report what people said.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level B1

Prefer explanations in French? Version française de cette leçon →

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Form the past perfect in all its forms and recognise the contraction 'd.
  • Show that one past action happened before another, especially with when, after, before, by the time, already and never.
  • Know when the past perfect is necessary and when the past simple is enough.
  • Meet the past perfect continuous (had been + -ing) for duration up to a past moment.

Form: had + past participle

We make the past perfect with had (the same for every subject) and the past participle: I had finished, she had left, they had gone.

In speech, had becomes 'd: I'd, you'd, she'd, we'd, they'd. Careful: 'd can also mean would (I'd like). The next word tells you: 'd + participle = had (I'd seen); 'd + base verb = would (I'd see).

Negative: hadn't + participle. Question: Had + subject + participle? Short answer: Yes, she had. / No, she hadn't.

FormStructureExample
Affirmativesubject + had + participleThe film had started when we arrived.
Negativesubject + hadn't + participleI hadn't eaten all day.
QuestionHad + subject + participle?Had you met him before?
Wh- questionWh- + had + subject + participle?Where had she put the keys?
Short answerYes, subject + had. / No, subject + hadn't.Had they left? – Yes, they had.

Main use: the earlier action

When we tell a story in the past simple, we use the past perfect to go back to an even earlier action. Imagine a timeline: action 1 (past perfect) → action 2 (past simple) → now.

Compare: “When I arrived, the train left” = the train left at the moment I arrived (or just after). “When I arrived, the train had left” = the train was already gone; I missed it.

  • When we got to the cinema, the film had already started.
    The film started first, then we arrived.
  • She was nervous because she had never flown before.
    No experience before that moment.
  • I recognised him because I had seen his photo.
    Seeing the photo came first.
  • They had sold the house by the time we called.
    The sale happened before the call.

Signal words

Some words often go with the past perfect because they show that something came earlier: already, just, never… before, by the time, by + date, until, after, before, when, because.

By the time is very typical: “By the time the police arrived, the thieves had escaped.”

WordExampleMeaning
alreadyThe guests had already left.before that moment
justI had just sat down when the phone rang.a very short time before
never … beforeI had never seen snow before that trip.not at any time before
by the timeBy the time I woke up, she had gone.when (something had already happened)
by + timeBy 2010, he had written five novels.not later than that time
afterAfter she had finished, she went out.following the end of

When the past simple is enough

If we tell actions in the order they happened, the past simple is enough: “I opened the door, switched on the light and sat down.”

With after and before, the order is already clear, so the past perfect is possible but not necessary: “After she finished / had finished, she went out.” The past perfect stresses that the first action was completely finished.

A common mistake is to use the past perfect for the “distant past”. Distance in time doesn't matter; the order between two past moments does. “The Romans built this bridge” (not “had built”) if no later past event is mentioned.

  • I got up, had a shower and left.
    Chronological order: past simple only.
  • Before I left, I checked the windows.
    Before makes the order clear: past simple is fine.

Reported speech and the past perfect continuous

We also use the past perfect when we report words or thoughts in the past: “She said she had lost her phone” (her words: “I've lost / I lost my phone”). Lessons 104 and 105 go further.

The past perfect continuous (had been + -ing) shows that an activity continued up to a moment in the past, just as the present perfect continuous does up to now: “We had been waiting for two hours when the bus finally arrived.” It can also explain past signs: “Her eyes were red: she had been crying.”

  • He told me he had already paid.
    Reported speech: one step back in time.
  • I had been working there for ten years when the company closed.
    Duration up to a past moment.
  • The ground was wet. It had been raining.
    Past signs of an earlier activity.

The formula

had + past participle (When I arrived, the train had left.)

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • Had is weak in the middle of a sentence: /həd/ or /əd/, and often contracted to 'd /d/: “she'd gone” /ʃiːd ɡɒn/.
  • The 'd is very light: listen to the difference between “I left” and “I'd left”. The /d/ links to the next word: “I'd already” /aɪd ɔːlˈredi/.
  • hadn't /ˈhædnt/ is always stressed: “I HADN'T seen it.”
  • Say by the time as one block /baɪ ðə ˈtaɪm/, with the stress on time.

Common mistakes

Don’t saySayWhy
✗ When I arrived, the train left already.✓ When I arrived, the train had already left.The earlier action (the train leaving) takes the past perfect.
✗ I had visited Paris in 2015.✓ I visited Paris in 2015.A single dated fact is past simple; the past perfect needs another past reference point.
✗ She had went home.✓ She had gone home.After had, use the participle (gone), not the past simple.
✗ I didn't know him because I never met him before.✓ I didn't know him because I had never met him before.Experience before a past moment: had never + participle.
✗ By the time we arrived, they finished dinner.✓ By the time we arrived, they had finished dinner.By the time + the earlier action in the past perfect.
✗ He said he has lost his job.✓ He said he had lost his job.After a reporting verb in the past, go one step back: has lost → had lost.

In a conversation

Listening Studio

Lyon, Monday morning. Chloé tells her colleague Malik about her disappointing weekend in Geneva.

Malik

So, how was Geneva?
A simple past question.

Chloé

A disaster! When we got to the station, our train had already left.
The train left before they arrived.

Malik

Oh no! Hadn't you checked the time?
Negative question in the past perfect.

Chloé

We had, but the company had changed the timetable and nobody had told us.
Short answer “We had”, then two earlier actions.

Malik

So what did you do?
Back to the main story: past simple.

Chloé

We took the next one. By the time we arrived, the museum had closed.
By the time + past perfect.

Malik

What a shame. Had you been to Geneva before?
Experience before that weekend.

Chloé

No, never. But the lake was beautiful, so it wasn't all bad.
It wasn't all bad = there were good things too.

Exercises

Writing Workshop

From easy to harder. Answer without looking at the lesson, then check each exercise.

1. Which happened first?

Write the action that happened first.

  1. When I got home, my sister had cooked dinner.
  2. He had left before I called him.
  3. The shop had closed when we arrived.
  4. I realised I had forgotten my bag.
Show the answers
  1. My sister cooked dinner.
  2. He left.
  3. The shop closed.
  4. I forgot my bag.

2. Complete

Put the verb in brackets in the past perfect.

  1. When we arrived, the concert ___ (start).
  2. I was hungry because I ___ (not / eat) all day.
  3. ___ you ___ (meet) her before the party?
  4. She ___ (never / see) the sea before that holiday.
  5. By 9 p.m., they ___ (finish) the work.
Show the answers
  1. had started
  2. hadn't eaten
  3. Had … met
  4. had never seen
  5. had finished

3. Past simple or past perfect?

Put one verb in the past simple and the other in the past perfect.

  1. When I (get) to the bank, it (close).
  2. She (be) sad because her cat (disappear).
  3. We (not / recognise) him because he (grow) a beard.
  4. By the time the taxi (arrive), we (decide) to walk home.
Show the answers
  1. got … had closed
  2. was … had disappeared
  3. didn't recognise … had grown
  4. arrived … had decided

4. Correct the mistakes

Each sentence has one mistake. Write it correctly.

  1. When I arrived, the meeting already finished.
  2. She had wrote the letter before lunch.
  3. I had bought this car in 2019.
  4. He told me he has seen the film.
Show the answers
  1. When I arrived, the meeting had already finished.
  2. She had written the letter before lunch.
  3. I bought this car in 2019.
  4. He told me he had seen the film.

5. Join the sentences

Join the two sentences into one, using the word in brackets and the past perfect.

  1. The bus left. Then we arrived. (When … already)
  2. I ate poutine for the first time on that trip. (never … before)
  3. She said: “I've lost my keys.” (She told me…)
  4. It started raining two hours before we left. It was still raining. (had been)
Show the answers
  1. When we arrived, the bus had already left.
  2. I had never eaten poutine before that trip.
  3. She told me (that) she had lost her keys.
  4. It had been raining for two hours when we left.

6. Your turn

In 4–5 sentences, tell the story of a day when everything went wrong. Use the past perfect at least three times (already, never… before, by the time…).

  1. Example answer
Show the answers
  1. Last Monday was terrible. When I woke up, I realised my alarm hadn't rung. By the time I got to the station, my train had already left. When I finally arrived at work, the meeting had started. I had never felt so embarrassed before.
Key points
  • Form: had + past participle, the same for every subject; contraction 'd.
  • Use: the earlier of two past actions (the “past of the past”).
  • Signals: already, just, never… before, by the time, by + time, after, before.
  • No past perfect for a single dated fact or a story told in order: use the past simple.
  • Past perfect continuous (had been + -ing): duration up to a past moment.
