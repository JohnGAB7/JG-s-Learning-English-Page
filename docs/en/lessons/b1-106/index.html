Modals of deduction in the present (must, might, can’t)

We use must, might, may, could and can't to make deductions: to say how sure we are about something, based on evidence. “She must be tired” (I'm almost sure), “He can't be at home” (it's impossible). This is very common in conversation.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level B1

Prefer explanations in French? Version française de cette leçon →

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Express near certainty with must (positive) and can't or couldn't (negative).
  • Express possibility with might, may and could, and negative possibility with might not / may not.
  • Use the continuous form (must be working) for an action in progress.
  • Avoid the big trap: using mustn't for a negative deduction.

A scale of certainty

These modals sit on a scale from “almost sure it's true” to “almost sure it's not true”. They are followed by the base verb, without to, and they never take -s.

We make a deduction from evidence: what we see, hear or know. If we are completely sure, we don't use a modal: “He's at home” (I can see him).

CertaintyModalExampleMeaning
≈ 95 % yesmustShe must be at work.I'm almost sure she is.
≈ 50 %might / may / couldShe might be at work.Perhaps she is.
≈ 50 % nomight not / may notShe might not be at work.Perhaps she isn't.
≈ 95 % nocan't / couldn'tShe can't be at work.I'm almost sure she isn't; it's impossible.

Must: almost sure it's true

Must expresses a logical conclusion based on strong evidence. We use it a lot to react to what someone says: “You must be exhausted!” (after a long trip), “That must be difficult.”

Careful: must also expresses obligation (You must wear a helmet), studied in lesson 107. The context shows which meaning it has.

  • The lights are on. They must be at home.
    Evidence: the lights.
  • You've been travelling for 20 hours. You must be tired.
    A reaction.
  • She speaks four languages. She must be very good at languages.
    A logical conclusion.

Can't: almost sure it's not true

The opposite of must (deduction) is not mustn't but can't. “He can't be hungry, he's just eaten.” Couldn't is possible with the same meaning.

Mustn't only means prohibition (You mustn't park here). This is the most common mistake: “He mustn't be at home” does not express a deduction.

  • That can't be Ahmed. He's in Toronto this week.
    Impossible: he's somewhere else.
  • You can't be serious!
    I can't believe you mean it.
  • It can't be far now. We've been walking for an hour.
    Logical conclusion.

Might, may, could: perhaps

Might, may and could express possibility, without certainty. Might is the most common in speech; may is a little more formal; could is common for guesses.

In the negative, use might not or may not (perhaps not). Couldn't means impossibility (= can't): “He might not be at home” (perhaps he isn't) ≠ “He couldn't be at home” (it's impossible).

Instead of maybe + a normal verb, English often prefers a modal: “Maybe he's ill” = “He might be ill.” Both are correct, but don't use both together.

  • Where's Fatou? – I'm not sure. She might be in the library.
    Possible.
  • Take an umbrella. It may rain later.
    Possible (a little more formal).
  • The noise could be the neighbours.
    A guess.
  • He might not know about the meeting.
    Perhaps he doesn't know.

Continuous form and questions

For a deduction about an action in progress, use modal + be + -ing: “He isn't answering. He must be driving.” / “She might be sleeping.”

In questions, use could or might (not may or must): “Could she be lost?”, “Who could that be?” We also often ask with “Do you think…?”: “Do you think she's lost?”

  • They aren't answering. They must be having dinner.
    Action in progress.
  • Someone's at the door. Who could it be?
    A deduction question.

The formula

must / might / may / could / can't + base verb (or + be + -ing) — must = sure it's true, can't = sure it's not

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • In neutral deductions, must is weak /məst/, often without t before a consonant: “She must be tired” /ʃi məs bi ˈtaɪəd/.
  • can't is /kɑːnt/ in British English and /kænt/ in American English. The final t is often hard to hear: the long vowel and the stress show the negative.
  • might /maɪt/: the gh is silent. may /meɪ/.
  • To stress the conclusion, stress the modal: “You MUST be joking!”, “It CAN'T be true!”
  • couldn't /ˈkʊdnt/: the l is silent.

✗ He musts be rich.✓ He must be rich.No -s on a modal.

✗ He couldn't be at home, I'm not sure.✓ He might not be at home, I'm not sure.Negative possibility: might not; couldn't = impossible.

In a conversation

Listening Studio

A Saturday evening in Montreal. Marie and her husband Tchami are waiting for their daughter Inès, who was supposed to be home at 8 p.m.

Marie

It's nine o'clock. Inès should be home by now.
Should = expected.

Tchami

She must be with Léa. They always study together on Saturdays.
Must: strong evidence (a habit).

Marie

She can't be with Léa. Léa's family is in Quebec City this weekend.
Can't: impossible.

Tchami

Then she might be at the library. It closes at ten.
Might: possible.

Marie

I've called her three times. She isn't answering.
Present perfect: three times so far.

Tchami

Her phone might be on silent. Or she could be on the bus.
Two possibilities.

Marie

Listen! Someone's at the door. Who could it be?
Could in a question.

Tchami

That must be her. Yes, it's Inès! You must be hungry, darling.
Must for a reaction.

Exercises

Writing Workshop

From easy to harder. Answer without looking at the lesson, then check each exercise.

1. How certain?

Say if the sentence means: almost sure it's true (S), possible (P) or almost sure it's not true (N).

  1. He can't be the thief.
  2. They might be in the garden.
  3. You must be Sophie's brother.
  4. It could be a mistake.
  5. She can't be 50! She looks 30.
Show the answers
  1. N
  2. P
  3. S
  4. P
  5. N

2. Must or can't?

Complete with must or can't.

  1. He's been working all night. He ___ be exhausted.
  2. That ___ be the right answer. It doesn't make sense.
  3. She's wearing a wedding ring. She ___ be married.
  4. You ___ be hungry. You've just had a big lunch.
Show the answers
  1. must
  2. can't
  3. must
  4. can't

3. Rewrite with a modal

Rewrite the sentence with must, might or can't.

  1. I'm sure he's at work.
  2. Perhaps she's at the gym.
  3. I'm sure this isn't your bag.
  4. Perhaps they don't know the address.
  5. I'm sure she's sleeping now.
Show the answers
  1. He must be at work.
  2. She might (may / could) be at the gym.
  3. This can't be your bag.
  4. They might not (may not) know the address.
  5. She must be sleeping now.

4. Correct the mistakes

Each sentence has one mistake. Write it correctly.

  1. He mustn't be Canadian: he doesn't speak English or French.
  2. She might to be at home.
  3. They must be watch TV: I can hear it.
  4. Maybe he might come later.
Show the answers
  1. He can't be Canadian: he doesn't speak English or French.
  2. She might be at home.
  3. They must be watching TV: I can hear it.
  4. He might come later. / Maybe he'll come later.

5. React with a modal

Write one sentence with a modal of deduction for each situation.

  1. Your friend has just arrived after a 15-hour trip.
  2. Someone says your 80-year-old grandmother has run a marathon. You don't believe it.
  3. Your colleague isn't at work. Perhaps she's ill.
  4. Your neighbours don't answer the door at 8 p.m. You think they're eating.
Show the answers
  1. You must be tired after that trip.
  2. That can't be true!
  3. She might (may / could) be ill.
  4. They must be having dinner.

6. Your turn

Your colleague didn't come to work this morning and isn't answering the phone. Write 4 guesses with must, might, could and can't, and give the evidence each time.

  1. Example answer
Show the answers
  1. He can't be on holiday, because his holiday starts next month. He might be ill: he was coughing yesterday. He could be stuck in traffic, because there was an accident on the motorway. His phone must be switched off, because it goes straight to voicemail.
Key points
  • must = almost sure it's true; can't = almost sure it's not true (never mustn't for deduction).
  • might / may / could = possible; might not / may not = perhaps not.
  • Modal + base verb, no to, no -s.
  • Action in progress: must / might / can't + be + -ing.
  • Deduction questions: Could / Might…? or Do you think…?
