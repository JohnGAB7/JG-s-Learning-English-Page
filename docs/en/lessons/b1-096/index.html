The second conditional

The second conditional (If I had more time, I would travel) talks about imaginary, unlikely or untrue present situations and their results. We also use it to give advice and to speak politely.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level B1

Prefer explanations in French? Version française de cette leçon →

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Build the second conditional with if + past simple and would + base verb.
  • Tell the difference between a real, likely situation (first conditional) and an imaginary or unlikely one (second conditional).
  • Use If I were you… to give advice, and were with every subject in careful English.
  • Vary the result with could and might, and ask hypothetical questions (What would you do if…?).

The structure

Condition: if + past simple (or past continuous). Result: would + base verb. Contraction: 'd (I'd, she'd); negative: wouldn't. The comma rule is the same as in the first conditional.

The past tense here doesn't talk about the past: it shows distance from reality. “If I had a car” = I don't have a car (now).

Condition (if + past)Result (would + base verb)Reality
If I had more money,I'd buy a house.I don't have enough money.
If she lived closer,we would see her more often.She lives far away.
If they spoke English,they'd understand the film.They don't speak English.
If it wasn't raining,we'd go for a walk.It's raining now.
Like will, would never goes in the if-clause: “If I would have money” is wrong.

Real or imaginary? First or second conditional

The choice depends on how the speaker sees the situation. First conditional: possible, realistic. Second conditional: imaginary, unlikely or impossible.

“If I win the match, I'll be happy” (I'm a good player; it's possible). “If I won the lottery, I'd stop working” (a dream; very unlikely).

  • If I get the job, I'll move to Lille.
    Possible: I've had the interview.
  • If I got a job in Japan, I'd learn Japanese.
    A distant idea.
  • If I were taller, I'd play basketball.
    Impossible to change: contrary to reality.
  • What would you do if you saw a lion in the street?
    An imaginary situation.

“If I were…” and advice

With be, we can use were with every subject: If I were, if he were, if it were. This is the recommended form in writing and in exams. Was (if I was, if she was) is common in speech but less formal.

“If I were you, I'd…” is the classic way to give advice. In this fixed phrase, always use were.

  • If I were you, I'd apologise.
    Advice.
  • If it were cheaper, more people would buy it.
    Were with it: careful style.
  • I wouldn't sign that contract if I were you.
    Negative advice.

Could, might and questions

In the result, could = would be able to, might = would perhaps. In the condition, could = was able to: “If I could drive, I'd help you move.”

Hypothetical questions are very useful in conversation and in job interviews: What would you do if…? Where would you live if…? Short answer: Yes, I would. / No, I wouldn't.

FormExampleMeaning
wouldIf I had time, I would help you.certain result in the imaginary situation
could (result)If I had a car, I could drive you home.I would be able to
might (result)If you asked him nicely, he might say yes.perhaps he would
could (condition)If I could speak Dutch, I'd apply for that job in Ghent.if I was able to

Politeness and delicate situations

The second conditional makes a request or suggestion softer: “Would it be OK if I left early today?”, “It would be great if you could send me the file.” The grammatical distance creates polite distance.

It also helps to say no kindly (“I'd love to come, but…”) and to talk about principles (“I would never lie to a friend.”).

  • Would you mind if I opened the window?
    Polite request (past simple after if).
  • It would be helpful if you arrived ten minutes early.
    An instruction, said politely.

The formula

If + past simple, would / could / might + base verb (If I had time, I'd help you. · If I were you, I'd…)

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • 'd is almost silent: “I'd go” /aɪd ɡəʊ/. Listen for the small /d/ that separates “I go” from “I'd go”. Before a vowel it links: “I'd ask” /aɪˈdɑːsk/.
  • would is weak in questions: “What would you do?” /ˈwɒt wəd jə ˈduː/; often “wouldja” /ˈwʊdʒə/ in fast speech.
  • wouldn't /ˈwʊdnt/: the l is silent (as in could and should).
  • were is weak /wə/ in “If I were you”: /ɪf aɪ wə ˈjuː/, with the stress on you.
  • The voice rises on the condition and falls on the result, as in the first conditional.

Common mistakes

Don’t saySayWhy
✗ If I would have more time, I would travel.✓ If I had more time, I would travel.No would in the if-clause: use the past simple.
✗ If I had money, I will buy a car.✓ If I had money, I would buy a car.Past simple in the condition → would in the result (not will).
✗ If I am you, I would accept.✓ If I were you, I would accept.Fixed phrase: If I were you.
✗ If I would be rich, I would help my family.✓ If I were rich, I would help my family.Be in the past (were) after if.
✗ What you would do if you lost your job?✓ What would you do if you lost your job?Question: would before the subject.
✗ If I could, I would to help you.✓ If I could, I would help you.No to after would.

In a conversation

Listening Studio

Abidjan, at a café terrace. Awa and her friend Pierre, visiting from Quebec City, imagine a different life.

Pierre

What would you do if you didn't have to work?
A hypothetical question.

Awa

I'd open a small restaurant by the sea. And you?
I'd = I would.

Pierre

If I had the money, I'd travel around Africa for a year.
If + past, would + verb.

Awa

You could start with Ghana. It isn't far from here.
Could = a possibility.

Pierre

True. But if I left my job, my boss would be furious!
Imaginary result.

Awa

If I were you, I'd ask for unpaid leave.
Advice with If I were you.

Pierre

That's a good idea. He might accept if I asked him early.
Might = perhaps he would.

Awa

And if you came back next year, you could eat at my restaurant!
Second conditional for a friendly idea.

Exercises

Writing Workshop

From easy to harder. Answer without looking at the lesson, then check each exercise.

1. Real or imaginary?

Choose the form that matches the meaning given.

  1. (Possible) If it (rains / rained) tomorrow, we'll cancel the picnic.
  2. (Imaginary) If I (am / were) a bird, I'd fly to Cameroon.
  3. (Imaginary) If she (has / had) wings, she would fly.
  4. (Possible) If you (call / called) me tonight, I'll explain.
Show the answers
  1. rains
  2. were
  3. had
  4. call

2. Put the verbs in the right form

Complete with the correct forms (second conditional).

  1. If I ___ (know) her number, I ___ (call) her.
  2. We ___ (not / live) here if we ___ (have) a choice.
  3. If he ___ (be) more patient, he ___ (be) a good teacher.
  4. What ___ you ___ (do) if you ___ (lose) your passport abroad?
  5. If they ___ (can) speak French, they ___ (move) to Quebec.
Show the answers
  1. knew … would call ('d call)
  2. wouldn't live … had
  3. were (was) … would be
  4. would … do … lost
  5. could … would move

3. Transform

Rewrite the sentence in the second conditional to imagine the opposite.

  1. I don't have a car, so I can't drive you home.
  2. She's very busy, so she doesn't call her parents.
  3. We don't speak Dutch, so we don't understand the signs.
  4. It's raining, so we aren't going to the beach.
Show the answers
  1. If I had a car, I could drive you home.
  2. If she weren't (wasn't) so busy, she would call her parents.
  3. If we spoke Dutch, we would understand the signs.
  4. If it weren't (wasn't) raining, we would go to the beach.

4. Correct the mistakes

Each sentence has one mistake. Write it correctly.

  1. If I would win the lottery, I would buy a house.
  2. If I am you, I would talk to her.
  3. If she had time, she will help us.
  4. What you would say if he asked you?
Show the answers
  1. If I won the lottery, I would buy a house.
  2. If I were you, I would talk to her.
  3. If she had time, she would help us.
  4. What would you say if he asked you?

5. What would you say?

Write a second conditional sentence for each situation.

  1. You don't have much time, but you'd love to learn the piano.
  2. Give a friend advice: don't say anything.
  3. Ask a friend to imagine living in Brussels.
  4. Ask politely if you can leave earlier.
Show the answers
  1. If I had more time, I would learn the piano.
  2. If I were you, I wouldn't say anything.
  3. What would you do if you lived in Brussels?
  4. Would you mind if I left earlier?

6. Your turn

Answer in 4 sentences: “What would you do if you could live anywhere in the world?” Use would, could and might.

  1. Example answer
Show the answers
  1. If I could live anywhere, I'd choose Vancouver. I would live near the mountains and go hiking every weekend. I could also work from home for a company in Europe. My family might visit me every summer.
Key points
  • If + past simple, would + base verb: an imaginary or unlikely situation (present or future).
  • The past tense shows distance from reality, not past time.
  • Never would in the if-clause.
  • If I were you, I'd… to give advice; were is possible with every subject.
  • Could = would be able to; might = would perhaps.
