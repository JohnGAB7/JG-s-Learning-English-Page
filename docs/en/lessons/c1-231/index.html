Fronting

Fronting means putting at the start of a sentence an element that normally comes after the verb: “This I can't accept”, “Tired as she was, she kept going”, “Gone are the days when…”. It is used to link ideas, create contrast or express concession elegantly.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level C1

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Front an object or an adjective to create contrast (This I know; Lucky we were).
  • Build concessive clauses with fronting: Tired as I was…, Much as I like…, Try as she might…, Strange though it may seem…
  • Front adverbials and participles for cohesion and style (Of the 300 respondents…; Gone are the days…).
  • Distinguish fronting without inversion (This I know) from inversion after a negative element (Never have I…).

The principle: moving to link or contrast

Normal English word order is subject + verb + complement. Fronting moves an element to the front for two main reasons: 1) to link the sentence to what came before (known information first), 2) to create a contrast with another idea.

In most cases, subject and verb stay in normal order after the fronted element: “This I can't accept” (not “This can't I accept”). Inversion only appears with certain elements: negatives (lesson 229), and participles or places followed by a noun subject (“Gone are the days”).

  • Most of the report is fine. This last paragraph, however, I can't accept.
    Fronted object: contrast with the rest of the report.
  • She promised to finish by Friday, and finish she did.
    Fronted verb: emphatic confirmation (and she really did).

Fronting an object or an adjective

Fronting the object is most common with this, that, these, those, or with a noun in contrast: “Some things I'll forgive; lies I won't.” It is mainly spoken or literary, and very expressive.

A complement adjective can also come first, with an exclamatory or commenting effect: “Lucky we were to escape.” (The concessive form, below, is more frequent.)

Normal orderFrontingEffect
I know this.This I know.insistence on “this”
I won't forgive lies.Lies I won't forgive.contrast with other faults
I can do the maths, but not the writing.The maths I can do; the writing, not so much.contrast between two things
We were lucky to escape.Lucky we were to escape.exclamation, literary style
  • The first exam I passed easily. The second was a nightmare.
    Fronted object to contrast two exams.

Concessive clauses: Tired as I was…

This is the most useful pattern at C1. Structure: adjective / adverb / noun + as (or though) + subject + verb, then the main clause. The meaning is “although”: “Tired as I was, I kept working” = Although I was very tired, I kept working.

Fixed variants: “Much as I like / admire / respect…” (= although I really like…); “Try as I might…” (= however hard I tried); “Strange / Hard as it may seem…”; “Child though he was…” (noun without article, very formal).

Note: in British English, don't add “as” at the start (“As tired as I was” is common in American English but can be read as a comparison). Never add “but” in the main clause.

With althoughWith fronting
Although I was tired, I finished the report.Tired as I was, I finished the report.
Although I like him a lot, I don't trust him.Much as I like him, I don't trust him.
Although she tried hard, she couldn't open it.Try as she might, she couldn't open it.
Although it may seem strange, it works.Strange as it may seem, it works.
Although he was very rich, he lived simply.Rich though he was, he lived simply.
  • Hard as it may be to believe, the village had no electricity until 2015.
    = Although it may be hard to believe…
  • Try as I might, I couldn't remember her name.
    = However hard I tried…; “might” in a past context.

Fronting adverbials and participles

Putting a time, place or quantity phrase first is very common in writing to organise information: “In 2020, …”, “Of the 300 respondents, 40 % said…”, “Among the most affected were…”. It is a cohesion tool: you start from what is known and move to what is new.

With a participle or adjective first, followed by “be”, you invert when the subject is a noun: “Gone are the days when…”, “Attached is a copy of the invoice”, “Included in the price are breakfast and Wi-Fi”. These forms are common in formal emails and journalism.

  • Of the 1,200 candidates, only 85 were selected.
    Quantity first: start from the known group.
  • Gone are the days when a degree guaranteed a job.
    = That time is over; participle + be + subject.
  • Attached is the signed contract.
    Formal email: = I have attached…
  • Among the guests were two former ministers.
    Phrase first + be + long subject.

Fronting or inversion? Summary

General rule: an ordinary element first → normal order (This I know). A negative or restrictive element first → inversion with an auxiliary (Never have I…). A place, participle or adjective + be / a verb of position, with a noun subject → inversion of the verb (Gone are the days; On the hill stood a church).

Fronting is expressive: in academic writing, keep to fronted adverbials (In 2020…, Of these…) and concessives; in speech, “This I know” or “That I doubt” are natural.

Element firstOrderExample
Object, adjective, verbsubject + verbThat I doubt.
Negative / restrictiveauxiliary + subject + verbRarely have I seen that.
Concessive (adj. + as)subject + verbCold as it was, we swam.
Participle / place + be (noun subject)verb + subjectEnclosed is my CV.

The formula

Known or contrasted element first + subject + verb (This I know) · Adj. / adv. + as / though + subject + verb, main clause (Tired as I was, …) · Participle + be + noun subject (Gone are the days…)

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • The fronted element is strongly stressed and forms its own intonation group: “THIS | I can't accept.”
  • In concessives, use a rise or fall-rise on the first part, then a fall on the main clause: “Tired as I WAS, | I kept GOing.”
  • “as” is reduced to /əz/: “Much as I like him” /ˈmʌtʃ əz aɪ ˈlaɪk ɪm/.
  • “though” is /ðəʊ/: “Rich though he was”; the “gh” is silent.
  • “Gone are the days”: stress “gone” and “days”, reduce “are the” to /ə ðə/.

In a conversation

Listening Studio

In Montreal, a team leader explains to a colleague why he rejects part of a draft contract.

Rachel

So, what do you think of the supplier's draft?
An open question.

Victor

Most of it is fine. The penalty clause, though, I simply can't accept.
Fronted object for contrast.

Rachel

Really? Strange as it may seem, their lawyers say it's standard.
Concessive fronting.

Victor

Standard it may be, but it's not fair to us.
Fronted adjective: he concedes the point, then objects.

Rachel

Much as I agree, we need this contract. Gone are the days when we could pick and choose.
“Much as” + “Gone are the days”.

Victor

True. Of the five suppliers we contacted, they're the only one with stock.
Fronted quantity phrase.

Rachel

Then let's propose a cap on the penalties. That they might accept.
Fronted object: “that” refers to the cap.

Victor

Good idea. I'll draft the email now: “Attached is our counter-proposal regarding clause 7.”
Participle + be + subject in a formal email.

Exercises

Writing Workshop

From easy to harder. Answer without looking at the lesson, then check each exercise.

1. Fronting or inversion?

Say whether the sentence shows fronting without inversion or inversion.

  1. That I doubt very much.
  2. Never have I been so embarrassed.
  3. Cold as it was, we went swimming.
  4. Gone are the days of paper tickets.
Show the answers
  1. fronting without inversion
  2. inversion
  3. fronting without inversion
  4. inversion (participle + be + subject)

2. Concessives

Rewrite with the “… as / though …” structure.

  1. Although she was exhausted, she finished the race.
  2. Although I like my job a lot, I need a change.
  3. Although he tried hard, he couldn't convince them.
  4. Although it may sound strange, I enjoy exams.
  5. Although the flat was small, it was very bright.
Show the answers
  1. Exhausted as she was, she finished the race.
  2. Much as I like my job, I need a change.
  3. Try as he might, he couldn't convince them.
  4. Strange as it may sound, I enjoy exams.
  5. Small as / though the flat was, it was very bright.

3. Put it first

Rewrite with the words in brackets at the front.

  1. I can't accept [this decision].
  2. Only 85 [of the 1,200 candidates] were selected.
  3. Breakfast and parking are [included in the price].
  4. The days when we wrote letters [are gone].
Show the answers
  1. This decision I can't accept.
  2. Of the 1,200 candidates, only 85 were selected.
  3. Included in the price are breakfast and parking.
  4. Gone are the days when we wrote letters.

4. Correct the mistake

Correct each sentence.

  1. Tired as I was, but I went out.
  2. This can't I believe.
  3. Try as I could, the door wouldn't open.
Show the answers
  1. Tired as I was, I went out.
  2. This I can't believe.
  3. Try as I might, the door wouldn't open.

5. Rewrite with fronting

Rewrite each sentence with a fronting structure.

  1. Although we were very tired, we carried on.
  2. The time when petrol cost a dollar is over.
  3. However hard I tried, I couldn't understand.
Show the answers
  1. Tired as we were, we carried on / kept going.
  2. Gone are the days when petrol (AmE: gas) cost a dollar.
  3. Try as I might, I couldn't understand.

6. Your turn

Write 3–4 sentences about a change in your town or job. Use at least two different fronting structures.

  1. Example answer
Show the answers
  1. Gone are the days when everyone in Yaoundé paid cash at the market. Of the traders I know, almost all now accept mobile money. Convenient as it is, the system still worries older customers. That, I think, is the real challenge for the next few years.
Key points
  • Fronting = moving an element to the front to link (known first) or contrast; usually no inversion (This I know).
  • Elegant concessives: Tired as I was…, Much as I like…, Try as I might…, Strange as it may seem… — with no although and no but.
  • Fronted adverbials for cohesion (Of the 300 respondents…); participle + be + noun subject with inversion (Gone are the days…, Attached is…).
  • Negative element first → auxiliary inversion; any other element → normal order.
