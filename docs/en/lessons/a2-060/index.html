A2 · Elementary · lesson 60

Something, anything, nothing, someone…

Something, nothing, someone, nobody, somewhere, nowhere, everyone… English builds these words with some-, any-, no- and every-, with the same rules as some and any (lesson 31). You need them in everyday conversation.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level A2

Prefer explanations in French? Version française de cette leçon →

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Build and understand the words with some-, any-, no-, every- (+ thing, one / body, where).
  • Choose between some- (positive, offers), any- (negatives, questions) and no- (negative meaning with a positive verb).
  • Avoid double negatives: I don't know anything / I know nothing, never “I don't know nothing”.
  • Put an adjective or “else” after these words (something nice, someone else) and use a singular verb.

The table of words

Combine some-, any-, no-, every- with -thing (a thing), -one or -body (a person) and -where (a place). “-one” and “-body” mean exactly the same; “-body” is a little more informal.

“No one” is two words (sometimes “no-one” in British English). The others are one word: someone, anybody, nothing, everywhere.

-thing (thing)-one / -body (person)-where (place)
some-somethingsomeone / somebodysomewhere
any-anythinganyone / anybodyanywhere
no-nothingno one / nobodynowhere
every-everythingeveryone / everybodyeverywhere

Some- or any-?

Use “some-” in positive sentences: There's someone at the door. I want to eat something. Also use it in questions when you offer or ask for something and expect “yes”: Would you like something to drink? Can someone help me?

Use “any-” in negative sentences (with not / n't) and in most questions: I can't see anything. Is anyone there? Did you go anywhere at the weekend?

  • I've got something for you.
    Positive: some-.
  • Would you like something to eat?
    An offer: some-.
  • I don't know anyone in this city.
    Negative: any-.
  • Is there anything in the fridge?
    Question: any-.
  • Let's go somewhere quiet.
    Positive: some-.

No- and double negatives

“Nothing, no one, nobody, nowhere” already have a negative meaning. So the verb stays POSITIVE: I know nothing. Nobody called. Never say “I don't know nothing”: in standard English, two negatives are wrong.

So there are two ways to say the same thing: I can't see anything (more common in speaking) / I can see nothing (stronger, more written). As the subject of a sentence, only no- is possible: Nobody knows (never “Anybody doesn't know”).

“Nothing / nobody” can also be short answers: What's in the box? — Nothing. Who's there? — No one.

MeaningWith any- + notWith no-
zero things seenI can't see anything.I can see nothing.
zero people hereThere isn't anyone.There's no one. / There's nobody.
zero places to goWe aren't going anywhere.We're going nowhere.
zero people know (subject)—Nobody knows. / No one knows.

Any- in positive sentences: “it doesn't matter which”

In a positive sentence, “any-” means “it doesn't matter which thing / person / place”: You can sit anywhere. Anyone can learn English.

“Every-” means “all”: everything, everyone, everywhere. Be careful: everyone / everybody takes a SINGULAR verb, even if the meaning is plural: Everyone is here.

  • Call me anytime.
    At any time (anytime: mostly US; UK: any time).
  • I'll eat anything. I'm so hungry!
    It doesn't matter what.
  • Everybody loves this song.
    Singular verb: loves.
  • There are flowers everywhere.
    In all places.

Adjectives, else, and the pronouns after

The adjective goes AFTER these words, not before: something interesting, nothing new, somewhere warm. No “of”: don't say “something of interesting”.

“Else” means “other” or “more”, and also goes after: someone else, anything else?, everywhere else.

You can add “to + verb”: something to eat, nothing to do, somewhere to sleep.

To refer back to “someone, anyone, everyone”, people often use they / their: Someone left their umbrella. Is everyone ready? Have they got their tickets?

  • Did you do anything special at the weekend?
    Adjective after anything.
  • Anything else? — No, thank you.
    In a shop.
  • There's nothing to do in this village.
    nothing + to + verb.
  • Someone forgot their phone.
    someone → their.

The formula

some- (positive, offers) · any- (negatives, questions; “it doesn't matter which” in positives) · no- + positive verb · every- + singular verb · something + adjective / else / to + verb

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • “some-” sounds like /ˈsʌm/ (like “sum”): something /ˈsʌmθɪŋ/, someone /ˈsʌmwʌn/.
  • “any-” sounds like /ˈeni/: anything /ˈeniθɪŋ/.
  • “nothing” /ˈnʌθɪŋ/: the o sounds like /ʌ/ as in “mother”, not like “no”. But “nobody” /ˈnəʊbədi/ starts like “no”.
  • “everyone” /ˈevriwʌn/: three syllables, stress on EV-.
  • The /θ/ in -thing: tongue between the teeth. Don't say “somesing” or “someting”.

In a conversation

Listening Studio

Two colleagues arrive for a meeting at a company's offices in Lausanne.

Inès

Hello? Is anyone here? The room is empty.
Question: anyone.

Marco

No, nobody's here. Maybe the meeting is somewhere else.
nobody + positive verb; somewhere else.

Inès

Did someone send an email about it?
someone: she expects yes.

Marco

Let me check… No, there's nothing in my inbox.
nothing.

Inès

Look, there's something on the table. It's a note.
Positive: something.

Marco

It says: “Everyone is in room 4B. Coffee for everybody!”
Everyone + is.

Inès

Great! I didn't have anything for breakfast.
Negative: anything.

Marco

Me neither. Let's go. I hope there's something to eat too!
something + to + verb.

Exercises

Writing Workshop

From easy to harder. Answer without looking at the lesson, then check each exercise.

1. Some- or any-?

Complete with something, anything, someone or anyone.

  1. There's ___ at the door.
  2. I didn't buy ___ at the market.
  3. Would you like ___ to drink?
  4. Does ___ speak Japanese here?
  5. I want to tell you ___ important.
  6. I can't hear ___. It's very quiet.
Show the answers
  1. someone
  2. anything
  3. something
  4. anyone
  5. something
  6. anything / anyone

2. With no-

Rewrite the sentence with nothing, nobody or nowhere (same meaning).

  1. I don't have anything to say.
  2. There isn't anyone in the office.
  3. We didn't go anywhere last summer.
  4. She doesn't know anything about it.
Show the answers
  1. I have nothing to say. / I've got nothing to say.
  2. There's nobody in the office. / There's no one in the office.
  3. We went nowhere last summer.
  4. She knows nothing about it.

3. Every-, else, adjective

Complete with the right word.

  1. ___ is ready. Let's start! (all the people)
  2. Do you want anything ___? (more, other)
  3. I want to watch something ___. (it makes you laugh)
  4. I looked ___ for my glasses. (in all places)
Show the answers
  1. Everyone / Everybody
  2. else
  3. funny
  4. everywhere

4. Correct the mistake

Each sentence has one mistake. Correct it.

  1. I didn't see nobody.
  2. Everybody know the answer.
  3. Is there something of interesting on TV?
  4. Anyone doesn't like him.
  5. Can you ask other someone?
Show the answers
  1. I didn't see anybody. / I saw nobody.
  2. Everybody knows the answer.
  3. Is there anything interesting on TV?
  4. Nobody likes him. / No one likes him.
  5. Can you ask someone else?

5. Write the sentence

Write one sentence for each idea, with a word from the lesson.

  1. No person is perfect.
  2. It doesn't matter which seat you take.
  3. The fridge is empty.
  4. You want to travel to a sunny place (you don't know which one).
Show the answers
  1. Nobody is perfect. / No one is perfect.
  2. You can sit anywhere.
  3. There's nothing to eat. / There isn't anything to eat.
  4. I'd like to go somewhere sunny.

6. Your turn

Write five sentences about your weekend or your week with at least five different words from the lesson (one with no- and one with else or an adjective).

  1. Example answer
Show the answers
  1. On Saturday I didn't go anywhere. I stayed at home and did nothing special. In the evening, someone from my family called me. On Sunday we went somewhere new: a small restaurant by the river. Everyone loved it.
Key points
  • some- (positive, offers), any- (negatives, questions), no- (positive verb), every- (all).
  • No double negatives: I don't know anything / I know nothing.
  • Negative subject: Nobody knows, Nothing happened.
  • Adjective, else or to + verb after: something new, someone else, nothing to do.
  • everyone / everybody + singular verb; refer back with they / their.
