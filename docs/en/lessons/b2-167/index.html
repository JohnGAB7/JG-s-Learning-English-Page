State and action verbs

Why do we say “I know” and never “I'm knowing”, but “I'm thinking about it” next to “I think so”? Some verbs describe a state (an opinion, knowledge, possession, feelings) and normally do not take the continuous form; others change their meaning with the form. This is a constant source of mistakes.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level B2

  • Recognise the main groups of state verbs (thoughts, feelings, possession, senses, description).
  • Distinguish the two meanings of think, have, see, taste, smell, look, weigh, be…
  • Use “can see / can hear” and sense verbs followed by an adjective (It smells good).
  • Understand modern uses (“I'm loving it”, “I'm hoping…”) and the rule in other tenses.

State or action?

An action (dynamic) verb describes something that happens, that starts and finishes: run, eat, write, cook. It can take the continuous form: “I'm cooking.”

A state (stative) verb describes a situation, a thought, a feeling or possession: there is no activity in progress. We normally use the simple form, even for the present moment: “I know the answer”, “She owns two shops”, “This book belongs to me”.

Ask yourself: is something happening, or is something simply true?

GroupCommon state verbsExample
Thoughts, opinionsknow, believe, understand, remember, forget, realise, suppose, mean, think (= have an opinion)I don't understand this word.
Feelings, wisheslike, love, hate, prefer, want, need, wish, mindI prefer tea to coffee.

Sensessee, hear, smell, taste, feel (involuntary)I hear music.
Description, measurementbe, seem, appear, look (= seem), consist, contain, cost, weigh, depend, matter, deserveIt depends on the weather.

Verbs with two meanings

Several verbs are state verbs with one meaning and action verbs with another. The meaning decides the form, not the verb itself.

The most common case is “think”: “I think it's a good idea” (opinion, state); “I'm thinking about changing jobs” (considering, a mental activity).

“Have” meaning possess or have a characteristic stays simple; in action expressions (have breakfast, have a shower, have a party, have fun, have a baby), it can be continuous.

VerbState meaning (simple)Action meaning (continuous possible)
thinkI think you're right. (opinion)I'm thinking about your offer. (considering)
haveShe has two brothers. (possess)She's having a shower. (taking)
seeI see what you mean. (understand)I'm seeing the doctor at 3. (meeting) / She's seeing someone. (dating)
tasteThe soup tastes salty. (has a flavour)The chef is tasting the soup. (trying it)
smellThe kitchen smells of garlic. (has a smell)Why are you smelling the milk? (sniffing)
lookYou look tired. (seem)I'm looking at the photos. (watching)
weighThe baby weighs 4 kg. (measurement)The nurse is weighing the baby. (action)
appearHe appears to be lost. (seem)She's appearing in a new film. (acting)

The senses: “can see”, “It smells good”

For an involuntary perception at the present moment, British English very often uses “can”: “I can see the sea from my window”, “Can you hear that noise?”. “I'm seeing the sea” is wrong.

Look, sound, smell, taste and feel can be followed by an adjective (not an adverb), like “be”: “This cake smells delicious”, “Your idea sounds great”, “You look tired”. Say “it smells good”, not “well”.

You can also use “like” + noun: “It looks like rain”, “It sounds like a good plan”.

  • I can hear someone in the garden.
    Perception now.
  • This fish smells strange. Don't eat it.
    smell + adjective.
  • Your plan sounds interesting.
    sound + adjective.
  • It looks like it's going to rain.
    look like + clause (informal).

“Be”, “feel” and physical sensations

“Be” is normally a state verb, but “be being + adjective” describes temporary, often unusual behaviour: “He's a kind person” (character); “You're being very rude today” (your behaviour now). Common adjectives: silly, rude, difficult, careful, kind, honest, lazy.

“Feel” for a sensation or physical state accepts both forms: “I feel tired / I'm feeling tired”. The -ing form stresses the moment. Meaning “have an opinion”, it stays simple: “I feel (that) we should wait.”

The same goes for “hurt” and “ache”: “My back hurts / My back is hurting”.

  • You're being silly. Calm down.
    Behaviour at the moment.
  • How are you feeling today?
    Common question from a doctor or friend.
  • I feel that we need more time.
    Opinion: simple.
  • My knee is hurting again.
    Physical sensation: both forms.

Modern uses and other tenses

In informal speech and advertising, you hear “I'm loving it”, “I'm liking this new job”: the -ing form shows enthusiasm or a current impression. It is accepted in speech, but avoid it in careful writing or exams.

“I'm hoping”, “I'm wondering” and “I was wondering” are very common polite phrases: “I was wondering if you could help me.” They soften a request.

The rule applies in all tenses: no continuous form with a state verb in the present perfect, the past or the future. “I've known her for years” (not “I've been knowing”), “I didn't understand” (not “I wasn't understanding”), “I'll need help” (not “I'll be needing”, except in some very polite uses).

  • I was wondering if you could send me the file.
    Polite request.
  • I'm hoping to finish by Friday.
    A careful way to express hope.
  • I've always believed in hard work.
    State verb: present perfect simple.

The formula

State verb → simple form (I know, it belongs, it tastes good) · Action meaning → continuous possible (I'm thinking about it, she's having lunch)

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • “tastes” /teɪsts/ and “costs” /kɒsts/: pronounce the whole final consonant group, without adding a vowel.
  • “weigh” /weɪ/: the “gh” is silent; “weight” /weɪt/.
  • Contrastive stress: “You're BEING rude” highlights the temporary behaviour.
  • “I can see”: “can” is weak, /kən/; in the negative “can't” /kɑːnt/ is stressed.
  • “I was wondering”: a soft falling intonation, polite tone: /aɪ wəz ˈwʌndərɪŋ/.

In a conversation

Listening Studio

In Abidjan, during the filming of a cooking show. Chef Aya is preparing grilled fish with her assistant, Kévin.

Aya

Kévin, what are you doing? Are you tasting my sauce already?
taste: action (trying it).

Kévin

Sorry, chef! But it smells amazing. I can smell the ginger from here.
smell: state + adjective; can smell.

Aya

And how does it taste?
taste: state (flavour).

Kévin

It tastes perfect. I think it needs a little more salt, though.
think: opinion; need: state.

Aya

You're being very honest today! OK, I'm thinking about it…
be being: behaviour; think: considering.

Kévin

The fish weighs almost two kilos. Do you want me to cut it?
weigh: measurement; want: state.

Aya

Yes, please. And the attiéké? Does it contain enough oil?
contain: state.

Kévin

It looks good to me. Everyone in the studio is having a great time!
look: seem; have a great time: action.

Exercises

Writing Workshop

From easy to harder. Answer without looking at the lesson, then check each exercise.

1. Choose

Choose the correct form.

  1. I (know / am knowing) the answer.
  2. Shh! I (think / am thinking).
  3. This coffee (tastes / is tasting) bitter.
  4. We (have / are having) dinner at the moment.
Show the answers
  1. know
  2. am thinking
  3. tastes
  4. are having

2. Complete

Put the verb into the present simple or present continuous.

  1. What ___ you ___ (think) of my new haircut?
  2. She ___ (see) a lawyer this afternoon about the contract.
  3. This box ___ (contain) old photos.
  4. Why ___ you ___ (be) so rude today? You're not usually like this.
  5. I ___ (not / understand) this sentence. Can you explain it?
  6. The chef ___ (taste) the soup to check the salt.
Show the answers
  1. do you think
  2. is seeing
  3. contains
  4. are you being
  5. don't understand
  6. is tasting

3. Explain the difference

Explain the difference in meaning between the two sentences.

  1. I think it's a good idea. / I'm thinking about changing jobs.
  2. She has a car. / She's having a shower.
  3. I see what you mean. / I'm seeing my doctor tomorrow.
  4. He's a difficult person. / He's being difficult.
  5. The baby weighs 4 kilos. / The nurse is weighing the baby.
Show the answers
  1. Opinion (state) / considering now (action).
  2. Possession / action (taking a shower).
  3. Understand / have an appointment.
  4. Permanent character / temporary behaviour now.
  5. Measurement (state) / the action of weighing.

4. Correct the mistakes

Each sentence has one mistake. Write it correctly.

  1. I'm not understanding the question.
  2. This bag is belonging to my sister.
  3. This cake smells deliciously.
  4. I'm seeing the sea from my window.
Show the answers
  1. I don't understand the question.
  2. This bag belongs to my sister.
  3. This cake smells delicious.
  4. I can see the sea from my window.

5. Sense verbs + adjective

Make a sentence with the words given (sense verb + adjective).

  1. your plan / sound / interesting
  2. this milk / smell / bad
  3. you / look / tired / today
  4. this soup / taste / too salty
Show the answers
  1. Your plan sounds interesting.
  2. This milk smells bad.
  3. You look tired today. (You're looking tired is also possible.)
  4. This soup tastes too salty.

6. Your turn

Write 4–5 sentences about what you feel, think and do right now, using at least three verbs with two meanings (think, have, see, taste, look…) in both uses.

  1. Example answer
Show the answers
  1. I'm having a quiet evening at home. I'm thinking about my holiday plans, but I think I'll stay in Belgium this year. My sister is seeing a new doctor, and she looks much better. The tea I'm drinking tastes of mint, and it smells wonderful.
Key points
  • State verbs (know, believe, like, want, own, belong, seem, contain, cost…): simple form, even for now.
  • Verbs with two meanings: think, have, see, taste, smell, look, weigh, appear — the meaning decides.
  • Involuntary perception: I can see / I can hear; look / smell / taste / sound + adjective.
  • be being + adjective = temporary behaviour; feel / hurt: both forms possible.
  • “I'm loving it” is informal; “I was wondering if…” is a polite formula.
