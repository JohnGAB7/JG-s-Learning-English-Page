Modality: fine nuances

Must or have to, needn't have or didn't need to, could or was able to, an irritated will, an expectant should: English modals express nuances that many languages express with one or two verbs. At C1 these nuances change the meaning of a message, especially in criticism, instructions and deductions.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level C1

Prefer explanations in French? Version française de cette leçon →

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Distinguish types of obligation precisely: must, have to, need to, be to, be supposed to, had better.
  • Contrast needn't have done with didn't need to do, and could with was able to.
  • Express past deduction and criticism precisely (can't have, may well have, should have, might have told me).
  • Recognise uses of will and would for typical behaviour, refusal and irritation.

Obligation: who imposes what?

“Must” expresses an obligation felt or imposed by the speaker (“I must call my mother”), or a written rule (“Passengers must wear seat belts”). “Have to” presents an external obligation, a constraint of the situation (“I have to wear a uniform at work”). In spoken British English, “have got to” is common.

“Be to” is used for official instructions and schedules: “You are to report to reception at 8 a.m.”, “The minister is to visit Kigali next week.” “Be supposed to” expresses what is planned or expected… and often not done: “We were supposed to finish yesterday.”

“Had better” is a warning about a specific situation (or there will be an unpleasant result): “You'd better leave now or you'll miss the train.” It is not general advice (should).

FormNuanceExample
mustspeaker's obligation, written ruleI must remember to call her.
have to / have got toexternal constraintI have to renew my visa every year.
need topractical necessityYou need to update the software.
be toofficial instruction, scheduleAll staff are to attend the briefing.
be supposed toplanned, expected (often not done)He was supposed to call me.
had betterwarning, specific situationWe'd better hurry.
  • You are not to leave the building without permission.
    Negative “be to”: an official prohibition.
  • The meeting was supposed to start at nine, but nobody was there.
    Planned, but it didn't happen.

No obligation: needn't have or didn't need to?

In the present, “needn't” (mainly BrE) and “don't need to / don't have to” mean “it isn't necessary”. “Mustn't”, by contrast, means “it is forbidden”.

In the past, the difference is essential. “I needn't have bought bread” = I bought some, but it was unnecessary (wasted action). “I didn't need to buy bread” = it wasn't necessary; usually I didn't buy any (or the context doesn't say).

FormWas the action done?Example
needn't have + past participleyes, but unnecessarilyYou needn't have waited for me. (= you waited for nothing)
didn't need to + baseusually notI didn't need to wait; the bus came at once.
mustn'tprohibitionYou mustn't park here.
don't have tono obligationYou don't have to come if you're busy.
  • We needn't have booked a table — the restaurant was empty.
    We booked, but it was unnecessary.
  • We didn't need to book a table, so we just turned up.
    No booking: it wasn't necessary.

Past ability: could, was able to, managed to

“Could” expresses general ability in the past: “She could speak three languages at the age of ten.” For a single success in a specific situation, use “was able to” or “managed to”: “Despite the storm, we were able to / managed to land.”

In the negative, “couldn't” works in both cases: “We couldn't find the hotel.” With verbs of perception (see, hear, smell) and understanding, “could” is possible for a specific moment: “I could hear voices next door.”

  • After three hours, the firefighters managed to put out the fire.
    A single success after effort: managed to.
  • My grandfather could repair almost anything.
    General ability: could.

Past deduction and criticism

The scale of past deduction: must have done (almost certain), may well have / probably (likely), may / might / could have (possible), can't / couldn't have (impossible). “Can't have” and “couldn't have” are almost synonyms; “couldn't have” is a little more frequent in narratives.

“Should have done” expresses regret or criticism (what would have been right); “shouldn't have” the opposite. “Could have done” shows a missed opportunity or a reproach: “You could have told me!”. “Might have” can carry the same reproach, mainly in British English: “You might have warned me.”

“Should” also expresses logical expectation: “They should have arrived by now” (normally, they will have arrived).

FormMeaningExample
must have + ppalmost certain deductionShe must have missed the bus.
may well have + pplikelyThe email may well have gone to spam.
might / could have + pppossibleHe might have forgotten.
can't / couldn't have + ppimpossibleThey can't have finished already.
should have + ppregret, criticism; expectationWe should have left earlier. / It should have arrived by now.
could / might have + ppreproach, missed opportunityYou could have asked me first!
  • You might have told me the meeting was cancelled!
    A reproach: why didn't you tell me?
  • The parcel should have arrived yesterday. I'll call the courier.
    An expectation that was not met.

Will and would: typical behaviour, refusal, irritation

“Will” describes characteristic behaviour in the present: “She'll sit and read for hours.” “Would” does the same in the past (habit): “My grandmother would tell us stories every evening.”

When stressed, “will” and “would” show irritation: “He WILL leave the door open!” (he always does it, annoyingly), “She WOULD say that!” (that's typical of her). “Won't” and “wouldn't” express refusal, even by objects: “The car won't start.”, “He wouldn't listen.”

“Would” also softens an opinion: “I would think so”, “I'd say it's about 20 %”.

  • The printer won't print in colour.
    An object “refusing”: won't.
  • When we were kids, we would spend every summer in Kribi.
    Past habit: would (with an action verb).

Traps: one verb, several modals

Many languages use a single verb for “must” and “have to”, or for “could” and “was able to”. In English, read the meaning first: “He must have forgotten” (deduction) is very different from “He had to leave early” (obligation). “You should have” = it would have been right; “You could have” = it was possible (and you didn't).

For a single past success, English says “I managed to reach him”, not “I could reach him”.

The formula

Choose the modal by source (speaker / situation / rule), by whether the action happened (needn't have vs didn't need to) and by degree of certainty (must / may well / might / can't have)

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • “have” is reduced in perfect modals: “must have” /ˈmʌstəv/, “should have” /ˈʃʊdəv/, “could've”. Write “should have”, never “should of”.
  • “needn't have” /ˈniːdnt əv/: the final t is often barely audible.
  • “had better” reduces to “'d better” /d ˈbetə/; “had” is almost silent: “You'd better go”.
  • For irritation, stress “will / would” strongly: “He WILL keep interrupting.”
  • “supposed to” is /səˈpəʊstə/: the d is not heard.

In a conversation

Listening Studio

The day after a trade fair in Abidjan, two colleagues review it on the phone.

Yasmine

The stand was supposed to be ready at eight, but the carpenters arrived at ten.
Planned, but not done.

Kevin

They must have got stuck in traffic. The roads were terrible.
An almost certain deduction.

Yasmine

Maybe. But they could have called us! We had no idea.
A reproach.

Kevin

True. Anyway, we managed to open on time in the end.
A single success: managed to.

Yasmine

Yes. And we needn't have printed 2,000 brochures — we gave away about 300.
Done, but unnecessary.

Kevin

We should have checked last year's numbers. I'll remember next time.
Regret.

Yasmine

And the projector wouldn't work on the second day. The technician will insist on using old cables.
Refusal of an object; “will insist” = annoying habit.

Kevin

We'd better find a new supplier before the Dakar fair, then.
A warning for a specific situation.

Exercises

Writing Workshop

From easy to harder. Answer without looking at the lesson, then check each exercise.

1. Which nuance?

Say which nuance it is: external obligation, official instruction, warning, or planned but not done.

  1. I have to wear a helmet on the site.
  2. Candidates are to arrive 30 minutes early.
  3. You'd better charge your phone before the trip.
  4. The train was supposed to leave at six.
Show the answers
  1. external obligation
  2. official instruction
  3. warning
  4. planned but not done

2. Needn't have or didn't need to?

Complete with the correct form of the verb in brackets.

  1. I took an umbrella, but it didn't rain. I ___ (take) it.
  2. The shop delivered for free, so we ___ (rent) a van. We just waited at home.
  3. You ___ (cook) so much food! There are only four of us.
  4. She already had a visa, so she ___ (apply) for one.
Show the answers
  1. needn't have taken
  2. didn't need to rent
  3. needn't have cooked
  4. didn't need to apply

3. Past deduction or criticism

Rewrite with a perfect modal (must / can't / might / should / could have).

  1. I'm sure she forgot her keys.
  2. It's impossible that they finished already.
  3. Perhaps he took the wrong train.
  4. It was a mistake not to leave earlier.
  5. Why didn't you tell me? It was possible!
Show the answers
  1. She must have forgotten her keys.
  2. They can't have finished already.
  3. He might / may / could have taken the wrong train.
  4. We should have left earlier.
  5. You could / might have told me!

4. Correct the mistake

Correct each sentence.

  1. After two hours, I could finally fix the leak.
  2. You had better to call the bank.
  3. They should of asked for permission.
  4. You mustn't bring food; lunch is provided. (meaning: not necessary)
Show the answers
  1. After two hours, I finally managed to / was able to fix the leak.
  2. You'd better call the bank.
  3. They should have asked for permission.
  4. You don't have to / needn't bring food; lunch is provided.

5. Choose the modal

Write a sentence with the right modal for the situation.

  1. Your friend didn't warn you about a problem. Reproach them.
  2. After many tries, you finally reached someone on the phone.
Show the answers
  1. You could have / might have warned me!
  2. I finally managed to reach him / get hold of him.

6. Your turn

Write 4 sentences about an event that went wrong, using: be supposed to, needn't have, must / can't have, should have.

  1. Example answer
Show the answers
  1. Our flight to Montreal was supposed to leave at 7 p.m., but it was delayed until midnight. We needn't have rushed to the airport after all. The airline must have known about the problem for hours, so they should have sent us a message earlier.
Key points
  • Must (speaker, rule) / have to (situation) / be to (official instruction) / be supposed to (planned, often not done) / had better + base (warning).
  • Needn't have done = done, but unnecessary; didn't need to do = not necessary (often not done).
  • Single success: managed to / was able to, not could; past deduction: must / may well / might / can't have + participle.
  • Should have = regret, criticism or expectation; could / might have = reproach; stressed will / would = irritation; won't = refusal.
