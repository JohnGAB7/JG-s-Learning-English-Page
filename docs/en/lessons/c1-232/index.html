Advanced ellipsis

English readily leaves out what is already clear: “I haven't finished, but Sara has”, “Anna ordered fish, and Tom pasta”, “Coming?”, “If necessary, call me”. Mastering ellipsis makes your English more natural in speech and smoother in writing, as long as you know what you can leave out and what you must keep.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level C1

Prefer explanations in French? Version française de cette leçon →

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Use ellipsis after an auxiliary, including with be, been and perfect modals (It should be. — It may have been.).
  • Use ellipsis with to (I'd love to), gapping (Anna ordered fish, and Tom pasta) and ellipsis in comparisons.
  • Recognise situational ellipsis in speech (Sounds good. — Seen Tom?) and ellipsis in subordinate clauses (If necessary, When in doubt).
  • Avoid typical mistakes: “Yes, I'm”, “I think” without so, “I didn't want” without to, missing be.

Ellipsis after an auxiliary

This is the core of English ellipsis: keep the auxiliary (or modal) and drop the rest of the verb phrase, which is already known. “I haven't finished the report, but Sara has (finished it).” “She can't drive, but her son can.”

If the first sentence has no auxiliary, use do / does / did: “I didn't enjoy the film, but my wife did.”

Key rule: contractions are impossible at the end of a sentence. Say “Yes, I am”, “I think she is”, never “Yes, I'm” or “I think she's”.

  • “Have you paid the bill?” “No, but I will.”
    Keep will; “pay the bill” is understood.
  • Nobody expected him to win, but he did.
    “did” replaces “won”.
  • “Are you ready?” “Yes, I am.”
    No contraction at the end.

Be, been and perfect modals: what to keep

When the verb left out is “be”, a modal on its own is not enough: keep “be”. “Is the room ready?” — “It should be.” (not “It should.”). Likewise: “He isn't at home, but he might be.”

With a passive or a perfect tense, you can keep several auxiliaries; British English often keeps “been” or “done”: “Has the parcel been delivered?” — “It may have (been).”; “Did he lock the door?” — “He might have (done).” (“done” is mainly British).

Question / sentenceCorrect ellipsisAvoid
Is it finished?It should be.It should.
Will they be there?They might be.They might.
Has it been fixed?It may have been. / It may have.It may.
Did she call?She might have. / She might have done. (BrE)She might.
  • “Is the report ready?” “It should be by Friday.”
    “be” is needed after the modal.

Ellipsis with “to” and in comparisons

After a verb followed by an infinitive (want, would like, need, try, hope, have, ought, used), keep “to” and drop the verb: “I'd love to.”, “You don't have to.”, “I used to.”. Dropping “to” as well is possible after “want” and “like” in some clauses (“You can leave if you want (to)”).

In comparisons, the second part is almost always reduced: “She earns more than I do” (more formal) / “than me” (everyday); “as soon as possible”; “Prices are higher than (they were) last year.”

  • “Do you want to come with us?” “I'd love to, but I can't.”
    “to” alone: “I'd love to come” is understood.
  • She speaks Spanish better than she did a year ago.
    “did” replaces “spoke Spanish”.

Gapping and ellipsis in coordinated clauses

In two parallel coordinated clauses, you can leave out the verb in the second one: this is gapping. “Anna ordered fish, and Tom pasta.” “Some prefer tea; others, coffee.” A comma often marks the gap. This is mainly a written style.

With the same subject, leave out subject and auxiliary in the second clause: “She was tired and (she) wanted to go home.” “We can stay or (we can) leave.”

  • In 2023, exports rose by 4 %; in 2024, by 7 %.
    Written gapping: “exports rose” is understood.
  • He picked up his keys, (he) locked the door and (he) left.
    Subject left out in a series of actions.

Ellipsis in noun phrases and subordinate clauses

In noun phrases, the noun is dropped after a determiner, possessive or superlative: “Some students passed; others didn't.”, “Her car is newer than mine.”, “This is the best I've seen.”

In time, condition and concession clauses, you can drop subject + be: “If necessary, call me.”, “When in doubt, ask.”, “Although tired, she continued.”, “Once finished, the form must be signed.”. Careful: the understood subject must be the same as in the main clause (otherwise the sentence “dangles”: see lesson 237).

  • If in doubt, contact your manager.
    = If you are in doubt…
  • While in Douala, we visited the port.
    = While we were in Douala…

Situational ellipsis in speech

In speech and messages, the beginning of a sentence (subject, auxiliary, it / there) is often dropped when the situation is clear: “(Have you) Seen Tom?”, “(It) Sounds great!”, “(Are you) Coming?”, “(I'm) Sorry, (I) can't make it.”, “(There's) No problem.”

This is very natural between colleagues or friends, but avoid it in formal writing. It works together with the substitutions “so / not” from B2: “I think so”, “I hope not”, “I'm afraid so”.

  • “Coffee?” “Love one, thanks.”
    = Would you like a coffee? — I'd love one.

The formula

Keep the auxiliary / modal (+ be / been when needed) or “to”, drop the rest that is already known; no contraction at the end

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • An auxiliary at the end is always in its full form and stressed: “Yes, I AM” /æm/, “She HAS” /hæz/, “I CAN” /kæn/.
  • Compare “I can swim” /aɪ kən ˈswɪm/ (reduced) and “Yes, I can” /ˈkæn/ (full).
  • “to” at the end keeps a full vowel: “I'd love to” /tuː/, never /tə/.
  • In gapping, a short pause marks the missing verb: “Anna ordered fish, | and Tom | pasta.”
  • Contrastive stress falls on what changes: “I haven't finished, but SAra HAS.”

In a conversation

Listening Studio

Two colleagues chat in an open-plan office in Brussels at the end of the day.

Julie

Finished the budget yet?
Situational ellipsis: (Have you) finished…?

Samuel

Not yet, but Karim has. I'm still on the travel costs.
Auxiliary ellipsis: Karim has (finished it).

Julie

Is the meeting with the auditors still on tomorrow?
“Still on” = still planned.

Samuel

It should be. Lena was going to confirm, but I don't think she has.
Keep “be”; “she has (confirmed)”.

Julie

I can call her if you want me to.
Ellipsis with to.

Samuel

Would you? That'd be great. Oh, and coffee before we go?
“Would you (call her)?”; “(Shall we have a) coffee…?”

Julie

Love to, but I can't. My train's at six.
(I'd) love to; I can't (have a coffee).

Samuel

No problem. If necessary, I'll stay late and finish the figures.
If (it is) necessary.

Exercises

Writing Workshop

From easy to harder. Answer without looking at the lesson, then check each exercise.

1. What is missing?

Write the words left out by ellipsis.

  1. I haven't read the book, but my sister has.
  2. Anna ordered fish, and Tom pasta.
  3. If necessary, I'll come back tomorrow.
  4. Sounds good to me.
Show the answers
  1. read the book
  2. ordered
  3. it is (If it is necessary)
  4. It (It sounds good to me.)

2. Short answers

Answer with a correct ellipsis (auxiliary or modal).

  1. “Are you tired?” (yes)
  2. “Is the report finished?” (probably, with should)
  3. “Did Paul lock the door?” (maybe, with might)
  4. “Do you want to join us?” (yes, with love)
  5. “Will it be expensive?” (I hope it won't)
Show the answers
  1. Yes, I am.
  2. It should be.
  3. He might have. / He might have done. (BrE)
  4. I'd love to.
  5. I hope not.

3. Remove the repetition

Rewrite the sentence using ellipsis.

  1. I can't come on Monday, but Lisa can come on Monday.
  2. She didn't like the hotel, but her husband liked the hotel.
  3. In 2022, sales rose by 3 %, and in 2023, sales rose by 5 %.
  4. You can leave early if you want to leave early.
Show the answers
  1. I can't come on Monday, but Lisa can.
  2. She didn't like the hotel, but her husband did.
  3. In 2022, sales rose by 3 %, and in 2023, by 5 %.
  4. You can leave early if you want (to).

4. Correct the mistake

Correct each sentence.

  1. “Is she your manager?” “Yes, she's.”
  2. “Will they be late?” “They might.”
  3. “Can you help me?” “I'd like, but I can't.”
  4. Although being exhausted, he kept running.
Show the answers
  1. “Yes, she is.”
  2. “They might be.”
  3. “I'd like to, but I can't.”
  4. Although exhausted, he kept running. / Although he was exhausted, he kept running.

5. Answer naturally

Write a natural short reply using ellipsis.

  1. “Are you coming tonight?” (You'd like to, but you can't.)
  2. “Will it be sunny tomorrow?” (You believe it will.)
Show the answers
  1. I'd love to, but I can't.
  2. I think so.

6. Your turn

Write a short exchange (5–6 lines) between two colleagues organising a meeting. Use at least four different kinds of ellipsis.

  1. Example answer
Show the answers
  1. A: Booked the room yet? B: No, but Marie has. A: Great. Is the projector working? B: It should be. I tested it on Monday, but the IT team might have changed something. A: I'll check if you want me to. B: Would you? Thanks. A: No problem. If necessary, we can use my laptop.
Key points
  • Keep the auxiliary or modal and drop the rest: I haven't, but Sara has. With no auxiliary, use do / does / did.
  • No contraction at the end (Yes, I am); keep “be” after a modal (It should be); keep “to” (I'd love to).
  • Gapping in writing (Anna ordered fish, and Tom pasta); ellipsis in subordinate clauses (If necessary, When in doubt, Although tired).
  • In speech, situational ellipsis (Sounds good, Seen Tom?); “I think so / I hope not”, never “I think” alone.
