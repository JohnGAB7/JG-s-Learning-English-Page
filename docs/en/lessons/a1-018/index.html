Prepositions of time (at, on, in)

At, on and in show when something happens: at 8 o'clock, on Monday, in June. The choice follows a simple logic, from the most exact (at) to the longest periods (in), with a few fixed expressions to learn.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level A1

Prefer explanations in French? Version française de cette leçon →

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Use at for clock times and exact moments, on for days and dates, in for months, seasons, years and parts of the day.
  • Remember the exceptions: at night, at the weekend, on Monday morning.
  • Know when to use no preposition: this, next, last, every, today, tomorrow.
  • Use from… to, until, before and after for a period or a simple time reference.

The pyramid: at, on, in

Imagine a pyramid: at the top, the most exact time (at + a clock time); in the middle, a day (on); at the bottom, longer periods (in + month, season, year, century).

The type of time decides, not the verb.

PrepositionWithExamples
atclock times, exact momentsat 7 o'clock, at half past six, at noon, at midnight, at lunchtime, at the moment
atholiday periods, the weekend (UK)at Christmas, at Easter, at the weekend
ondayson Monday, on Fridays, on my birthday, on Christmas Day
ondateson 5 May, on 21 March 2026
inmonths, seasons, years, centuriesin June, in (the) winter, in 2025, in the 21st century
inparts of the dayin the morning, in the afternoon, in the evening
  • The meeting is at ten o'clock.
    Clock time → at.
  • I don't work on Sundays.
    Day → on.
  • My birthday is in April.
    Month → in.
  • We go to the beach in summer.
    Season → in.

Exceptions to learn

We say “in the morning / in the afternoon / in the evening”, but “at night”, with no article.

When you say the day, “on” wins: on Monday morning, on Friday evening, on the morning of 3 May.

The weekend: “at the weekend” in British English, “on the weekend” in American English. Holidays: “at Christmas” (the Christmas period), but “on Christmas Day” (25 December).

  • I can't sleep at night.
    at night.
  • See you on Saturday afternoon!
    Day + part of day → on.
  • What do you do at the weekend?
    UK; US: on the weekend.
  • We visit our family at Christmas.
    The Christmas period.

No preposition

Don't use at, on or in before this, next, last, every, or before today, tomorrow, yesterday, tonight. Say “next Monday”, not “on next Monday”; “this morning”, not “in this morning”.

This is a very common mistake: “every day”, “next week”, “tonight” need no preposition.

WrongRight
on next Fridaynext Friday
in this morningthis morning
at tomorrowtomorrow
on every Mondayevery Monday
in last yearlast year
  • I'm busy this afternoon.
    No preposition before this.
  • Our exam is next Thursday.
    No preposition before next.

Saying a date

In British writing: on 5 May or on 5th May. In American writing: on May 5 or on May 5th. When you speak, use the ordinal number: “on the fifth of May” (UK) or “on May fifth” (US). Ordinal numbers (first, second, third…) are in lessons 22 and 89.

For a year alone: in 2026 (“in twenty twenty-six”). For a month and a year: in May 2026.

  • The concert is on 14 July.
    Say: on the fourteenth of July.
  • She was born in 1998.
    “was born”: learn it as a fixed phrase.

From… to, until, before, after

For a period with a start and an end: “from … to …” or “from … until …”: The shop is open from 9 to 6. “Until” (or “till”, more informal) shows the end: I work until five.

“Before” and “after” go before a time or a noun: before lunch, after work, after ten o'clock. For a time in the future, “in” means “after this amount of time”: The bus leaves in ten minutes.

  • The bank is open from Monday to Friday.
    Start and end.
  • I'm at the office until six.
    End point.
  • Let's meet after work.
    after + noun.
  • The film starts in five minutes.
    in + amount of time in the future.

The formula

at + clock time / exact moment / night / the weekend · on + day / date · in + month / season / year / the morning · Ø + this / next / last / every / today / tomorrow

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • In the sentence, “at” is weak: /ət/ (at six /ət ˈsɪks/). The time has the stress.
  • “on” /ɒn/ and “in” /ɪn/ join the next word when it starts with a vowel: on_April, in_August.
  • Days of the week are stressed on the first syllable: MONday /ˈmʌndeɪ/, WEDnesday /ˈwenzdeɪ/ (the first d is silent), THURSday /ˈθɜːzdeɪ/.
  • “until” /ənˈtɪl/: stress on the second syllable.
  • In a spoken date, “the” and “of” are weak: on the fifth of May /ɒn ðə ˈfɪfθ əv ˈmeɪ/.

In a conversation

Listening Studio

Two colleagues in Brussels plan a meeting with a Canadian client.

Sarah

When is the meeting with the Canadian client?
When…? question.

Ben

It's on Thursday, at three o'clock our time.
on + day, at + time.

Sarah

Thursday? I'm at the dentist's in the afternoon. Can we do it in the morning?
in the afternoon / in the morning.

Ben

Not really. In Montréal it's six hours earlier, so nine in the morning here is three in the morning for them.
Time difference.

Sarah

Ha! They don't work at night! What about next Monday?
at night; next Monday (no preposition).

Ben

Monday is fine. They're free from eight to eleven, their time.
from… to.

Sarah

Perfect. So on Monday at four, our time. I'm in the office until six.
until.

Ben

Great. I'm sending them an email in five minutes.
in + amount of time.

Key points
  • at + clock time, exact moment, night, Christmas, the weekend (UK).
  • on + day, date, Monday morning, my birthday.
  • in + month, season, year, century, the morning / afternoon / evening.
  • No preposition before this, next, last, every, today, tomorrow, tonight.
  • from… to / until for a period; in + amount of time = after that time from now (in ten minutes).
