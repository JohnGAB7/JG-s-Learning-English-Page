The language of research papers

A research paper in English follows a highly standardised structure and set of phrases: each section (introduction, methods, results, discussion) has its own verbs, tenses and expressions. Knowing them helps you read faster and write a paper that sounds like those in international journals.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level C1

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Know the IMRaD structure and the typical phrases of each section.
  • Choose the right tense for each section: present for established facts, present perfect for the state of research, past simple for your own method and results.
  • Present results, refer to a table or figure, and state the limitations of a study.
  • Use abbreviations correctly (e.g., i.e., et al.) and avoid common awkward phrasing.

The IMRaD structure and its phrases

Most empirical papers follow the IMRaD plan: Introduction, Methods, Results and Discussion, preceded by an abstract (see lesson 254) and followed by a conclusion. Each section answers a question: why? how? what was found? what does it mean?

The introduction moves from general to specific: the field, what is known, what is not yet known (the gap), then the aim of the paper.

SectionFunctionTypical phrases
Introductionset the context, show the gap, state the aimRecent years have seen a growing interest in… / However, little is known about… / This paper aims to…
Methodsdescribe what was doneParticipants were recruited from… / Data were analysed using… / A total of 312 responses were obtained.
Resultspresent without interpretingTable 2 presents… / As shown in Figure 1,… / X was significantly higher than Y.
Discussioninterpret, compare, qualifyThese findings are consistent with… / One possible explanation is that… / This study has several limitations.
Conclusionsummarise the contribution, look aheadThis study has shown that… / Future research should…
  • However, little is known about the long-term effects of remote learning.
    A classic phrase to show the research gap.
  • This paper aims to examine how small firms in Cameroon use mobile payments.
    Stating the aim: aim to + verb.

Tenses by section

The choice of tense is not free: it tells the reader the status of the information.

Present simple: general truths and established facts, what tables show, what the paper does (“This paper argues…”, “Table 3 shows…”). Present perfect: the state of research up to now (“Several studies have examined…”). Past simple: what you did and found (method, results), and a specific dated study (“Smith (2019) found that…”).

TenseUseExample
Present simpleestablished facts; what the text or figure saysSleep plays a key role in memory. / Figure 2 shows the distribution.
Present perfectoverview of research, period up to nowFew studies have addressed this question.
Past simpleyour method and results; a specific studyWe interviewed 40 teachers. / Ngo (2021) found a similar effect.
Modalscautious interpretation (discussion)This may reflect differences in income.
  • Previous research has focused mainly on urban areas.
    Present perfect: the state of research up to now.
  • The questionnaire was distributed in March 2025.
    Past simple passive: a specific fact of the method.

Passive voice or “we”?

The Methods section traditionally uses the passive, because the action matters more than the person: “Samples were collected…”. But many journals now accept or encourage “we” for more direct prose: “We collected samples…”. Follow the journal's author guidelines and be consistent.

“I” is rare in experimental sciences but common in the humanities when there is a single author. “One” (“one can observe”) sounds old-fashioned and is best avoided.

  • All interviews were recorded and transcribed.
    Passive: the focus is on the procedure.
  • We hypothesised that older users would trust the app less.
    “We”: an active style accepted by many journals (AmE: hypothesized).

Presenting and discussing results

In Results, you describe without interpreting: you refer to the table, give the figures and state what is significant. Interpretation belongs in the Discussion.

References to visuals: “as shown in Table 1”, “(see Figure 3)”, “Table 1 summarises…”. Comparisons: “significantly higher than”, “no significant difference was found between…”, “a positive correlation between X and Y”.

FunctionPhrases
ReferringAs shown in Table 1, … / Figure 2 illustrates… / (see Appendix A)
ComparingX was significantly higher / lower than Y. / No significant difference was found between…
RelatingThere was a strong positive correlation between X and Y.
Interpreting (discussion)This suggests that… / One possible explanation is… / These results are in line with…
Comparing with the literatureThis finding is consistent with / contrasts with earlier work by…
LimitationsThis study has several limitations. First, the sample was small. / The results should be interpreted with caution.
  • As shown in Table 2, satisfaction was significantly higher in the rural group.
    Reference + comparison, no interpretation.
  • These results should be interpreted with caution, given the small sample size.
    A standard limitation sentence.

Citing other work

There are two ways to cite: author-focused (“Diallo (2020) argues that…”) or information-focused (“Mobile money has reduced transaction costs (Diallo, 2020)”). Lesson 236 looks at reporting verbs (argue, claim, suggest…) in detail.

“et al.” (Latin et alii, “and others”) is used for three or more authors in most styles: “Chen et al. (2022)”. It takes a full stop, and the verb is plural: “Chen et al. report…”.

  • Chen et al. (2022) report similar results in Kenya.
    “et al.” + plural verb (report).
  • This pattern has been observed in other regions (e.g., Asogwa, 2019; Petit, 2023).
    “e.g.” = for example, before a list that is not complete.
“e.g.” (exempli gratia) = for example; “i.e.” (id est) = that is. American English puts a comma after them (“e.g.,”); British English often leaves it out.

Awkward phrasing to avoid

Some academic traditions like long sentences and elaborate announcements (“In a first time, we will see…”). Scientific English prefers shorter sentences, one main idea per sentence, and brief signposting: “Section 2 reviews the literature; Section 3 describes the method.”

Watch “actual” (= real, not current), “precise” used as a verb (“we precise that” is wrong: “we specify / clarify that”), and note that “the present study” means “this study” (correct and frequent).

The formula

Introduction (present / present perfect → gap → aim) · Methods (past simple, often passive) · Results (past simple + references to tables) · Discussion (present + modals, comparison, limitations)

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • “et al.” is read /et ˈæl/; “e.g.” is read /ˌiː ˈdʒiː/ or “for example”; “i.e.” /ˌaɪ ˈiː/ or “that is”.
  • “analysed / analyzed” is /ˈænəlaɪzd/: the final -ed is /d/; “hypothesised” is /haɪˈpɒθəsaɪzd/.
  • “significantly” is /sɪɡˈnɪfɪkəntli/, stressed on the second syllable.
  • “methodology” is /ˌmeθəˈdɒlədʒi/, main stress on “dol”, as in all words ending in -ology.
  • At a conference, “Table 2” is /ˈteɪbl ˈtuː/ and “Figure 3” is /ˈfɪɡə ˈθriː/ (BrE); American English says /ˈfɪɡjər/.

Common mistakes

Don’t saySayWhy
✗ In a first time, we will present the literature.✓ Section 2 reviews the literature. / First, we review the literature.“In a first time” is a translation, not English.
✗ We precise that the sample was small.✓ We should point out that the sample was small. / It should be noted that the sample was small.“Precise” is an adjective, not a verb.
✗ Many studies showed this effect since 2010.✓ Many studies have shown this effect since 2010.State of research up to now (since): present perfect.
✗ We have interviewed 40 teachers in May 2024.✓ We interviewed 40 teachers in May 2024.A dated method step: past simple.
✗ Chen et al. reports similar results.✓ Chen et al. report similar results.“et al.” = several authors: plural verb.
✗ The actual situation of research is limited.✓ The current state of research is limited.“Actual” = real; for “now”, use current.
✗ The results prove clearly our hypothesis.✓ The results clearly support our hypothesis.Adverb position (before the verb) and an overly strong verb (prove).

In a conversation

Listening Studio

In Louvain-la-Neuve, two researchers check the draft of a paper before submission.

Sophie

Your introduction starts with “In a first time, we will see the context.” Reviewers will notice that.
A translated phrase.

Ibrahim

Right. What about “Recent years have seen a growing interest in urban farming”?
A standard opening line.

Sophie

Perfect. Then show the gap: “However, little is known about its economic impact.”
The gap comes next.

Ibrahim

In the methods, I wrote “We have collected data in 2024.”
A tense problem.

Sophie

With a date, it's the past simple: “Data were collected in 2024”, or “We collected data”.
Passive or “we”, but past simple.

Ibrahim

And in the results, can I say the yields are higher because of the soil?
He wants to interpret too early.

Sophie

Keep that for the discussion. In the results, just write “Yields were significantly higher in Plot B (see Table 3).”
Results describe; discussion interprets.

Ibrahim

And then in the discussion: “One possible explanation is the soil quality.”
Cautious interpretation.

Sophie

Exactly. And add a sentence on limitations: the sample is small.
Every paper needs limitations.

Exercises

Writing Workshop

From easy to harder. Answer without looking at the lesson, then check each exercise.

1. Which section of the paper?

Say which section (Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion) the sentence comes from.

  1. However, little is known about the role of grandparents in childcare.
  2. Participants were recruited through three local schools.
  3. As shown in Table 4, response times were significantly shorter in Group B.
  4. One possible explanation for this difference is the training received.
Show the answers
  1. Introduction
  2. Methods
  3. Results
  4. Discussion

2. The right tense

Put the verb in brackets into the correct tense.

  1. Several studies ___ (examine) this issue since 2015.
  2. We ___ (interview) 25 nurses in June 2024.
  3. Table 1 ___ (show) the characteristics of the sample.
  4. Diallo (2019) ___ (find) a similar effect in Senegal.
  5. Water ___ (boil) at 100 °C at sea level.
Show the answers
  1. have examined
  2. interviewed
  3. shows
  4. found
  5. boils

3. Complete the phrase

Write the missing word.

  1. These findings are consistent ___ previous research.
  2. ___ shown in Figure 2, the trend is upward.
  3. This paper ___ to examine the effects of the reform.
  4. The results should be interpreted with ___.
Show the answers
  1. with
  2. As
  3. aims
  4. caution

4. Correct the mistake

Correct each sentence.

  1. We precise that all participants were volunteers.
  2. Lopez et al. argues that the policy failed.
  3. We have collected the data in 2023.
Show the answers
  1. It should be noted that all participants were volunteers.
  2. Lopez et al. argue that the policy failed.
  3. We collected the data in 2023. / The data were collected in 2023.

5. Write the academic sentence

Write a research-paper sentence that expresses the idea.

  1. Say that your study has some weaknesses.
  2. Say that not many studies have looked at this question.
  3. Say that readers should be careful when interpreting the results.
Show the answers
  1. This study has several limitations.
  2. Few studies have addressed / examined this question.
  3. The results should be interpreted with caution.

6. Your turn

Write a short paper introduction (4 sentences): context, what is known, the gap, the aim.

  1. Example answer
Show the answers
  1. Recent years have seen a growing interest in online learning in sub-Saharan Africa. Several studies have shown that it can widen access to higher education. However, little is known about how students in rural areas cope with unstable internet connections. This paper aims to examine the strategies used by 120 students in the North-West Region of Cameroon.
Key points
  • IMRaD: Introduction (context → gap → aim), Methods, Results (describe), Discussion (interpret, compare, state limitations).
  • Present for established facts and what the text shows; present perfect for the state of research; past simple for your method, your results and a dated study.
  • Key phrases: little is known about, this paper aims to, as shown in Table 1, consistent with, should be interpreted with caution.
  • “et al.” + plural verb; e.g. = for example, i.e. = that is; no “in a first time”, no “we precise”.
