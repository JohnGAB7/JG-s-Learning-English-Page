Nuances of the present perfect

The present perfect is one of the most difficult tenses for learners, because many languages use a past tense or a present tense where English uses it. At B2 you need its finer points: the link with now, experience, duration, result or activity, been / gone, and the differences between British and American English.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level B2

Prefer explanations in French? Version française de cette leçon →

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Choose between the present perfect and the past simple depending on whether the time period is finished.
  • Express duration with for / since and ask “How long have you…?”.
  • Distinguish the present perfect simple (result, quantity) from the continuous (activity, duration).
  • Use “It's the first time I've…”, “the best … I've ever…”, been / gone and the present perfect after when.

The key idea: a link with now

The present perfect connects the past to the present: a past action whose result matters now, an experience in a life that continues, a situation that is still going on.

The past simple places the action in a finished period, often stated (yesterday, in 2010, last week, when I was a child, ago). With a specific past time, the present perfect is impossible.

Watch the time period: “this morning” takes the present perfect if it is still morning (“I've had three coffees this morning”), and the past simple if it is already afternoon (“I had three coffees this morning”).

Present perfect (open period, link with now)Past simple (finished period)
I've lost my keys. (I still don't have them)I lost my keys yesterday, but I found them.
She has written three novels. (she is still writing)Chinua Achebe wrote several novels. (he is no longer alive)
Have you seen the news today?Did you see the news yesterday?
ever, never, already, yet, just, so far, recently, lately, sinceyesterday, last…, ago, in 2010, when…, at 5 o'clock
In the news, a story is often announced in the present perfect, then the details are given in the past simple: “A fire has destroyed a market in Kumasi. It started at 3 a.m. and firefighters arrived an hour later.”

Experience: ever, never, the first time, the best…

To talk about what you have (or haven't) done in your life, without saying when: present perfect with ever (in questions), never, before, once, twice, three times.

After “It's / This is the first (second…) time”, use the present perfect: “It's the first time I've been here.” Many learners wrongly use the present simple.

After a superlative: “It's the best film I've ever seen.”

  • Have you ever eaten ndolé?
    Experience question.
  • I've been to Canada twice.
    Number of times.
  • It's the first time I've driven on the left.
    first time + present perfect.
  • That's the most beautiful beach I've ever seen.
    Superlative + ever.

Duration: for and since

For a situation that started in the past and continues now, English needs the present perfect: “I've lived here since 2015”. “I live here since 2015” is one of the most common learner errors.

For + a period (for ten years, for ages); since + a starting point (since 2015, since Monday, since I was a child). After “since”, a clause is in the past simple: “since I moved to Liège”.

The duration question is “How long have you…?”. “Since when…?” exists, but often expresses surprise or irony.

Another structure: “It's (been) two years since I last saw her” = “I haven't seen her for two years”.

  • I've known Aïcha since primary school.
    since + starting point.
  • How long have you been learning English?
    Duration question.
  • We've been married for twelve years.
    for + period.
  • It's been ages since we spoke.
    It's been + time + since.

Simple or continuous: result or activity?

The present perfect simple focuses on the result or the quantity: “I've written three emails” (how many). The continuous focuses on the activity and how long it lasted: “I've been writing emails all morning.”

The continuous also explains a visible result: “You're all wet! – I've been running.”

With for / since, both are often possible with verbs like live, work, teach and study: “I've lived / I've been living here for ten years”. But state verbs (know, believe, own, love, understand) stay simple: “I've known her for years”, never “I've been knowing”.

Simple: result / quantityContinuous: activity / duration
I've painted two rooms.I've been painting all day.
She has read the report.She has been reading reports since eight.
How many kilometres have you run?How long have you been running?
I've known him for years. (state verb)Impossible: *I've been knowing him

Been or gone? And British / American differences

“He has gone to Paris”: he went and he is still there (or on the way). “He has been to Paris”: he went and came back (experience).

In American English, people often use the past simple with just, already and yet: “Did you eat yet?”, “I already did it”. In British English, the present perfect is still the norm: “Have you eaten yet?”, “I've already done it”.

After when, as soon as, after and until, you can use the present perfect for a future action that will be finished: “I'll call you when I've finished.”

  • Where's Paul? – He's gone to the bank.
    He is at the bank now.
  • Paul has been to the bank. Here's your money.
    He went and came back.
  • British: Have you finished yet? / American: Did you finish yet?
    Both are correct in their variety.
  • I'll send it as soon as I've checked it.
    Future, finished action.

The formula

have / has + past participle (+ ever, never, already, yet, for, since) · have / has been + -ing for the activity

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • Contractions: I've /aɪv/, you've /juːv/, he's /hiːz/ (= he has before a past participle), we've, they've. The final /v/ is light but must be heard.
  • “he's been”: 's = has; “he's going”: 's = is. The next word tells you which.
  • “been” is weak, /bɪn/: “I've been waiting” /aɪv bɪn ˈweɪtɪŋ/.
  • “for” is weak, /fə/: “for ten years” /fə ˈten ˈjɪəz/; “since” /sɪns/ is not reduced.
  • In “Have you ever…?” questions, the voice rises at the end: “Have you ever been to Dakar ↗?”

In a conversation

Listening Studio

A job interview in Geneva. Mrs Keller, the HR manager, meets Ousmane, a candidate from Cameroon.

Mrs Keller

Thank you for coming. Have you been to Geneva before?
Experience: been to.

Ousmane

Yes, twice. But it's the first time I've visited your offices.
first time + present perfect.

Mrs Keller

Tell me about your current job. How long have you worked there?
Duration question.

Ousmane

I've been working for a logistics company in Lyon since 2021. Before that, I worked in Douala for three years.
Present perfect continuous (still true) vs past simple (finished).

Mrs Keller

And what have you achieved there?
Results up to now.

Ousmane

I've reduced delivery times by 20%, and I've trained a team of twelve people.
Present perfect simple: results.

Mrs Keller

Impressive. Have you ever managed a project in English?
Experience question with ever.

Ousmane

Yes. Lately I've been leading a project with our partners in Toronto, so I use English every day.
lately + continuous: recent activity.

Exercises

Writing Workshop

From easy to harder. Answer without looking at the lesson, then check each exercise.

1. Present perfect or past simple?

Choose the correct form.

  1. I (lost / have lost) my keys. Can you help me look for them?
  2. We (went / have been) to Kribi last summer.
  3. (Did you ever eat / Have you ever eaten) cassava leaves?
  4. Picasso (painted / has painted) thousands of works.
Show the answers
  1. have lost
  2. went
  3. Have you ever eaten (in American English, Did you ever eat is also possible)
  4. painted

2. Complete

Put the verb in brackets into the present perfect simple or continuous.

  1. I ___ (know) Aïcha since we were at school.
  2. It's the first time I ___ (drive) on the right.
  3. She ___ (work) here for ten years, and she still loves it.
  4. Sorry, I'm covered in paint. I ___ (paint) the kitchen.
  5. I ___ (write) three emails so far this morning.
  6. He isn't here. He ___ (go) to the bank.
Show the answers
  1. have known
  2. have driven
  3. has worked (or has been working)
  4. have been painting
  5. have written
  6. has gone

3. Rewrite

Rewrite each sentence starting with the words given.

  1. I started working here in 2019. (I… since)
  2. The last time I saw Marc was in June. (I haven't…)
  3. This is my first visit to Quebec. (It's the first time…)
  4. I've never seen such a beautiful beach. (It's the most…)
  5. It's ages since we spoke. (We haven't…)
Show the answers
  1. I have worked (I have been working) here since 2019.
  2. I haven't seen Marc since June.
  3. It's the first time I've visited (been to) Quebec.
  4. It's the most beautiful beach I've ever seen.
  5. We haven't spoken for ages.

4. Correct the mistakes

Each sentence has one mistake. Write it correctly.

  1. I live in Brussels since 2015.
  2. I have seen him yesterday.
  3. She has gone to Canada three times.
  4. I've been knowing her for years.
Show the answers
  1. I've lived (I've been living) in Brussels since 2015.
  2. I saw him yesterday.
  3. She has been to Canada three times.
  4. I've known her for years.

5. Ask the question

Write a question with the words given (How long…? / Have you ever…? / …yet?).

  1. you / know / your best friend (how long)
  2. you / ever / be / to Africa
  3. she / wait / here (how long, activity)
  4. they / finish / the project / yet
Show the answers
  1. How long have you known your best friend?
  2. Have you ever been to Africa?
  3. How long has she been waiting here?
  4. Have they finished the project yet?

6. Your turn

Present your background in 4–5 sentences, as in an interview: an experience (ever / never), a duration (for / since), a result, a recent activity (been + -ing) and a finished fact in the past simple.

  1. Example answer
Show the answers
  1. I've worked as an accountant for eight years. I joined my current company in 2020, and since then I've reorganised the whole invoicing system. I've never worked abroad, but I've been to Canada three times for conferences. Lately I've been studying for a finance certificate in English.
Key points
  • Present perfect = link with now; past simple = finished period (yesterday, ago, in 2010).
  • Situations that continue: I've lived here since 2015 / for ten years; How long have you…?
  • It's the first time I've…; the best … I've ever…
  • Simple = result, quantity; continuous = activity, duration; no continuous with state verbs.
  • gone (still there) is not been (there and back); “when I've finished” = a finished future action.
