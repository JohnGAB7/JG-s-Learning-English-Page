A1 · Beginner · lesson 23

Family

Family is one of the first things people talk about. This lesson gives you the words for your close family, your wider family and your family by marriage, and the sentences to present them.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level A1

Prefer explanations in French? Version française de cette leçon →

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Name the people in your close and wider family (parents, brothers and sisters, uncles, cousins…).
  • Use words for family by marriage and new families (in-laws, stepfather, half-sister).
  • Present your family with “have got”, the possessive ’s and possessive adjectives.
  • Avoid common traps: “parents” vs “relatives”, and irregular plurals (children).

Close family

Many words come in male / female pairs. At home, people often say “mum” and “dad” (American English: “mom” and “dad”).

“Child” has an irregular plural: “children”. “Siblings” (brothers and sisters) is a little formal; in conversation, people usually say “brothers and sisters”.

  • My mum is a teacher and my dad is a taxi driver.
    Informal words for parents.
  • They have two children: a son and a daughter.
    children = plural of child.
  • This is my wife, Aïcha.
    Introducing a family member.
“Wife” means a married woman (someone's partner). For any female adult, say “a woman”.

The wider family

Add “grand-” for the generation of your grandparents and your grandchildren: grandmother (grandma, granny), grandfather (grandpa), grandson, granddaughter.

“Cousin” is the same word for a man and a woman. For the generation before your grandparents, add “great-”: great-grandmother.

    grandparents = grandmother + grandfather.
  • I've got ten cousins!
    cousin: male or female.

Family by marriage and new families

For your husband's or wife's family, add “-in-law”: mother-in-law, brother-in-law. In the plural, the first word takes the s: sisters-in-law. All of them together: “my in-laws”.

In a new family, “step-” is for your parent's new partner or their children: stepfather (your mother's husband, not your father), stepsister. “Half-” is for a brother or sister with only one parent the same as you: half-brother.

Careful: “mother-in-law” is your partner's mother; “stepmother” is your father's wife. They are two different people.

  • My mother-in-law makes the best ndolé.
    My husband's or wife's mother.
  • My stepfather is from Ghent.
    My mother's husband.
  • Leo is my half-brother: we have the same dad.
    One parent in common.

“Parents” and “relatives”

In English, “parents” means only your mother and father. For other people in your family (uncles, cousins…), say “relatives” or “relations”.

“Family” is a singular noun: “My family is big.” In British English you also hear “My family are all tall” when you think of the people one by one.

  • My parents live in Montreal.
    My mother and father.
  • We have relatives in France and Belgium.
    Other people in our family.

Presenting your family

To say how many brothers, sisters or children you have, use “have got” (lesson 5) or “have”: “I've got two sisters.” / “I have two sisters.” The question: “Have you got any brothers or sisters?” or “Do you have any brothers or sisters?”.

For family links, use the possessive ’s (lesson 15): “my sister's husband”, “my parents' house”.

Useful phrases: “I'm an only child” (no brothers or sisters), “He's my older brother”, “She's my younger sister”, “We're twins”, “I'm married / single / divorced”.

  • Have you got any brothers or sisters? — Yes, I've got one brother.
    Question and answer with have got.
  • No, I haven't. I'm an only child.
    Use “an” before “only child”.
  • My sister's husband is a nurse.
    Possessive ’s for family links.

The formula

I've got + number + family word · my sister's husband · my parents' house

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • “mother”, “brother”, “other”: the o is /ʌ/ (like in “but”) and the th is /ð/: /ˈmʌðə/, /ˈbrʌðə/.
  • “daughter” /ˈdɔːtə/: you don't say the gh. The same in “granddaughter” /ˈɡrændɔːtə/.
  • “aunt” is /ɑːnt/ in British English and /ænt/ in American English (like “ant”, the insect).
  • “cousin” /ˈkʌzn/ and “husband” /ˈhʌzbənd/: the s sounds like /z/.
  • “niece” /niːs/ rhymes with “piece”; “nephew” /ˈnefjuː/ has the stress on the first syllable.

Common mistakes

Don’t saySayWhy
✗ I have three childs.✓ I have three children.Irregular plural: child → children.
✗ My parents are all at the wedding: my uncles, my cousins…✓ My relatives are all at the wedding.“Parents” = mother and father only; the wider family = relatives.
✗ She is my cousine.✓ She's my cousin.“Cousin” is the same for male and female.
✗ the brother of my mother✓ my mother's brother / my uncleUse the possessive ’s for family links.
✗ I am only child.✓ I'm an only child.You need the article “an”.
✗ He's my niece.✓ He's my nephew.nephew = male; niece = female.
✗ How many brothers do you have got?✓ How many brothers have you got? / How many brothers do you have?Don't mix “do” and “have got”.

In a conversation

Listening Studio

In Brussels, Inès shows family photos to her colleague Kofi.

Kofi

Is this your family?
A simple question with “to be”.

Inès

Yes! These are my parents, and this is my younger brother, Sami.
“younger brother” = little brother.

Kofi

And who's the woman with the baby?
“who's” = who is.

Inès

That's my aunt, my mum's sister. The baby is her granddaughter.
Possessive ’s for family links.

Kofi

So the baby is your cousin's daughter?
He checks the family link.

Inès

Exactly. Have you got a big family?
Question with have got.

Kofi

Very big! I've got four sisters and about thirty cousins in Ghana.
about = more or less.

Inès

Thirty cousins! I'm jealous. I've only got two.
“only” = not more than.

Exercises

Writing Workshop

From easy to harder. Answer without looking at the lesson, then check each exercise.

1. Male and female

Give the female word.

  2. son
  3. brother
  4. husband
  5. uncle
  6. nephew
Show the answers
  1. mother
  2. daughter
  3. sister
  4. wife
  5. aunt
  6. niece

2. Who is it?

Complete with the right family word.

  1. My mother's mother is my ___.
  2. My aunt's son is my ___.
  3. My brother's daughter is my ___.
  4. My father's brother is my ___.
  5. My wife's mother is my ___.
Show the answers
  1. grandmother
  2. cousin
  3. niece
  4. uncle
  5. mother-in-law

3. The possessive ’s

Rewrite with ’s or ’.

  1. the husband of my sister
  2. the children of my brother
  3. the house of my grandparents
  4. the wife of Paul
Show the answers
  1. my sister's husband
  2. my brother's children
  3. my grandparents' house
  4. Paul's wife

4. Correct the mistake

Each sentence has one mistake. Write it correctly.

  1. I've got three childs.
  2. We have parents in Douala: two uncles and a cousin.
  3. I'm only child.
  4. My mother-in-law is my father's new wife.
  5. Do you have got any brothers or sisters?
Show the answers
  1. I've got three children.
  2. We have relatives in Douala.
  3. I'm an only child.
  4. My stepmother is my father's new wife.
  5. Have you got any brothers or sisters? / Do you have any brothers or sisters?

5. Your turn

Present your family in four or five sentences: your parents, your brothers and sisters, your wider family.

  1. Model answer
Show the answers
  1. I've got a small family. My parents live in Liège. I've got one older sister, Clara. Her husband is from Canada and they've got two children, so I've got a nephew and a niece. My grandparents live in a village near Namur.
Key points
  • parents = mother and father only; the wider family = relatives.
  • child → children; wife → wives; cousin is male and female.
  • -in-law for family by marriage (mother-in-law), step- for a new family (stepfather), half- for a half-brother.
  • Family links with ’s: my sister's husband, my parents' house.
  • I've got two brothers. / I'm an only child. / Have you got any brothers or sisters?
