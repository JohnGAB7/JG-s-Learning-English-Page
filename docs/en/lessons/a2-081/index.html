At the doctor’s

Making an appointment, saying where it hurts, describing your symptoms, understanding the doctor’s advice and buying medicine at the pharmacy: these situations need exact words. This lesson gives you the key structures (I’ve got a…, My… hurts, I feel…) and advice with “should” (lesson 56). Of course, it does not replace medical advice.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level A2

Prefer explanations in French? Version française de cette leçon →

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Make an appointment with a doctor or a dentist.
  • Say where it hurts and describe symptoms with I’ve got, My… hurts, I feel.
  • Understand the doctor’s questions and advice (How long…?, You should…).
  • Manage at the pharmacy (prescription, how often to take medicine).

Making an appointment

On the phone: “I’d like to make an appointment with Dr Patel, please.” (for a doctor, always “an appointment”, never “a rendez-vous”). They may ask: “Is it urgent?” / “Can you come in at ten tomorrow?”

Places: in the UK you see “the doctor” or “the GP” (general practitioner, a family doctor) at the “surgery” or “health centre”; in the US you go to “the doctor’s office”. In an emergency: “A&E” (UK) / “the ER” (US) = the hospital emergency department.

We say “go to the doctor’s”, “go to the dentist’s”: the ’s means “the doctor’s place / surgery”.

  • I’d like to make an appointment with the doctor, please. — Is tomorrow morning OK?
    Booking by phone.
  • I need to see a dentist. I’ve got terrible toothache.
    toothache = pain in a tooth.
  • If it’s an emergency, call 999 in the UK or 911 in the US.
    Emergency numbers.

Saying where it hurts

Three main structures. 1) “I’ve got a + -ache word”: “I’ve got a headache”, “a stomach ache”, “a backache”, “earache”, “toothache” (in the UK, earache and toothache often have no “a”).

2) “My + body part + hurts”: “My back hurts.” “My feet hurt.” (plural → hurt). You can also say “My leg is sore.” / “I’ve got a sore throat.”

3) “It hurts when…”: “It hurts when I walk.” Careful: English uses a possessive (“my arm”), not “the”: “My arm hurts”, not “The arm hurts me”.

  • I’ve got a headache and a sore throat.
    Two problems with I’ve got.
  • My back hurts when I sit for a long time.
    My + body part + hurts.
  • Where does it hurt? — Here, in my chest.
    The doctor’s question.

Describing symptoms

With “I’ve got”: “I’ve got a cold”, “a cough”, “a temperature / a fever” (“a temperature” is common in the UK), “the flu”, “a rash” (red spots on the skin).

With “I feel” + adjective: “I feel sick” (UK: you want to vomit; US: “sick” = ill in general), “I feel dizzy” (the room seems to turn), “I feel tired”, “I don’t feel well”.

For how long, use the present perfect (lesson 51) with “for” or “since”: “I’ve had a cough for three days.” / “I’ve felt sick since yesterday.” Don’t say “I have a cough since three days”.

  • I don’t feel well. I think I’ve got the flu.
    the flu = influenza.
  • I’ve had a temperature since Monday.
    since + starting point.
  • I feel dizzy when I stand up.
    I feel + adjective.

The doctor’s questions and advice

Common questions: “What’s the matter?” / “What seems to be the problem?”, “How long have you had it?”, “Are you taking any medicine?”, “Are you allergic to anything?”

The examination: “Can you open your mouth?”, “I’m going to take your temperature / your blood pressure.”

Advice with “should” / “shouldn’t” (lesson 56): “You should stay in bed for two days.” “You shouldn’t go to work.” “Drink plenty of water.” And the medicine: “I’ll give you a prescription.” (a paper from the doctor to get medicine).

  • What seems to be the problem? — I’ve had a bad cough for a week.
    A polite opening question.
  • You should rest and drink plenty of water.
    plenty of = a lot of.
  • You shouldn’t do any sport for a few days.
    Negative advice.

At the pharmacy

The shop: “the chemist’s” or “the pharmacy” (UK), “the drugstore” or “the pharmacy” (US). The person: “the pharmacist” (or “the chemist” in the UK).

Useful phrases: “Have you got anything for a cough?”, “I’ve got a prescription from my doctor.”, “How often should I take it?”

Instructions: “Take two tablets three times a day, after meals.” “medicine” is often uncountable: “some medicine”; “drugs” can also mean illegal drugs, so “medicine” is safer.

  • Have you got anything for a sore throat?
    anything for + problem.
  • Take one tablet twice a day for five days.
    twice = two times.
  • Can I take this medicine with food?
    A safety question.
To wish someone a quick recovery: “Get well soon!” or “I hope you feel better soon.”

The formula

I’ve got a headache / a sore throat / a cold. · My back hurts. · I feel sick / dizzy. · I’ve had it for three days. · You should rest.

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • “ache” /eɪk/ sounds like “make”: headache /ˈhedeɪk/, stomach ache /ˈstʌmək eɪk/.
  • “stomach” /ˈstʌmək/: the ch is /k/, “STUM-uk”.
  • “cough” /kɒf/: the gh is /f/, “koff”.
  • “throat” /θrəʊt/: start with th (tongue between your teeth), then “rote”.
  • “medicine” /ˈmedsn/ or /ˈmedɪsn/: often two syllables in British English, “MED-sn”.
  • “appointment” /əˈpɔɪntmənt/: stress on “POINT”.

In a conversation

Listening Studio

Kofi, a Ghanaian student in Bristol, goes to see a GP because he doesn’t feel well.

Doctor

Good morning. What seems to be the problem?
A polite opening question.

Kofi

I don’t feel well. I’ve got a sore throat and a headache.
Two symptoms.

Doctor

How long have you had these symptoms?
Present perfect question.

Kofi

For three days. And I’ve had a temperature since last night.
for + period / since + starting point.

Doctor

Are you allergic to anything?
An important question.

Kofi

Yes, I’m allergic to penicillin.
allergic to.

Doctor

OK. Can you open your mouth? … It’s a throat infection. I’ll give you a prescription.
The examination and the result.

Kofi

How often should I take the tablets?
How often = how many times.

Doctor

Three times a day, after meals. And you should stay at home for two days.
Instructions and advice.

Kofi

Thank you, doctor.
“doctor” can be used to address the doctor.

Key points
  • an appointment; at the doctor’s; emergency = A&E (UK) / the ER (US).
  • Pain: I’ve got a headache / stomach ache / sore throat · My back hurts · It hurts when I…
  • Symptoms: I’ve got a cold / a cough / a temperature · I feel sick / dizzy · I don’t feel well.
  • How long: I’ve had a cough for three days / since Monday (present perfect).
  • Advice: You should rest. You shouldn’t go to work. Pharmacy: Take two tablets three times a day.
