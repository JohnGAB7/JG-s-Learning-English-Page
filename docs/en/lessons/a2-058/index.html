A2 · Elementary · lesson 58

Much, many, a lot of, a few, a little

These words talk about quantity: a large or a small amount. The right choice depends on two questions: is the noun countable or uncountable, and is the sentence positive, negative or a question?

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level A2

Prefer explanations in French? Version française de cette leçon →

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Choose between much (uncountable) and many (plural countable).
  • Use a lot of / lots of in positive sentences, and much / many mostly in negatives and questions.
  • Understand the difference between a few / a little (some: positive) and few / little (not enough: negative).
  • Ask How much…? / How many…? and use too much, too many, so many, plenty of.

Countable or uncountable: the starting point

A countable noun can be counted: one apple, two apples; it has a plural. An uncountable noun can't be counted directly: water, money, time, bread, information; it has no plural and takes a singular verb (lesson 59 looks at these nouns and their traps).

“Many” and “a few” go with plural countable nouns. “Much” and “a little” go with uncountable nouns. “A lot of” and “lots of” go with both.

Much / many or a lot of?

In positive sentences, everyday English uses “a lot of” or “lots of” (more informal): I've got a lot of friends. She drinks lots of coffee. “Much” in a positive sentence sounds very formal or strange: don't say “I have much money”.

In negatives and questions, “much” and “many” are natural: I haven't got much time. Are there many people? “A lot of” is also possible: Have you got a lot of work?

“Many” is a little easier than “much” in positive sentences, especially in writing: Many people think that… And after “too, so, as, very”, much and many are normal in positive sentences: There are too many cars. I've got so much work!

  • There are a lot of tourists in Bruges in August.
    Positive: a lot of.
  • We don't have much money this month.
    Negative: much.
  • Did you take many photos?
    Question: many.
  • I've got so much homework tonight!
    so much in a positive sentence.
With no noun after it, say “a lot” (not “a lot of”): I like it a lot. Thanks a lot. “Much” alone is very common in negatives: I don't eat much.

A few / a little: some, a small amount

“A few” + plural countable noun means a small number, but enough: I've got a few friends in Lyon. “A little” + uncountable noun means a small amount: Can I have a little milk?

“A little” can also go before an adjective, or alone: I'm a little tired. In speaking, “a bit” is more common: I'm a bit tired, a bit of sugar.

  • Wait a few minutes, please.
    a few + minutes.
  • I speak a little Spanish.
    a little + language.
  • Add a little salt.
    a little + uncountable.

Few / little without “a”: a negative meaning

Without “a”, the meaning changes: “few” and “little” mean “not many, not much, not enough”, with a negative idea. Compare: I've got a few friends (some friends, that's OK) / I've got few friends (not many friends, that's sad).

These forms are a little formal. In speaking, people often say “not many / not much”: I haven't got many friends. There isn't much hope.

Other useful expressions

“Plenty of” (more than enough) goes with both kinds of nouns: We've got plenty of time. There are plenty of chairs.

“Too much / too many” = more than is good or necessary: too much sugar, too many people. Don't confuse it with “very much”: I like it very much, where “much” is an adverb.

“How much” is also used to ask a price: How much is this jacket? How much does it cost?

  • Don't hurry. We've got plenty of time.
    More than enough.
  • There's too much noise in this café.
    Uncountable → too much.
  • How much are the tickets? — Twelve euros each.
    Asking a price.

The formula

many / a few + plural · much / a little + uncountable · a lot of + both · much / many mostly in negatives and questions

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • “a lot of” joins: /ə ˈlɒt əv/, often /əˈlɒdə/ in fast American English (“a lotta”).
  • “much” /mʌtʃ/ and “many” /ˈmeni/: the a in many sounds like the e in “bed”.
  • “a few” /ə ˈfjuː/: the “a” is weak but you must say it, or the meaning becomes negative (few).
  • “a little” /ə ˈlɪtl/: in American English, the t sounds like a quick d (/ˈlɪdl/).
  • In “How much is it?”, stress MUCH; the voice goes down at the end.

In a conversation

Listening Studio

Two friends prepare dinner for eight people in their shared flat in Montréal.

Lucie

How many people are coming tonight?
How many + plural.

Omar

Eight. So we need a lot of rice. How much rice have we got?
How much + uncountable.

Lucie

Not much. Only a little. And there are only a few tomatoes.
a little rice, a few tomatoes.

Omar

OK, I'll go to the shop. Do we need many drinks?
Question: many.

Lucie

No, we've got plenty of juice. But buy a few bottles of water.
plenty of = more than enough.

Omar

And bread? We haven't got much.
much alone, in a negative.

Lucie

Buy two baguettes. Don't buy too many sweets this time!
too many + plural.

Omar

Ha ha, OK. I've got very little money anyway.
very little = almost no money.

  2. much

  5. much

  1. Can I ask you ___ questions?
  2. I need ___ help with this exercise.
  3. We're staying in Kribi for ___ days.
  4. Put ___ oil in the pan.
  5. There are ___ biscuits left.
Show the answers
  1. a few
  2. a little
  3. a few

3. Choose the natural form

Choose the most natural form in speaking.

  1. I've got (much / a lot of) work this week.
  2. We don't have (much / many) bread.
  3. There are (too much / too many) people here.
  4. Thank you (a lot / a lot of)!
Show the answers
  1. a lot of
  2. much
  3. too many
  4. a lot

4. Correct the mistake

Each sentence has one mistake. Correct it.

  1. How many money do you need?
  2. I have much friends in Ghent.
  3. There's too many sugar in this tea.
  4. Can you give me a few information?
  5. We've got a lot of homeworks.
Show the answers
  1. How much money do you need?
  2. I have a lot of friends in Ghent.
  3. There's too much sugar in this tea.
  4. Can you give me a little information? / Can you give me some information?

5. Rewrite the sentence

Rewrite the sentence with the word in brackets, keeping the same meaning.

  1. The roads are quite empty today. (not much traffic)
  2. I know three or four people in Toronto. (a few)
  3. What is the number of people who work here? (How many)
  4. We have more than enough time. (plenty of)
Show the answers
  1. There isn't much traffic today.
  2. I've got a few friends in Toronto. / I know a few people in Toronto.
  3. How many people work here?
  4. We've got plenty of time. / We have plenty of time.

Key points
  • many / a few / few + plural; much / a little / little + uncountable; a lot of + both.
  • Positive: a lot of; negatives and questions: much / many.
  • a few / a little = some (positive); few / little = not enough (negative).
  • How many + plural? How much + uncountable? How much is it? = price.
  • too much / too many = more than is good; plenty of = more than enough; with no noun: a lot.
