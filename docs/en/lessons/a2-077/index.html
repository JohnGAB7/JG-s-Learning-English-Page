Writing a simple email

A clear email in English helps you book things, ask for information, say thank you or say sorry. An English email follows a simple plan and uses fixed phrases. This lesson gives you those phrases, from friendly to formal.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level A2

Prefer explanations in French? Version française de cette leçon →

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Organise an email: subject, greeting, opening, body, closing, sign-off.
  • Choose the greeting and the sign-off for the situation (Hi Tom / Dear Ms Martin; Best wishes / Kind regards).
  • Use key phrases: I'm writing to…, Could you…?, I've attached…, I look forward to hearing from you.
  • Avoid common mistakes (“I write you”, “in attachment”, “Cordially”).

The plan of an email

A good email is short and organised. Each part has a job: the subject gives the topic in a few words, the first sentence gives the reason for writing, then come the details and the request, and finally a closing sentence.

In English, put a comma after the greeting (“Dear Anna,”) and start the next sentence with a capital letter on a new line. Leave an empty line between paragraphs, with no indent.

Greetings and sign-offs

Formal (a company, an office, someone you don't know): “Dear Mr Lambert,” (man), “Dear Ms Lambert,” (woman, whether married or not), “Dear Sir or Madam,” (name unknown). Finish with “Kind regards,” or “Best regards,”; in a very formal letter, “Yours sincerely,” (name known) or “Yours faithfully,” (UK, name unknown).

Neutral (a colleague, a client you know): “Dear Anna,” or “Hello Anna,”; finish with “Best wishes,” or “Kind regards,”.

Friendly (a friend, family): “Hi Tom,”, “Hey Tom,” (very informal); finish with “See you soon,”, “Love,” (family, close friends), “Cheers,” (UK, informal).

In British English, “Mr”, “Ms”, “Mrs”, “Dr” have no full stop; in American English they do (Mr., Ms.). Use the family name, never the first name alone after Mr / Ms: “Dear Ms Dubois”, not “Dear Ms Claire”.

Opening phrases

To say why you are writing: “I'm writing to…” (+ verb) or “I'm writing about…” (+ noun). This is the present continuous, never “I write you”.

To answer an email: “Thank you for your email.”, “Thanks for your message.” (more informal). To a friend: “I hope you're well.” / “How are you?”

  • I'm writing to ask about your English courses.
    writing to + verb.
  • I'm writing about my order number 4521.
    writing about + noun.
  • Thank you for your quick reply.
    Answering an email.
  • Hi Léa, I hope you're well! Thanks for the photos.
    Friendly opening.

Asking, informing, attaching a file

To ask politely: “Could you send me…?”, “Could you tell me…?”, “I'd like to…” (lessons 34 and 57). With “please”: “Please let me know if…”.

For an attachment: “I've attached…” or “Please find attached…” (formal). Don't say “in attachment”.

To say sorry: “Sorry for the late reply.” / “I'm sorry, but I can't come on Friday.”

  • Could you send me the price list, please?
    A polite request.
  • I've attached my CV.
    An attachment.
  • Please let me know if you need more information.
    An offer of help.
  • Sorry for the late reply. I was on holiday.
    Saying sorry.

Closing

Formal: “I look forward to hearing from you.” Careful: “look forward to” takes -ing (“to hearing”, not “to hear”).

Neutral: “Thanks in advance.”, “Let me know if you have any questions.”

Friendly: “See you soon!”, “Write soon!”, “Speak soon!”

  • I look forward to hearing from you.
    Formal closing.
  • Thanks in advance for your help.
    Neutral closing.
  • Can't wait to see you! Love, Mariam
    Friendly closing.

The formula

Dear Ms… / Hi…, — I'm writing to… — Could you…? / I've attached… — I look forward to hearing from you. — Kind regards, / Best wishes,

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • “email” /ˈiːmeɪl/: stress on the first syllable (“EE-mail”).
  • “attached” /əˈtætʃt/: only two syllables; the -ed sounds like /t/.
  • “Ms” is /mɪz/ or /məz/; “Mrs” /ˈmɪsɪz/; “Mr” /ˈmɪstə/.
  • “sincerely” /sɪnˈsɪəli/: stress on the second syllable; “regards” /rɪˈɡɑːdz/.
  • To say an address: @ = “at”, . = “dot”, - = “hyphen” or “dash”, _ = “underscore”: anna.diallo@mail.com = “anna dot diallo at mail dot com”.

In a conversation

Listening Studio

In an office in Brussels, Ahmed helps his colleague Julie write an email in English to a hotel in Dublin.

Julie

Ahmed, can you help me? I need to write to a hotel in Dublin.
Asking for help.

Ahmed

Sure. Do you know the manager's name?
The name decides the greeting.

Julie

No, I don't.
Short answer.

Ahmed

Then start with “Dear Sir or Madam”. Then say why you're writing.
Formal greeting, name unknown.

Julie

“I'm writing to book two single rooms for the 3rd and 4th of June.” Is that OK?
Opening phrase.

Ahmed

Perfect. Now ask your question politely.
Next part: the request.

Julie

“Could you tell me if breakfast is included?”
A polite question.

Ahmed

Great. And finish with “I look forward to hearing from you. Kind regards.”
Closing + sign-off.

Julie

Not “Cordially”?
A common mistake.

Ahmed

No, nobody says that! “Kind regards” is perfect.
The natural sign-off.

Key points
  • Plan: subject → greeting → reason (I'm writing to…) → details and request → closing → sign-off.
  • Formal: Dear Mr / Ms + family name, or Dear Sir or Madam; Kind regards / Yours sincerely / Yours faithfully.
  • Friendly: Hi + first name; See you soon / Love / Cheers.
  • Key phrases: Could you…? · I've attached… · Please let me know if… · I look forward to hearing from you (+ -ing).
  • Never: I write you, in attachment, Cordially, Dear Madam Smith.
