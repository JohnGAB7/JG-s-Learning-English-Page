Linking words: and, but, because

Linking words join ideas and turn a list of short sentences into real speech. “And” adds, “but” shows a contrast, “because” gives a reason, and “or” gives a choice. Using them well makes your English smoother and more natural.

⏱ 30–45 min✎ Written by JG◎ Level A1

Prefer explanations in French? Version française de cette leçon →

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
  • Join two ideas with and (adding), but (contrast) and because (reason).
  • Use or for a choice and in negative sentences.
  • Write a correct list (A, B and C) with commas.
  • Know the difference between because (+ subject + verb) and because of (+ noun).

And: adding

“And” adds information. It joins two words, two groups or two sentences: “bread and butter”, “I get up and I have a shower”.

When the subject is the same, you don't need to repeat it: “I get up and have a shower.”

In a list, separate the items with commas and put “and” before the last one: “I speak French, English and Ewondo.” In American English, people often put a comma before “and” too (“French, English, and Ewondo”): this is the “Oxford comma”.

  • My sister is a nurse and my brother is a teacher.
    Two sentences joined.
  • I buy tomatoes, onions and rice.
    A list.
  • She finishes work and goes to the gym.
    Same subject, not repeated.

But: contrast

“But” shows a contrast or a surprise. We often put a comma before “but” when it joins two full sentences: “The hotel is small, but it's very nice.”

Don't use “but” when there is no contrast: “I like cats and I like dogs too.” (not “but”).

In more formal English, you will later use “however” and “although” (lesson 118).

  • I like my job, but it's very tiring.
    Positive + negative.
  • The flat is small but bright.
    Two adjectives in contrast.
  • He can speak English, but he can't write it.
    can + can't.

Because: giving a reason

“Because” gives a reason. It is followed by a subject and a verb: “I'm tired because I work at night.” It is the natural answer to “Why?”.

“Because of” is followed by a noun: “The train is late because of the snow.” Compare: “because it's snowing” / “because of the snow”.

In speech, you can answer “Because I'm tired.” alone. In writing, don't write a sentence that starts with “Because” and has no main part; write “I'm staying at home because I'm tired.” or “Because I'm tired, I'm staying at home.”

because + subject + verbbecause it's raininga full sentence after it

  • I'm learning English because I want to work in Canada.
    because + subject + verb.
  • We're staying at home because of the storm.
    because of + noun.
In informal speech, “because” often becomes “'cause” or “cos” /kəz/. Don't write it in a formal text.

Or: choosing… and negatives

“Or” gives a choice: “Tea or coffee?”, “We can walk or take a taxi.”

In a negative sentence, English uses “or”, not “and”: “I don't like tea or coffee.” (I don't like either of them.) “I don't like tea and coffee” sounds wrong or changes the meaning.

For the opposite of “because” (the result), use “so”: “It's raining, so I'm taking an umbrella.” Lesson 75 looks at so, then, after that.

  • Do you want to stay here or go out?
    A choice.
  • She doesn't eat meat or fish.
    Negative: or.
  • I'm hungry, so let's eat.
    so = the result.

Making longer sentences

With these words you go from very short sentences (“I live in Liège. I like it. It's rainy.”) to more natural speech: “I live in Liège and I like it, but it's often rainy.”

But don't make very long sentences with five “and”s: in writing, two clear sentences are better.

  • I love Brussels because it's international, but the weather isn't great.
    because + but in one sentence.
  • We can go to the beach or visit the museum, and then have dinner.
    or + and.

The formula

A and B (adding) · A, but B (contrast) · A because B (reason) · A or B (choice; negatives)

Pronunciation

Pronunciation Lab

  • “and” is almost always weak: /ən/ or even /n/. “fish and chips” = /ˌfɪʃ n ˈtʃɪps/, “rock and roll” = “rock'n'roll”.
  • “but” is weak in the middle of a sentence: /bət/. It is strong /bʌt/ when you want to stress it.
  • “because” is /bɪˈkɒz/ (UK) or /bɪˈkʌz/ (US), with the stress on the second syllable. In fast speech: /kəz/.
  • In a list, the voice goes up on each item and down on the last: “tomatoes ↗, onions ↗ and rice ↘”.
  • In a choice question, the voice goes up on the first choice and down on the second: “Tea ↗ or coffee ↘?”.

In a conversation

Listening Studio

In Abidjan, Koffi phones his friend Mariam to plan the weekend.

Koffi

Hi Mariam! Are you free on Saturday?
free = not busy.

Mariam

In the morning, no, because I work. But I'm free in the afternoon.
because = reason; but = contrast.

Koffi

Great! We can go to the beach or watch a film.
or = choice.

Mariam

The beach! I love the sea and it's very hot this week.
and = adding.

Koffi

OK. Let's take the bus, because my car is at the garage.
because = reason.

Mariam

No problem. Can I bring my sister? She's on holiday and she's bored.
bored = with nothing to do.

Koffi

Of course! But she doesn't swim, right?
“right?” = is that true?

Mariam

No, she can't swim, but she loves sitting in the sun and reading.
but + and.

Exercises

Writing Workshop

From easy to harder. Answer without looking at the lesson, then check each exercise.

1. And, but or because?

Complete with and, but or because.

  1. I'm hungry ___ I want a sandwich.
  2. The film is long ___ it's very good.
  3. She's tired ___ she works at night.
  4. I've got a brother ___ two sisters.
  5. I can't come ___ I'm ill.
  6. He's rich ___ he isn't happy.
Show the answers
  1. and
  2. but
  3. because
  4. and
  5. because
  6. but

2. And or or?

Complete with and or or.

  1. Do you want rice ___ pasta? (a choice)
  2. I don't like tea ___ coffee.
  3. I buy bread, eggs ___ milk.
  4. We can walk ___ take a taxi.
Show the answers
  1. or
  2. or
  3. and
  4. or

3. Join the sentences

Make one sentence with and, but or because.

  1. I'm hungry. I want a sandwich.
  2. I like Lyon. It's expensive.
  3. She's tired. She works at night.
  4. He speaks French. He speaks English.
  5. The shop is closed. It's Sunday.
Show the answers
  1. I'm hungry and I want a sandwich.
  2. I like Lyon, but it's expensive.
  3. She's tired because she works at night.
  4. He speaks French and English.
  5. The shop is closed because it's Sunday.

4. Correct the mistake

Each sentence has one mistake. Write it correctly.

  1. I don't like tea and coffee.
  2. The train is late because the snow.
  3. I'm learning English because of I want to travel.
  4. The hotel is small and comfortable but cheap and nice.
  5. Do you want juice and water? (a choice)
Show the answers
  1. I don't like tea or coffee.
  2. The train is late because of the snow.
  3. I'm learning English because I want to travel.
  4. The hotel is small but comfortable. (or: The hotel is small, cheap and comfortable.)
  5. Do you want juice or water? / Would you like juice or water?

5. Your turn

Write four or five sentences about your town or your job. Use and, but, because and or at least once.

  1. Model answer
Show the answers
  1. I live in Douala and I work in a bank. I like my job because my colleagues are friendly, but the days are long. After work I go to the gym or I visit my parents. I'm learning English because I want a job in Canada.
Key points
  • and = adding; in a list: A, B and C.
  • but = contrast (often with a comma before it); no “but” without a contrast.
  • because + subject + verb (because it's raining); because of + noun (because of the rain).
  • or = choice; in a negative sentence: I don't like tea or coffee.
  • so = result (It's late, so I'm going home).
